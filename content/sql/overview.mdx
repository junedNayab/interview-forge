## What this track is assessed on

If you already write SQL comfortably, the risk in this round is not that you
cannot produce the query. It is that you produce the query, the interviewer says
"this table has 200 million rows, what happens?", and the conversation ends.

**A SQL interview at a product company is a database-reasoning interview wearing
a query-writing costume.** Writing the correct `JOIN` takes two minutes and
proves you are not a fraud. The remaining forty minutes are spent on four things
that have nothing to do with syntax: which index the planner will choose and why,
what the query does as the table grows, what two concurrent transactions do to
each other, and what you would change if this ran a thousand times a second.

Concretely, the questions that separate candidates are:

- "Show me the execution plan you expect, and where it would go wrong."
- "You have a composite index on `(a, b, c)`. Which of these five queries use it?"
- "Two users click 'buy' on the last item at the same time. Walk me through what
  the database does at your isolation level."
- "This endpoint is `LIMIT 20 OFFSET 200000` and it is slow. Why, and what do
  you replace it with?"

None of those are answerable by knowing more SQL. They are answerable by knowing
what the storage engine and the planner do, which is what this track is about.

A second thing is assessed more quietly: whether you express intent declaratively
rather than procedurally. Reaching for a correlated subquery, or a loop in
application code, where a window function would do marks you as someone who will
write slow systems. That is why window functions get a whole lesson and why they
are the clearest differentiator in this round.

## Where you stand, and what to prioritise

You know SQL and DSA well, which means the joins lesson is mostly confirmation
and the problem-solving lessons will feel comfortable. Do not skip them, but read
them quickly.

Your real gap is the boundary between SQL and the database as a running system.
Two specific areas:

**Indexing and the planner.** Knowing that indexes make things faster is where
most people stop. The interview lives one level down: B-tree structure, why the
leftmost-prefix rule is a consequence of the sort order rather than an arbitrary
restriction, what a covering index buys, why an index on a low-cardinality column
is often ignored, and why adding an index can make writes measurably worse.

**Isolation and concurrency.** Where strong SQL developers most reliably lose
points, because you can write correct queries for years without needing to name a
phantom read. It also has the highest crossover value: `@Transactional`
propagation in the [Java track](/java/5-transaction-management-transactional),
consistency models in the
[system design track](/system-design/7-cap-theorem-consistency-models), and the
lost-update question asked in all three.

Everything else here is either already comfortable for you or reference material.

## The order to work through it

1. [1. JOINs — Know Them Cold](/sql/1-joins-know-them-cold) — read it fast as a
   confirmation pass, but do stop at the `NULL` semantics and the difference
   between filtering in `ON` and filtering in `WHERE` on an outer join, which is
   the one part of joins that trips experienced people.
2. [3. Indexing & Performance](/sql/3-indexing-performance) — out of numeric
   order deliberately. This is the highest-value lesson in the track and
   everything after it reads better once you can picture a B-tree. It is also
   the longest, at nearly 4,000 words.
3. [2. Window Functions — The Senior Differentiator](/sql/2-window-functions-the-senior-differentiator)
   — after indexing, because the interesting question about a window function is
   usually how it is executed, not what it returns.
4. [4. Transactions & Isolation Levels](/sql/4-transactions-isolation-levels) —
   your biggest gap. Read this one the way you would read the concurrency
   lesson in Java: slowly, and with the anomaly examples drawn out on paper.
5. [5. Query Optimization Checklist](/sql/5-query-optimization-checklist) — the
   synthesis lesson. It only works once you have the previous three, because
   optimisation is just applying them in a fixed order.
6. [6. Common Interview SQL Problems (Quick Reference)](/sql/6-common-interview-sql-problems-quick-reference)
   — the recognisable question shapes: second-highest salary, gaps and islands,
   running totals, duplicate detection, top-N per group.
7. [Practice: SQL Problems](/sql/practice-sql-problems) — do these with the
   answers covered and a timer, not as reading.
8. [Quick Reference: What Seniors Say](/sql/quick-reference-what-seniors-say) —
   a junior-answer versus senior-answer comparison across the whole track. Read
   it last, as a diagnostic: every row where the junior answer is the one you
   would have given points at a lesson to revisit.

## What "ready" looks like

Say these out loud, unprompted, in complete sentences. This is the actual bar,
not a feeling of familiarity.

- Draw a B-tree index on paper and use it to explain why
  `WHERE name LIKE '%smith'` cannot use an index but `WHERE name LIKE 'smith%'`
  can.
- Given a composite index on `(tenant_id, created_at, status)`, classify five
  queries as using the index fully, partially, or not at all — and say why for
  each.
- Name the four standard isolation levels, the anomaly each one permits, and
  then state what the default is in PostgreSQL and MySQL. Add the sentence most
  candidates miss: a lost update is still possible at Read Committed, and
  preventing it needs either `SELECT ... FOR UPDATE` or an optimistic version
  column.
- Explain what `EXPLAIN ANALYZE` tells you that `EXPLAIN` does not, and name the
  three things you look at first in a plan.
- Rewrite an `OFFSET`-based pagination query as keyset pagination and explain
  exactly which part of the work `OFFSET` was doing that keyset avoids.
- Take a top-N-per-group problem and solve it twice: once with a window
  function, once with a correlated subquery, and say which you would ship.
- Explain why adding a fifth index to a hot write table is a decision that needs
  justification rather than an obvious improvement.

If you can also say *when the index you designed will be ignored by the
planner*, you are past the bar for most loops.

## How long this track takes

About 22,000 words across eight lessons, with indexing, isolation and
optimisation each running well past 3,000. Budget four to five hours of reading
plus two to three hours on the practice problems, over one to two weeks. Because
your baseline here is strong, the returns are concentrated: if you only have one
evening, spend it on
[indexing](/sql/3-indexing-performance) and
[isolation levels](/sql/4-transactions-isolation-levels) and skip everything
else.

One habit worth building alongside the reading. Take a query from your own
project, run `EXPLAIN ANALYZE` on it against a table with real data volume, and
narrate the plan out loud. Reading about plans and reading a plan are different
skills, and only the second one gets tested.
