## What this track is assessed on

There is usually no interview called "CS fundamentals". That is exactly why this
track gets under-prepared and why it costs people offers.

The material shows up in two places. The first is the rapid-fire segment at the
end of an otherwise technical round, when the interviewer has ten minutes left:
what happens when you type a URL into a browser, what is the difference between a
process and a thread, why is HTTPS secure, what is a deadlock. The second, and
more consequential, is as a follow-up *inside* another round. You are twenty
minutes into a design discussion, you say "the client retries", and the
interviewer asks "at which layer, and what does the TCP connection do while that
happens?"

**Breadth questions are how interviewers test whether your depth is real or
memorised.** Somebody who genuinely understands concurrency can describe context
switching; somebody who learned the Java API cannot. The questions are short
because their purpose is to probe, not to grade a long answer.

That sets a different standard from the other tracks here. You are not being asked
to design a congestion control algorithm. You are being asked to explain each
concept clearly and correctly to a colleague, in two or three minutes, without
hedging. Depth beyond that has low returns; hesitation has a high cost.

## Where you stand, and what to prioritise

Your notes and study are concentrated on [Java internals](/java) and databases
([SQL](/sql)), both of which you know well. This track is the breadth that sits
underneath those and around them, and it is likely the least-covered ground in
your preparation after system design.

Two things about your position make the prioritisation easy.

**Your Java concurrency work gives you a head start on the operating systems
material, and a blind spot.** You know what a thread is from the API side. What you
likely have not done is connect it to processes, scheduling, context-switch cost
and the memory hierarchy — the difference between "I use a thread pool" and "I size
the pool differently for CPU-bound and IO-bound work, and here is why". That
connection is worth more than anything else in this track, so the OS lesson comes
early.

**Networking is probably your weakest area and the most frequently asked.** The
walk-me-through-a-URL question is close to universal, and a gift once rehearsed
because it demonstrates DNS, TCP, TLS, HTTP and load balancing in one answer. With
limited time, this is where it goes.

Security is the area where you have a concrete advantage worth using: your own
[URL shortener project](/system-design/case-1-design-a-url-shortener-your-project-master-this-one)
has authentication and rate limiting in it, so every security concept here has a
place in your own code to attach to. Use that, and audit the project as you read
— a security answer that cites something you fixed in your own service is far
stronger than a definition.

Distributed systems concepts overlap heavily with the
[system design track](/system-design/7-cap-theorem-consistency-models). Read it
here as vocabulary consolidation rather than as new material.

## The order to work through it

1. [1. Networking](/cs-fundamentals/1-networking) — first, because it is the most
   frequently asked and because the URL walkthrough is the single highest-value
   rehearsed answer in this track.
2. [2. Operating Systems & Concurrency](/cs-fundamentals/2-operating-systems-concurrency)
   — second, and read it in conjunction with
   [Java concurrency](/java/3-concurrency-senior-must-have). Each explains the
   other: the JVM lesson tells you what the API guarantees, this one tells you
   what the machine is doing to provide the guarantee.
3. [3. Distributed Systems Concepts](/cs-fundamentals/3-distributed-systems-concepts)
   — third, once networking is in place, since every distributed-systems problem
   is ultimately a consequence of the network being unreliable. Pairs directly
   with the system design track.
4. [4. Security (also see your project's OWASP requirements)](/cs-fundamentals/4-security-also-see-your-projects-owasp-requirements)
   — fourth, and read it with your own project open. Authentication, token
   handling, injection and secret management all have examples in your code.
5. [5. Rapid-fire self-test (answer out loud)](/cs-fundamentals/5-rapid-fire-self-test-answer-out-loud)
   — last, and used as a test rather than as reading. Cover the answers, say
   yours out loud, and treat every hesitation as a pointer back to one of the
   four lessons above.

The ordering principle is simply dependency: the network explains distributed
systems, and the operating system explains concurrency. Security draws on both.

## What "ready" looks like

The bar for this track is fluency, not depth, so the self-check is a set of
short explanations delivered out loud without notes. Two to three minutes each,
no hedging, no "I think".

- Walk through everything that happens between typing a URL and seeing the page,
  naming DNS resolution, the TCP handshake, the TLS handshake, the HTTP request
  and the response path. Then answer the follow-up: which of those steps would
  you cache, and where?
- Explain the difference between TCP and UDP, then name a case where you would
  deliberately choose UDP and justify it.
- Explain what a process is, what a thread is, and what a context switch costs.
  Then answer: why is a thread pool for IO-bound work sized differently from one
  for CPU-bound work?
- Define a deadlock by its four required conditions, then say which one you would
  remove in practice and how.
- Explain what HTTPS actually protects against and what it does not, including
  why certificate validation is the part that matters.
- Explain why exactly-once delivery is not achievable in a distributed system,
  and what people mean when they claim it.
- Explain what idempotency is and how you would make a payment endpoint
  idempotent.
- Name three vulnerability classes from your own project's threat surface and how
  each is mitigated in the code.

Passing bar: you can answer any item in the
[rapid-fire self-test](/cs-fundamentals/5-rapid-fire-self-test-answer-out-loud)
without pausing to reconstruct it, and your URL walkthrough is smooth enough that
you can be interrupted mid-answer and resume.

## How long this track takes

About 12,000 words across five lessons, each around 2,200 to 2,600 words. That is
the smallest track on the site, and the least of it is genuinely new to you, so
budget three to four hours of reading and two hours of out-loud rehearsal, over
about a week.

Where that week should sit in your schedule matters more than its length. Do this
track *after* the [system design fundamentals](/system-design), not before.
Reading about consistency and partitioning here is abstract; reading about them
after you have tried to design a replicated system gives every term something to
attach to. The exception is networking, which you can move earlier at any point,
because the URL walkthrough is useful to have rehearsed from day one.
