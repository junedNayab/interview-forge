## How to use this page

Read it once a week before your first onsite, then again the morning of each
round. It is deliberately boring in places: most interview-day damage comes from
logistics and nerves rather than from not knowing things.

## The week before

- [ ] **Confirm the format in writing.** Ask the recruiter: how many rounds, how
      long each, which platform, shared editor or your own IDE, whether code
      execution is allowed, and whether there is a drawing tool for design.
- [ ] **Ask what the rounds actually are.** "Is the system design round HLD, LLD,
      or both?" saves you from preparing the wrong thing. Also ask who you are
      meeting and their role.
- [ ] **Rehearse your stories out loud, timed.** Six to eight of them, four
      minutes each, from bullets not a script. See
      [general behavioral tips](/behavioral/general-behavioral-tips).
- [ ] **Do one full-length timed mock per round type.** A 45-minute design
      problem standing at a whiteboard, two mediums under time, one machine
      coding problem. Silent practice does not transfer.
- [ ] **Re-read your resume and interrogate it.** Every line is fair game. For
      each bullet, ask yourself the three follow-ups you would ask. See
      [resume guidance](/roadmap/6-resume-guidance-projects-skills-sections).
- [ ] **Re-read your project code,** so you are not surprised by your own
      decisions. For the URL shortener: the caching strategy, code generation,
      the rate limiter, and what you would change before production.
- [ ] **Write your questions for the interviewer.** Two or three per round, and
      different ones for the hiring manager.

## The day before

- [ ] **Test the tooling.** Open the actual platform — CoderPad, HackerRank,
      Meet, Zoom, whatever it is — and run through it. Discovering that screen
      share needs an OS permission grant is fine today and a disaster tomorrow.
- [ ] **Set up your editor.** If you can use your own: a scratch file open, a
      main method ready, collection and stream imports already there. If you are
      in a browser editor, pick your language and write a hello world so you know
      whether autocomplete exists.
- [ ] **Silence everything.** Notifications, calendar alerts, phone, Slack, the
      second monitor.
- [ ] **Save the recruiter's phone number in your phone.** If the link fails at
      the start time you need a human in thirty seconds, not an inbox search.
- [ ] **Light review only.** Skim your one-page notes and the
      [rapid-fire self-test](/cs-fundamentals/5-rapid-fire-self-test-answer-out-loud).
      Do not learn a new topic the night before.
- [ ] **Sleep.** Not filler advice: sleep loss degrades exactly what interviews
      test — working memory, verbal fluency, and recovering from a mistake
      without spiralling.

## Two hours before

- [ ] Eat something. Low blood sugar in the fourth round is a real phenomenon.
- [ ] Restart your machine. Close everything you do not need.
- [ ] Re-test camera, microphone and share. Again. It takes ninety seconds.
- [ ] Fill a large glass or bottle of water and put it within reach.
- [ ] Put paper and a pen next to you. Physical, not a second window.
- [ ] Warm up your voice and your hands: solve one easy problem you have already
      solved, talking out loud the whole time. The goal is not practice, it is to
      not have the first words you say all day be in the interview.
- [ ] Use the bathroom. Back-to-back rounds have short gaps.

## Five minutes before

- [ ] Close every tab except the interview platform and one blank notes file.
- [ ] Join two or three minutes early.
- [ ] Have ready: your one-line introduction, your questions for this specific
      interviewer, and the name of the person you are meeting.
- [ ] Take four slow breaths with a long exhale. It is a physiological trick
      rather than a psychological one and it works on the shakiness.

## During the round

**Thinking out loud is scored.** This is the single most important thing on this
page. An interviewer cannot give credit for reasoning they cannot hear, and
silence reads as being stuck even when you are making progress. Narrate: "I'm
considering two approaches here…", "I'm going to check the empty case…", "let me
re-read the constraint before I commit to this."

**Restate the problem before you start.** Thirty seconds of "so, to confirm:
input is X, output is Y, and I can assume Z" catches misunderstandings while
they are still free.

**Write down constraints and examples as they are given.** On paper. You will
forget the edge case they mentioned in passing.

### When you blank

It happens, and how you handle it is itself observed. The recovery sequence:

1. **Say it.** "Give me a moment to think" is a complete and acceptable sentence.
   Twenty seconds of acknowledged silence is normal; twenty seconds of unexplained
   silence is not.
2. **Go back to the concrete.** Work a small example by hand. In a design round,
   go back to the numbers: "let me recalculate the write volume, because that
   decides this." Returning to first principles is always a legal move and it
   buys thinking time while still producing signal.
3. **State what you do know.** "I know this is a graph problem and I know BFS
   gives shortest path in an unweighted graph; what I'm not sure about yet is how
   to represent the state." That is a partial answer, and partial answers score.

### Asking for a hint

You lose fewer points than you think, and far fewer than you lose by burning
fifteen minutes in silence. The cost depends entirely on how you ask.

- Weak: "I don't know, can you help?"
- Strong: "I've got a working brute force at O(n²) and I think the improvement
  involves preprocessing the array, but I can't see what to precompute. Am I on
  the right track, or should I be thinking differently?"

The second version shows where you are, what you have tried, and what specific
gap you have. Interviewers are generally instructed to give hints, and many
score the *use* of a hint rather than the need for it. **Ask at around the
ten-minute mark of being genuinely stuck, not at thirty.**

### When you get something wrong

If you notice the error yourself, say so immediately and out loud — "actually,
that's wrong, this breaks when the list is empty" — and fix it. Self-correction
is a positive signal, not a negative one.

If the interviewer points it out, do not defend it reflexively. "You're right,
let me look at that" costs nothing. If you genuinely believe you are correct, say
why once, calmly, and then defer: "I may be wrong about that — let me trace
through your example." Arguing past two pushes is a reliable way to fail a round
you were otherwise passing.

### Watch the clock

Reserve the last five minutes of a design round for bottlenecks and
self-critique, and the last five of a coding round to trace your solution on an
example and name the complexity. Running out of time with nothing tested reads
as poor planning.

## Immediately after each round

- [ ] **Write down every question you were asked.** Do this within ten minutes,
      before the next round if you can. Memory for this decays remarkably fast
      and it is the highest-value artifact of the whole day — it tells you what
      that company actually asks, it is your study list if you are rejected, and
      it is your preparation list for the next loop.
- [ ] Note what you fumbled and what you would say differently.
- [ ] Note the interviewer's name and anything specific they mentioned, for the
      thank-you note and for later rounds.
- [ ] Then close the notebook. Do not relitigate the last round during the next
      one — a round you think went badly has often gone fine, and the reverse
      also happens.

## At the end of the day

- [ ] Send a short thank-you to the recruiter, and to interviewers if you have
      their addresses. Two or three sentences with one specific reference to
      something discussed. It rarely changes an outcome but it is cheap.
- [ ] Add every question from the day to a running document. Over three or four
      loops this becomes the most accurate picture you will have of what these
      companies ask.
- [ ] Update [your tracker](/roadmap/5-progress-tracking), then stop thinking
      about it. The decision is out of your hands.

<Callout type="note" title="If a round goes badly">
Candidates routinely misjudge this, in both directions. Interviewers are
comparing you against a bar, not against your own expectations, and a round where
you struggled and recovered out loud frequently scores better than one that felt
smooth. Finish the day properly regardless — the remaining rounds are independent,
and a visibly demoralised candidate in round four converts one bad round into
four.
</Callout>
