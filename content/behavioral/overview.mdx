## What this track is for

Four of the six lessons here are built around a single behavioral question each.
That is deliberate: the questions repeat across companies, and the difference
between a candidate who has thought about them and one who is improvising is
visible within thirty seconds.

But the more important thing to understand before you start is what these lessons
are *not*. They are not stories for you to memorise.

## Why there are no ready-made stories here

An earlier version of this track contained complete, polished STAR answers about
production incidents and caching decisions. They read well. They were also
invented, and using an invented story in an interview is one of the more
dangerous things you can do.

The reason is that behavioral interviewers drill. You give a four-minute answer
about a latency problem and they ask what the actual error looked like, how you
first noticed it, what you ruled out before you found it, what the fix was in
code, and how you verified it. **A borrowed story survives the first question and
collapses somewhere around the third**, and the collapse is worse than never
having told the story — you have now demonstrated that you will assert things you
cannot support, which contaminates everything else you said in the loop.

So every story lesson here has the same shape:

- **Why the question is asked** and what the interviewer is actually collecting.
- **The structure that works** for that specific question type.
- **A worked example built from the `url-shortener` project**, using real bugs
  that genuinely exist in that codebase — so if you built it, the story is true.
- **Fill-in prompts** for drafting your own version from your own work.
- **The follow-up questions** an interviewer will ask, and what each one checks.
- **A weak answer versus a strong one**, side by side.

You are meant to finish each lesson with a page of notes about something you
actually did. The worked example exists to show you what "specific enough" looks
like, not to be recited.

<Callout type="pitfall" title="The one rule for the worked examples">
Only use a worked example if you actually did that work or actually found that
bug. If you built the URL shortener but never investigated its caching behaviour,
you can honestly say "I went back and profiled my own project and found this" —
that is a real story, and a good one. What you cannot do is describe an
investigation that did not happen.
</Callout>

## The order to work through it

1. **[General behavioral tips](/behavioral/general-behavioral-tips)** — start
   here. It is the anchor lesson: STAR with the right time proportions, the
   story-bank approach, how to talk about failure, the red flags, and the
   "why are you leaving?" pair. Everything else assumes it.
2. **[Story 1 — a challenging bug](/behavioral/story-1-tell-me-about-a-challenging-bug-you-solved)** —
   technical depth and debugging method.
3. **[Story 2 — improving performance](/behavioral/story-2-describe-a-time-you-improved-performance)** —
   measurement, tradeoffs and quantified results.
4. **[Story 3 — code reviews](/behavioral/story-3-how-do-you-approach-code-reviews)** —
   how you work with other people's code.
5. **[Story 4 — disagreements](/behavioral/story-4-how-do-you-handle-disagreements-with-your-team)** —
   conflict, influence and being wrong gracefully.
6. **[Day-of interview checklist](/behavioral/day-of-interview-checklist)** —
   read the week before, then again the morning of.

Budget an evening for the anchor lesson and an hour per story, most of which
should be spent writing and saying your own version rather than reading.

## How much this round matters

More than you probably think, and more as you get more senior. The behavioral
round is frequently the deciding input when a hiring committee is looking at a
candidate with adequate but unspectacular technical scores, and it is the round
where "no signal" is treated as a negative rather than a neutral. See
[how product-company hiring actually works](/roadmap/1-how-product-company-hiring-actually-works)
for where it sits in the loop and who reads the write-up.

The compensating factor is that it is the cheapest round to improve. Four evenings
of honest preparation moves you further here than it will anywhere else on the
loop.
