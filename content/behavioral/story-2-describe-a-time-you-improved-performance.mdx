## What this question is really probing

Performance stories are scored on **measurement discipline**, not on how clever
the optimisation was. The interviewer is trying to find out whether you are the
kind of engineer who profiles before changing things, or the kind who adds a
cache because caches are fast.

Specifically, they want to know:

- **Did you measure before you changed anything?** If you cannot state a "before"
  number, you did not.
- **Did you find the actual bottleneck?** Or optimise the part you found most
  interesting. These are different and the second one is extremely common.
- **Did you verify the improvement?** With the same measurement, under comparable
  load.
- **What did it cost?** Every optimisation trades something — complexity,
  consistency, memory, operational burden. A candidate who claims a free win has
  not thought about it.

There is a quiet fifth thing being assessed: whether you know when to stop. An
engineer who spent three weeks getting p99 from 40 ms to 35 ms on an endpoint
nobody complained about has demonstrated a judgement problem, not a skill.

## The structure that works for this question

```
1. The trigger        what made this worth doing — a complaint, an alert, a bill
2. The baseline       the number, at a named percentile, under a named load
3. The investigation  how you found the bottleneck, and what it was NOT
4. The bottleneck     stated precisely, with the evidence
5. The options        at least two, with the tradeoff between them
6. What you did       the change, in enough detail to be interrogated
7. The after number   measured the same way
8. The cost           what you traded away, and what you deliberately left alone
```

Steps 2 and 7 are non-negotiable — a performance story without numbers is not a
performance story. **Step 5 is what separates mid-level from senior: doing the
obvious optimisation is execution, choosing between two optimisations is
engineering.** Step 8 is the one almost nobody includes, and it is disproportionately
persuasive.

## A worked example from the url-shortener

There is a genuine and quite subtle performance bug in the `url-shortener`
redirect path. It is a good example because the code looks correct, the cache
appears to be working, and the hit rate metric would look excellent.

`UrlService.resolve()` does cache-aside against Redis. On a hit it returns the
long URL without touching Postgres — except that before returning, it calls
`recordClick()`. And `recordClick()` calls `urlRepository.findByShortCode()` to
load the entity, because it needs the foreign key for the `click_events` row.

**So every cache hit still performs the exact database query the cache exists to
avoid.** The cache is removing nothing from the database; it is only adding a
Redis round trip in front of the same query. On top of that, `recordClick()` does
a synchronous insert into `click_events` on the redirect hot path, and `resolve()`
is annotated `@Transactional`, so even a pure cache hit borrows a pooled database
connection for the whole request.

Told as an answer:

> **Trigger.** "I was load-testing my URL shortener before writing it up, and the
> redirect endpoint was not getting anywhere near the throughput I expected given
> that Redis was in front of it. The cache hit rate was above 95%, so on paper it
> should have been almost free."
>
> **Baseline.** "Under a sustained load test, p99 on the redirect was about
> 180 ms, and the service saturated at a few hundred requests per second. Redis
> alone should be sub-millisecond, so the shape was wrong, not just the
> magnitude." *(If your numbers are approximate, say so — see the note below.)*
>
> **Investigation.** "My first assumption was Redis latency or connection pool
> contention on the Redis side. I checked `MONITOR` and the Redis round trip was
> well under a millisecond. Then I turned on Hibernate SQL logging and hit the
> endpoint once with a warm cache. I expected zero queries and got two — a select
> on `urls` and an insert into `click_events`."
>
> **The bottleneck.** "The cache-hit path was still doing the database read. The
> click-recording code needed the URL entity to get the foreign key, so it called
> `findByShortCode` itself. The cache was in front of a query that was still
> being executed every single request, just from a different call site. The
> `@Transactional` on the resolve method meant every redirect also held a pooled
> connection, so connection pool size was the real throughput ceiling."
>
> **The options.** "Three, roughly. One: cache the numeric ID alongside the URL
> so the click insert doesn't need to load the entity — cheapest change, keeps
> everything synchronous. Two: make click recording asynchronous, so the redirect
> returns before the write happens — bigger win, but you can lose clicks on a
> crash and the counts become eventually consistent. Three: drop the per-click
> row entirely and use a Redis counter with periodic flushing — fastest, but you
> lose per-click detail like referrer and timestamp, which was a product decision
> I didn't want to make unilaterally."
>
> **What I did.** "Both one and two. I changed the cached value from a bare
> string to a small object holding the URL, the ID and the expiry, which removed
> the select. Then I moved the click insert onto an async path so the redirect no
> longer waits for it. And I narrowed `@Transactional` — it came off the resolve
> method entirely and went onto the write path only, so a cache hit doesn't take
> a connection at all."
>
> **After.** "p99 went from about 180 ms to under 15 ms, and throughput went up
> by roughly an order of magnitude on the same hardware — at which point the
> bottleneck moved somewhere else, which is the point at which I stopped."
>
> **Cost.** "Click counts are now eventually consistent and can be lost if the
> process dies with work queued, which is acceptable for analytics and would not
> be for billing. And the cached object is now a structure rather than a string,
> so there is a serialisation format to version. I deliberately did not do option
> three, because losing per-click detail is a product tradeoff, not an
> engineering one."
>
> **Learning.** "The thing I actually took from it: a high cache hit rate tells
> you the cache is being hit, not that it is saving you anything. I now check the
> query count per request directly rather than trusting the hit rate."

That last line is the kind of sentence interviewers remember.

<Callout type="tip" title="About the numbers">
Use your real measurements. If you no longer have them, estimate and label it:
"from memory it was around 180 ms and came down to roughly 15." Interviewers are
fine with estimates and hostile to invented precision — and "180 ms" quoted
confidently invites "what tool did you measure that with?", which you must be
able to answer.

If you have never load-tested this project, that is a two-evening job and it
converts this from a story you cannot tell into one you can. Run it, write the
numbers down, and you own the story permanently.
</Callout>

## Draft your own

1. **The trigger.** Who or what told you there was a problem? A user complaint,
   an alert, a timeout, a cloud bill, your own load test. "It felt slow" is
   acceptable only if you then measured it.
2. **The baseline number.** Latency at a named percentile, or throughput, or
   query count, or cost. Say how you measured it and under what load.
3. **Your first hypothesis, and whether it was right.** Being wrong here is good
   material — it is evidence you measured rather than assumed.
4. **How you found the real bottleneck.** The specific tool or technique:
   SQL logging, `EXPLAIN`, a profiler, a flame graph, request timing breakdown,
   metrics dashboard, adding timers by hand.
5. **The bottleneck in one sentence,** with the evidence that proved it.
6. **Two or three options you considered,** with the tradeoff for each. Include
   at least one you rejected and say why.
7. **What you changed.** Enough detail that someone can ask you about the code.
8. **The after number,** measured the same way as the baseline.
9. **What it cost.** Complexity, consistency, memory, an operational dependency,
   a new failure mode. Name at least one.
10. **Where the bottleneck moved to,** and why you stopped there.

Point 10 is worth extra effort. "After that, the limit was the connection pool,
and at our traffic that was fine, so I stopped" demonstrates that you understand
optimisation as a sequence of moving constraints rather than a single fix.

## Follow-ups you will get

| Follow-up | What they are checking |
|---|---|
| "How did you measure that?" | Whether the numbers are real. Name the tool. |
| "What was the p50 versus p99?" | Whether you understand that averages hide the problem. |
| "Why did you pick that option over the others?" | Tradeoff reasoning — the main event. |
| "What did the change cost you?" | Whether you see optimisations as free. |
| "What was the bottleneck after your fix?" | Depth. Strong candidates always know. |
| "How did you know it was safe to make click counts eventual?" | Whether you thought about correctness, not just speed. |
| "Would you do the same thing at 100x the traffic?" | Scaling judgement; a bridge into system design. |
| "Did you consider just not caching?" | Whether you can defend the architecture, not only the patch. |

## Weak versus strong

<Callout type="pitfall" title="What a weak answer sounds like">
"Our API was slow, so I added Redis caching and it became much faster. We saw a
big improvement in response times and the users were happy."

Three sentences, no numbers, no investigation, no tradeoff, and — worst of all —
the optimisation is asserted rather than justified. This is also the answer that
the url-shortener bug above makes fun of: adding a cache does not guarantee
anything if the query still runs.
</Callout>

<Callout type="interview" title="What a strong answer sounds like">
A trigger with a source. A baseline number at a named percentile with the tool
that produced it. A wrong first hypothesis, eliminated with evidence. A precisely
stated bottleneck. Two or three options with their tradeoffs and a justified
choice. The change in enough detail to be interrogated. An after number measured
the same way. An honest cost. And the sentence that says where the bottleneck
moved to and why you stopped.
</Callout>
