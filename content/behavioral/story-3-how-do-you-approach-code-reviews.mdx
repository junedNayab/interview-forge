## What this question is really probing

This one is different from the other three. It is not primarily a story question —
it is asking for a **philosophy**, and then checking whether you have examples
that prove you actually hold it.

What the interviewer is collecting:

- **Do you know what a review is for?** Reviews catch bugs, but their larger
  function is spreading context and keeping the codebase coherent. Candidates who
  only talk about catching bugs are describing a linter.
- **Can you separate what matters from what does not?** An engineer who blocks a
  pull request over variable naming while missing a race condition is a net
  negative on a team.
- **How do you say difficult things?** This is the real content of the question.
  Reviews are where most engineering disagreement happens in writing, permanently,
  in front of an audience.
- **How do you receive them?** Almost always asked as a follow-up, and answered
  badly more often than the first half.

There is a specific failure mode to avoid: answering entirely in the abstract.
"I look for correctness, readability and maintainability" is true, generic, and
gives the interviewer nothing. **The structure that works is a short philosophy
followed immediately by two concrete things you have actually flagged.**

## The structure that works

```
1. What you think a review is for        two sentences, a real opinion
2. Your ordering — what you check first  a severity ladder, not a checklist
3. How you phrase feedback               blocking vs non-blocking, question vs assertion
4. Two concrete examples                 something you caught; something you were wrong about
5. How you receive reviews               the follow-up, answered before it is asked
6. Speed and volume                      the practical constraint everybody has
```

## A review philosophy worth stating

Something close to this, in your own words:

> "The main thing I'm doing in a review is trying to understand the change well
> enough to have caught it if it were wrong — which means the first thing I need
> is why it exists, not what it does. After that I go in severity order, because
> the expensive mistake in reviewing is spending attention on the cheap stuff."

Then the ladder. Reviewing in a fixed order is what stops you from anchoring on
whatever you noticed first:

1. **Correctness and data integrity.** Does it do the thing? What happens on
   the error path, at the boundary, at zero, on a retry? Is anything that
   modifies data non-idempotent when it will be retried?
2. **Security.** Anything touching authentication, authorization, user input,
   or credentials. This tier is always blocking.
3. **Concurrency and failure modes.** What happens under two simultaneous
   requests, a partial failure, a slow dependency, a restart mid-operation?
4. **Data access and performance.** N+1 queries, missing indexes on new query
   paths, unbounded result sets, work on a hot path that could be deferred.
5. **Interface design.** Is the public shape of this thing something we will be
   able to change later?
6. **Tests.** Not coverage percentage — whether the tests would fail if the
   logic were wrong.
7. **Readability and naming.** Real, but last, and usually non-blocking.

**Saying the ladder out loud is worth more than any single item on it,** because
it demonstrates that you triage rather than react.

## Worked examples from the url-shortener

Use your own findings. These are real issues in the `url-shortener` project and
they illustrate what "specific enough" looks like in this answer.

**A blocking security finding.** The `application.yml` in that project contains a
hardcoded JWT signing secret and hardcoded database credentials. In a review this
is a stop-the-line comment, and the useful thing is explaining *why* rather than
citing a rule: a signing secret in version control is held forever by everyone
with repository access, including anyone who had access and left, and rotating it
becomes a coordinated deploy rather than a config change. The fix is to read both
from the environment or a secrets manager, fail fast at startup when they are
absent, and rotate the committed value as already compromised.

> "The comment I'd leave is: *this needs to move to an environment variable or a
> secrets manager before this merges, and the current value should be rotated
> since it's in git history. Happy to pair on the config change if it's easier.*
> Blocking, brief, with a reason and an offer."

**A correctness finding that is easy to miss.** The `RateLimitInterceptor` is
Redis-backed and keys its counters on `request.getRemoteAddr()`. Behind a load
balancer or reverse proxy, that returns the proxy's address, so every user in the
world shares a single bucket and the rate limiter either blocks everyone or
protects nobody. Code that is completely correct on a laptop and wrong the moment
it is deployed behind infrastructure is exactly the category reviews exist to
catch, because the tests pass either way.

**A non-blocking observation.** The same interceptor fails open — if Redis is
unavailable, requests pass through unlimited. That is very likely the right
choice for this service, since availability matters more than perfect limiting on
a redirect endpoint. But it is a decision, and a decision should be visible:

> "Worth a comment in the code saying this is deliberate. Non-blocking, but the
> next person to read it will otherwise wonder whether the empty catch block is a
> bug."

That third example is the most useful one to include in your answer, because
**it shows you distinguish between "this is wrong" and "this is a choice that
should be written down".** Reviewers who cannot make that distinction are the
ones everybody dreads.

## How to phrase feedback

Two mechanics are worth stating explicitly in your answer, because they are
concrete and most candidates do not mention them.

**Label severity.** Prefix comments so the author knows what to do with them:
blocking, non-blocking, or a question. A review with eleven comments and no
labels is intimidating and slow; the same eleven comments where two are blocking
and nine are marked optional is a five-minute conversation.

**Ask rather than assert when you are not certain.** "What happens if two
requests hit this at the same time?" is better than "this has a race condition"
for two reasons: it is not wrong if you have misread the code, and it gives the
author the chance to find it themselves, which is how the context actually
transfers. Save assertions for when you are certain and the stakes are high — the
hardcoded secret above does not need to be phrased as a question.

**Say it the way you would in person.** Tone does not survive writing, and a
terse comment reads as hostile when it was only brief. "Nit:" and "I might be
missing something, but…" are not weakness; they are the written equivalent of
tone of voice.

## How you receive reviews

This is asked as a follow-up in most interviews, and it is where the answer either
becomes credible or does not. The strongest thing you can include is a specific
time a reviewer was right and you were initially annoyed.

> "The one I remember is someone pushing back on a change where I'd widened a
> transaction boundary to make a test easier to write. My first reaction was that
> it was a small thing and the test mattered. They were right — it meant a
> connection was held across an external HTTP call, which is exactly how you
> exhaust a pool under load. I've been suspicious of `@Transactional` scope ever
> since."

Being able to name a time you were wrong in review is worth more than any amount
of describing how open to feedback you are.

If a review comment is wrong or you disagree, say the rule you follow: respond
once in writing with the reasoning, and if there are two round trips without
convergence, move it to a call. Comment threads past three replies are a signal
that the disagreement is not about the code. That connects directly to
[Story 4 on disagreements](/behavioral/story-4-how-do-you-handle-disagreements-with-your-team).

## Speed, and the honest constraint

Mention throughput, because everyone who has reviewed code knows it is the
binding constraint. Reviews that sit for two days block people, and a reviewer
who is thorough but slow can be worse for a team than one who is fast and good
enough.

A defensible position: small changes within a few hours, a real pass on large
ones the same day, and say so when you cannot — "I won't get to this properly
until tomorrow morning" beats silence. And if a pull request is too large to
review well, say that instead of rubber-stamping it. **A large change reviewed
superficially is worse than an unreviewed one, because it carries a signature.**

## Draft your own

1. **Your one-sentence view of what a review is for.** In your words, and it
   should be arguable — a view nobody could disagree with is not a view.
2. **Your ordering.** What you look at first, second, third, and why that order.
3. **A real thing you caught,** with enough detail to be asked about: what the
   code did, what was wrong, how you phrased it, what happened next.
4. **A real thing you got wrong or missed,** and what changed as a result.
5. **A time a reviewer was right and you resisted.** Name the technical point.
6. **Your phrasing conventions.** Severity labels, questions over assertions,
   when you move to a call.
7. **Your turnaround expectation,** and what you do when you cannot meet it.
8. **The thing you will not compromise on.** Most engineers have one — security,
   or tests on anything that touches money, or no unexplained magic numbers.
   Naming it makes the rest of the answer sound like it comes from a person.

<Callout type="tip" title="If you have not reviewed much code">
Say so directly and pivot to what you do have. "My current team does lightweight
review, so most of what I've done is on my own code" is honest, and you can
follow it with what you look for when re-reading your own work before pushing,
plus what you would want from a reviewer. Interviewers respond well to that.
Claiming a review culture you do not have gets you a follow-up about your team's
process that you cannot answer.
</Callout>

## Follow-ups you will get

| Follow-up | What they are checking |
|---|---|
| "Tell me about a time you had to reject a pull request." | Whether you can hold a line. |
| "What do you do when the author disagrees with you?" | Conflict handling in writing. |
| "How do you review code in a part of the system you don't know?" | Honesty — the answer is you ask, or you review narrowly and say so. |
| "How do you handle reviewing someone more senior than you?" | Whether hierarchy stops you raising real issues. |
| "What's the last review comment you received that changed your mind?" | Coachability, and whether your answer was rehearsed. |
| "How do you keep reviews from blocking the team?" | Pragmatism. |
| "Do you approve with comments, or wait?" | Team-fit; either answer is fine with a reason. |

## Weak versus strong

<Callout type="pitfall" title="What a weak answer sounds like">
"I review PRs carefully and look for correctness, readability, and adherence to
best practices. I try to give constructive feedback and I'm always open to
feedback myself. I think code reviews are important for knowledge sharing."

Nothing here is false and nothing here is evidence. There is no ordering, no
example, no phrasing, no time you were wrong, and no acknowledgement that reviews
have a cost. It is what someone who has read about code review says.
</Callout>

<Callout type="interview" title="What a strong answer sounds like">
A one-sentence view with an actual opinion in it. A severity ladder, stated as an
ordering rather than a list. One blocking thing you caught and how you worded it.
One non-blocking thing you flagged as a decision rather than a defect. A time a
reviewer was right and you resisted, named specifically. Your rule for escalating
a comment thread to a call. And an honest word about turnaround.

Around three to four minutes, and every part of it is checkable.
</Callout>
