## What this question is really probing

Every candidate knows they are supposed to sound collaborative, so everyone
performs collaboration. The interviewer knows that, and is therefore looking past
the surface for three things:

- **Can you disagree at all?** A candidate who has never had a technical
  disagreement worth describing is either not engaged enough to have opinions or
  is sanitising. Both are problems. Conflict avoidance is a real negative signal
  at senior level, because senior engineers are hired partly to push back.
- **Can you be wrong?** The most valuable conflict story is one where you lost or
  changed your mind. It is also the one candidates almost never tell, because
  they think the question is asking them to win.
- **What do you do after the decision?** Whether you commit to a decision you
  argued against is close to the whole question for anyone hiring at senior level.

There is also a trap built into the phrasing. "How do you handle disagreements"
invites a process answer — "I listen to the other perspective and we find common
ground." That is a fine sentence and it is worth nothing on its own. **Answer
with a process in one sentence and then spend the rest of the time on one
specific disagreement.**

## The structure that works

```
1. The disagreement       both positions, stated fairly
2. Why it mattered        stakes — otherwise this is bikeshedding
3. What you did first     understand their position before arguing yours
4. How you moved it       data, a prototype, a written doc, a small experiment
5. The outcome            who was right, honestly
6. What you did after     especially if you lost
7. The relationship       what it is like with that person now
```

**Step 1 is the one that quietly decides the answer.** If you cannot state the
other person's position in a way they would agree with, you did not understand
it, and the interviewer can hear that instantly. Steel-man it. Say the strongest
version of their case, not the version that makes your case look good.

Step 5 is where you should resist the urge to have been right. A story where you
lost and the decision turned out fine is stronger than a story where you won.

## A worked example from the url-shortener

The `url-shortener` project has a decision in it that is a genuine two-sided
technical argument: the `RateLimitInterceptor` is Redis-backed — `INCR` plus
`EXPIRE` on a per-IP key — and it **fails open**. If Redis is unreachable, the
interceptor lets every request through unlimited.

That is a real disagreement waiting to happen, because both positions are
defensible:

- **Fail open.** A redirect service exists to redirect. Degrading to "unlimited
  traffic" keeps the product working; degrading to "everything is rejected"
  means a Redis blip takes down a service that does not otherwise need Redis. A
  protective measure that can cause the outage it protects against is badly
  designed.
- **Fail closed.** If the limiter exists to stop abuse, failing open means the
  protection evaporates at exactly the moment an attacker would most like it to —
  and an attacker who can knock over Redis has also disabled the limiter. A
  limit you cannot rely on is not a limit.

Told as an answer, with the second position held by someone else:

> **The disagreement.** "We disagreed about what the rate limiter should do when
> Redis is unavailable. I'd built it to fail open. A colleague argued it should
> fail closed, and their case was genuinely strong: a rate limiter that
> disappears under pressure isn't a control, it's a decoration, and the failure
> mode is correlated with the attack."
>
> **Why it mattered.** "It wasn't a style question. It decided which of two
> outages we'd have. Fail open means a Redis incident becomes an abuse incident;
> fail closed means a Redis incident becomes a total outage of the redirect
> path — which is the entire product."
>
> **What I did first.** "I asked what they were actually worried about, because
> I'd assumed it was correctness and it turned out to be a specific scenario:
> someone deliberately overwhelming Redis in order to bypass the limit. That's a
> better argument than the one I'd been preparing to counter, and it changed what
> I thought the disagreement was about."
>
> **How I moved it.** "I stopped arguing the principle and looked at what we
> actually had. Redis was a single instance with no replication, so its
> availability was worse than the application's — under fail-closed, the most
> likely outcome by a wide margin was a self-inflicted outage from an
> infrastructure blip, not a thwarted attacker. I wrote that up in about half a
> page with the two failure modes and rough likelihoods rather than making the
> case verbally, because a written comparison is much harder to argue past than
> a conversation."
>
> **The outcome.** "We landed on something neither of us proposed. Fail open, but
> with the failure made loud — a metric and an alert when the limiter is
> degraded, so we know we're unprotected rather than finding out later. And we
> agreed that if Redis ever became a replicated, genuinely highly available
> dependency, fail-closed becomes the better default and we should revisit it.
> Their concern was right; the conclusion depended on infrastructure we didn't
> have."
>
> **Afterwards.** "I added the alert and a comment in the interceptor explaining
> that failing open is deliberate, with a pointer to the reasoning — because the
> next person to read an empty catch block will assume it's a bug."
>
> **Learning.** "What I took from it was to find out what specific scenario
> someone is worried about before arguing against their position. I'd spent two
> days preparing a counter-argument to a point they weren't making."

Note what makes this work. The other position is stated strongly. The resolution
is neither person's original proposal. The deciding factor is a fact about the
system rather than seniority or volume. And the ending includes a concrete
artifact — the alert and the comment.

## The disagree-and-commit ending

Have a second, shorter story ready where **you lost and executed anyway.** This
is the one hiring managers listen for, and a bank of conflict stories where you
were always eventually vindicated is a yellow flag.

The shape is short:

> "I argued for X, the team went with Y, and I thought Y was the wrong call. I
> said so once more in writing so the reasoning was on record, and then I built Y
> properly — I didn't want it to fail because I'd half-built it. It worked out
> better than I expected, mainly because I'd underweighted how much the team's
> familiarity with it mattered. I still think X was technically cleaner, and I
> also think we made the right decision."

That last sentence — holding a technical view while endorsing the decision — is
the thing being tested. It is also true, which is why it is worth saying.

<Callout type="pitfall" title="The sabotage tell">
Interviewers listen for whether you undermined the decision after losing.
Phrasings that hurt you: "I did it their way but I made sure everyone knew it
would fail", "I was proven right six months later", "I let them find out the hard
way". Being right is not the point; being someone a team can make a decision with
is the point.
</Callout>

## Draft your own

Prepare two: one where you prevailed or reached a synthesis, and one where you
lost.

1. **The two positions.** Write theirs first, in the strongest form you can
   manage. If it sounds stupid when you write it, you have not understood it.
2. **The stakes.** What was the actual consequence of getting it wrong? If you
   cannot answer this, pick a different disagreement — a story about tabs versus
   spaces makes you look like someone who fights about tabs versus spaces.
3. **What you did to understand their position.** Specifically: what you asked,
   what you read, what you tried.
4. **What changed the conversation.** A measurement, a prototype, a written
   comparison, a spike, a customer data point, a third opinion. Something
   external to both of you is much stronger than persuasion.
5. **Who was right.** Honestly. Partial credit is realistic and sounds it.
6. **What you did after the decision.** Especially if it went against you.
7. **What the relationship is like now.** One sentence. "We still disagree about
   this and it's fine" is a good answer.
8. **What you would do differently.** Usually some version of "raise it earlier",
   "write it down sooner", or "find out what they were actually worried about".

## Other disagreement material in this project

- **Making click recording asynchronous.** A real tradeoff between redirect
  latency and losing click events on a crash. Reasonable people argue both that
  analytics counts should not be lossy and that analytics is exactly where
  lossiness is acceptable, which is what makes it a good story.
- **The seven-character random code with a retry loop.** `generateUniqueCode()`
  checks `existsByShortCode` and then inserts — a check-then-act race. A
  colleague might reasonably say the probability is negligible; the counter is
  that the failure surfaces as a 500 and costs nothing to handle properly.
- **Caching at all.** Someone arguing the database can handle it and the cache
  adds a consistency problem is a disagreement you can lose gracefully — and it
  connects to the expiry bug in
  [Story 1](/behavioral/story-1-tell-me-about-a-challenging-bug-you-solved),
  where the cache did introduce a correctness problem.

## Follow-ups you will get

| Follow-up | What they are checking |
|---|---|
| "Tell me about a time you were wrong." | The main event. Have this ready as its own story. |
| "What if they still disagreed after all that?" | Escalation judgement — do you know when to involve a manager? |
| "Have you ever disagreed with your manager?" | Whether hierarchy silences you. |
| "How did the other person describe this?" | Whether you can hold their perspective. Very revealing. |
| "What would you do if you'd lost that one?" | Disagree-and-commit, asked hypothetically. |
| "How long did the disagreement go on?" | Whether you let things fester or resolve them. |
| "Did it affect how you work together?" | Whether conflict costs you relationships. |

On escalation specifically: the defensible line is that you escalate when the
decision is blocking work and two people have stopped making progress, you
escalate the *decision* rather than the person, and you tell them you are doing
it. Escalating quickly is not weakness; escalating silently is.

## Weak versus strong

<Callout type="pitfall" title="What a weak answer sounds like">
"I had a disagreement with a teammate about using Redis versus an in-memory
cache. They wanted Redis even though we only had one instance. I explained that
Redis would add unnecessary complexity, and eventually they came around and
agreed with me. We ended up using the simpler solution and it worked well."

The other position is stated as obviously wrong. Nothing external decided it —
the resolution is "they came around", which means you out-argued them. You were
right, they were wrong, nothing was learned, and there is no artifact. The
interviewer's next question will be "tell me about a time you were the one who
came around", and if that answer does not exist, this one retroactively looks
worse.
</Callout>

<Callout type="interview" title="What a strong answer sounds like">
Both positions stated fairly, with theirs first and at full strength. Real stakes
named. Evidence that you tried to understand before you tried to persuade. Something
external — data, a prototype, a written comparison — moved the decision rather
than force of argument. An honest verdict, ideally partial. A concrete artifact
that came out of it. And a second, shorter story where you lost and built their
version properly anyway.
</Callout>
