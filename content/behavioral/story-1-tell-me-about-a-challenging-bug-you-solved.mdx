## What this question is really probing

On the surface it asks about a bug. What the interviewer is collecting is your
**debugging method** — whether you form hypotheses and eliminate them, or whether
you change things until the symptom goes away.

Three signals in particular:

- **Did you reason, or did you guess?** The tell is whether your narrative
  contains things you ruled out. Someone who only reports the answer usually
  found it by luck or is retelling someone else's investigation.
- **Did you find the cause or the symptom?** Restarting the service fixes many
  things and explains none of them.
- **Did you do anything about the class of bug?** A senior engineer adds the
  monitoring, the test or the environment change that would have caught it. A
  mid-level engineer fixes the line and moves on.

There is a fourth, unstated one: **is this story real?** This is the question
interviewers drill hardest on, because it is the easiest one to fabricate and the
easiest to catch. Which is why the rest of this lesson is about building the
story from something you actually did.

## The structure that works for this question

STAR applies, but the Action section has its own internal shape for debugging
stories, and following it is most of what makes the answer sound credible:

```
1. The symptom          what was observed, by whom, how it was noticed
2. Why it was hard      what made it non-obvious — intermittent, no error, etc.
3. What you ruled out   two or three hypotheses eliminated, and how
4. The breakthrough     the observation that cracked it, and how you got it
5. The root cause       stated in one precise sentence
6. The fix              what you changed, and why that and not something else
7. The systemic change  the test, the metric, the environment fix
```

Step 3 is the one candidates skip and the one interviewers value most. Step 7 is
the one that reads as seniority. **Spend most of your time in steps 3 through 7 —
the symptom and the setup should take under a minute.**

## A worked example from the url-shortener

This is built from a bug that genuinely exists in the `url-shortener` project.
Read it as a demonstration of specificity, and only tell it if you found it.

In `UrlService.resolve()`, the redirect path is cache-aside: check Redis for the
short code, return the long URL if present, otherwise read Postgres, populate
Redis with a 24-hour TTL, and return. Standard, and correct as far as it goes.

The expiry check — whether the link's `expiresAt` has passed — lives only in the
database-fallback branch. On a cache hit, nothing checks it. So a link that
expires while it is sitting in Redis keeps redirecting successfully for up to
another twenty-four hours.

<Callout type="note" title="Why this makes a good story">
It has every property interviewers like. The symptom is intermittent and
user-visible. It cannot be reproduced locally if your local setup has a cold
cache or no Redis at all. The code that is wrong looks completely reasonable in
review. And the root cause is a general principle — **a cache does not just store
data, it stores the outcome of every check you performed before you wrote to it** —
which gives you something to say when they ask what you learned.
</Callout>

Told as an answer, roughly four minutes:

> **Situation and task.** "I built a URL shortener — Spring Boot, Postgres,
> Redis as a read cache on the redirect path. Links can have an expiry date.
> I was testing expiry behaviour before adding a cleanup job and found that
> expired links were still resolving." *(Around thirty seconds. That is all the
> context needed.)*
>
> **Why it was hard.** "It was intermittent in a way that made no sense at first.
> The same short code would 404 correctly one minute and redirect the next. My
> integration tests all passed, because each test starts with a clean Redis."
>
> **What I ruled out.** "My first hypothesis was a timezone problem — that
> `expiresAt` was being compared against the wrong clock. I checked the column
> type and the comparison and they were consistent in UTC, and an expired link
> queried directly through the repository correctly returned nothing. Second
> hypothesis was clock drift between the app and the database. I ruled that out
> because the behaviour depended on *which* request I made rather than on when."
>
> **The breakthrough.** "The thing that cracked it was noticing the correlation
> with cache state rather than with time. If I flushed Redis, the link 404'd
> correctly every time. If I hit it once before it expired and then again after,
> it kept redirecting. That pointed at the cache, not the clock."
>
> **Root cause.** "The expiry check was only in the database branch of the
> cache-aside read. A cache hit returned the URL without ever evaluating expiry —
> so the cache was effectively serving a stale authorization decision, not just
> stale data."
>
> **The fix.** "The narrow fix would have been to also check expiry on the cache
> hit path, which means caching `expiresAt` alongside the URL. I did that, but I
> also set the Redis TTL to the minimum of the standard 24 hours and the time
> remaining until the link expires, so the entry cannot outlive the link. Two
> layers, because the TTL alone still leaves a window if expiry is changed after
> the write."
>
> **Systemic change.** "The reason this got past me was that my tests ran against
> a cold cache. I added a test that populates the cache, advances expiry, and
> asserts the 404 — and more generally I stopped treating the cache as an
> invisible optimisation in tests."
>
> **Learning.** "The lesson I actually took is that anything you check *before*
> writing to a cache is something the cache will now serve you a stale answer
> for. Expiry, permissions, feature flags. I look for that pattern specifically
> now whenever I see cache-aside code."

Notice what is doing the work there: two ruled-out hypotheses, a named
breakthrough observation, a root cause stated in one sentence, a fix with a
justification for choosing it over the simpler option, and a general principle at
the end.

## Other bugs in the same project you could use

If the expiry bug is not one you found, these are also real and each supports a
different emphasis. Again — only if you actually investigated it.

- **`generateUniqueCode()` has a check-then-act race.** It generates a random
  seven-character Base62 code and loops while `existsByShortCode` returns true.
  Between the check and the insert, another request can take the same code, and
  the unique constraint violation surfaces as a 500 rather than a retry. This is
  a good story if you want to talk about concurrency, about why it is almost
  impossible to reproduce by hand, and about the difference between "unlikely"
  and "impossible" at scale.
- **`RateLimitInterceptor` uses `request.getRemoteAddr()`.** Behind a load
  balancer or reverse proxy that returns the proxy's address, so every user
  shares one rate-limit bucket. Good for a bug that only appears after a
  deployment topology change.
- **`resolve()` is annotated `@Transactional`.** Even a pure cache hit that
  touches no data borrows a pooled connection for the duration. Good for a story
  about resource exhaustion, and about framework annotations having costs that
  are invisible at the call site.

## Draft your own

Work through these in writing. The prompts are in the order you should say them.

1. **The bug.** What was observed, and by whom? One or two sentences. If it was
   user-visible, say what the user saw.
2. **Why it was not obvious.** Pick the honest reason: intermittent, no error in
   the logs, worked locally, the wrong component looked guilty, the error message
   was misleading.
3. **Your first hypothesis, and how you eliminated it.** If you cannot remember
   one, this story may be too old or too easy — pick a different bug.
4. **Your second hypothesis, and how you eliminated it.**
5. **The observation that cracked it.** Be specific about *how* you got it: a log
   line you added, a metric, a database query you ran, a profiler, bisecting the
   change history, a colleague's offhand comment.
6. **The root cause in one sentence.** If it takes three sentences, you have not
   finished understanding it.
7. **The fix, and one alternative you rejected.** Naming the rejected option is
   what turns a fix into a decision.
8. **What you changed so this class of bug cannot recur.** Test, metric, alert,
   environment, review checklist.
9. **Time and impact.** How long the investigation took, and what the bug was
   costing while it existed.
10. **The general principle.** One sentence you now apply elsewhere.

<Callout type="tip" title="If your best bug is from a side project">
That is fine, and you should say so plainly. "This is from a service I built
myself rather than production work" costs you nothing if the reasoning is real,
and interviewers ask about side projects specifically because the ownership is
unambiguous. What costs you is presenting a side project as production work and
then being unable to answer "how many users did this affect?"
</Callout>

## Follow-ups you will get, and what each one checks

| Follow-up | What they are checking |
|---|---|
| "What was the actual error message?" | Whether you were there. The most common story-collapse point. |
| "How did you first notice it?" | Whether you have monitoring instincts or wait for users to complain. |
| "What else did you consider before you found it?" | Debugging method. If you have nothing, it reads as guessing. |
| "How long did it take?" | Calibration, and honesty. Three days is a perfectly good answer. |
| "How did you verify the fix?" | Rigour. "It stopped happening" is weaker than a test or a metric. |
| "What would have caught this earlier?" | Seniority. This is the systemic-change question. |
| "Did anything similar happen again?" | Whether the systemic change actually worked. |
| "Who else was involved?" | Collaboration, and whether you are claiming a team's work. |

Prepare an answer to every one of these before the interview. They are
predictable, and a story that has survived all eight in your own kitchen will
survive the interview.

## Weak versus strong

<Callout type="pitfall" title="What a weak answer sounds like">
"We had a really tricky production bug where the API was slow. It took us a while
to figure out, but eventually we found it was a database issue. We fixed the
query and added an index, and after that performance was much better. It was a
good learning experience about the importance of monitoring."

Why it fails: no symptom detail, no timeline, no hypotheses, "we" throughout so
the contribution is invisible, "a database issue" is not a root cause, no
numbers, and the learning is a platitude. An interviewer cannot write anything on
a scorecard from this. The next question will be "what was the query?" and the
answer will decide the round.
</Callout>

<Callout type="interview" title="What a strong answer sounds like">
Specific symptom with a time and an observer. One sentence on why it was hard.
Two hypotheses named and eliminated with the evidence that eliminated them. A
breakthrough observation and how it was obtained. A root cause in one precise
sentence. A fix, plus the alternative you rejected and why. A systemic change so
the class of bug cannot recur. A general principle in one line.

Roughly four minutes, and every claim in it survives a follow-up because you were
there.
</Callout>
