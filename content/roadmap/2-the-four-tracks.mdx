## The four things you are being graded on

An interview loop is not one assessment with several parts. It is four separate
assessments that happen to occur on the same day, scored by different people
against different rubrics, and then combined. That structure has one dominant
consequence: **your weakest track sets your ceiling**, because a weak round is
rarely rescued by a strong one.

| Track | Rounds it feeds | Share of outcome at mid-to-senior | Your position |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A. DSA and coding** | OA, phone screen, 2 onsite rounds | ~30% | Strong — maintenance, not growth |
| **B. Java and SQL depth** | Backend deep dive, project interrogation | ~20% | Good foundations, thin on internals |
| **C. System design (HLD + LLD)** | 1–2 onsite rounds, sometimes machine coding | ~35% | **The gap. Main focus.** |
| **D. CS fundamentals + behavioral** | Breadth questions, HM round | ~15% | Unprepared rather than weak |

The percentages are a rough weighting, not a published rubric — companies differ
and the split moves with level. Treat them as a guide to time allocation rather
than a fact. The one number that is not negotiable in spirit is track C: at
senior level, design is where candidates with strong coding scores get rejected.

## Track A — DSA and coding

### What it is assessed on

Not whether you can solve hard problems given a week. Four narrower things:

- **Pattern recognition speed.** Can you tell within two or three minutes that
  this is a sliding window problem? Interview problems are almost always a known
  pattern in unfamiliar clothing, and the candidates who do well are the ones who
  recognise rather than derive.
- **Optimal complexity.** A working brute force scores partial credit. Reaching
  the intended complexity is the bar, and you should be able to state it.
- **Correctness on edge cases.** Empty input, one element, duplicates, overflow,
  the boundary of the loop. Interviewers keep a mental list.
- **Communication while solving.** Silence is scored as absence of reasoning.

### Where you stand

Strong. Several hundred problems solved is well past the threshold where
additional volume helps. **The risk on this track is not weakness, it is
rust** — pattern recall degrades fast and comes back fast.

Your realistic failure mode is freezing on a problem you would solve comfortably
at your desk, because you have not done a timed, narrated attempt in a year.

### What to do, in order

1. Take [how to approach any coding problem](/dsa/how-to-approach-any-coding-problem-say-this-out-loud-in-the)
   and use its script on every practice problem from now on. The script is the
   thing that transfers to the interview, not the problems.
2. Diagnose: skim the fifteen pattern lessons from
   [arrays and hashing](/dsa/pattern-1-arrays-hashing) through
   [bit manipulation and math](/dsa/pattern-15-bit-manipulation-math), and for
   each one, without solving anything, decide whether you could write the
   template from memory. You will find three or four you cannot. Those are your
   weak patterns.
3. Drill only the weak ones. For most people coming back after a gap that means
   [dynamic programming](/dsa/pattern-12-dynamic-programming),
   [graphs](/dsa/pattern-11-graphs-bfs-dfs-union-find),
   [backtracking](/dsa/pattern-10-backtracking) and
   [tries](/dsa/pattern-8-tries-prefix-trees).
4. Keep [the complexity cheat sheet](/dsa/complexity-cheat-sheet-memorize-this)
   genuinely memorised. You will be asked to state complexity out loud and
   hesitating there costs more than it should.
5. Then switch to maintenance: one or two timed mediums a day, narrated out
   loud, forever. [The six-week sprint](/dsa/6-week-dsa-sprint-pairs-with-the-master-roadmap-phase-1-2)
   is the structured version if you want one.
6. Before the loop, read
   [coding round survival tips](/dsa/coding-round-survival-tips).

**Time budget: 30 to 45 minutes a day, as a secondary activity.** Do not let this
track expand — it is the most comfortable one and therefore the one that steals
time from track C.

## Track B — Java and SQL depth

### What it is assessed on

This is the round where the interviewer stops asking puzzles and starts asking
whether you understand the tools you claim to use. It is heavily biased toward
things you would only know from having hit them.

- **Language semantics below the API.** How HashMap actually resolves collisions,
  what `volatile` guarantees, why `equals` and `hashCode` travel together, what
  the JVM does with your garbage.
- **Framework behaviour, not framework usage.** What `@Transactional` does to a
  self-invocation, when a lazy association throws, what the persistence context
  is doing between your two repository calls.
- **SQL that goes past joins.** Window functions, what the planner does with your
  query, which index a predicate can actually use.
- **Your own project, interrogated.** Expect forty-five minutes of "why did you
  do it that way?" on whatever you listed.

### Where you stand

Foundations are solid — Spring Boot basics, working SQL, a real project. The gap
is depth: knowing that JPA has lazy loading versus being able to explain what
happens to a lazy collection after the session closes and why.

### What to do, in order

Core Java first, because it underpins everything else:

1. [Collections framework deep dive](/java/2-collections-framework-deep-dive) —
   the most reliably asked topic in the entire track.
2. [Concurrency](/java/3-concurrency-senior-must-have) — the actual senior
   differentiator, and the one people bluff. Do the
   [producer-consumer](/java/practice-producer-consumer) and
   [thread-safe singleton](/java/practice-thread-safe-singleton) exercises rather
   than only reading.
3. [Java 8+ features](/java/4-java-8-features) and
   [OOP and design principles](/java/5-oop-design-principles) — the second feeds
   straight into LLD.
4. [JVM internals](/java/1-jvm-internals) — enough to answer a garbage collection
   question without guessing.
5. [Exception handling](/java/6-exception-handling) and
   [generics](/java/7-generics) as a second pass.

Then the Spring and data layer, which is where your project lives:

6. [Spring Data JPA and Hibernate](/java/2-spring-data-jpa-hibernate), then
   [the N+1 problem](/java/3-the-n-1-problem-most-asked-jpa-interview-question) —
   which is asked more often than any other JPA question, by a distance.
7. [Transaction management](/java/5-transaction-management-transactional) — read
   this one carefully, because it explains why the `@Transactional` on your
   URL shortener's resolve method is a problem.
8. [Spring internals — IoC, beans and AOP proxies](/java/8-spring-internals-ioc-beans-and-aop-proxies),
   which is what makes the previous lesson make sense.

SQL is a refresher rather than a build, since you already know it:

9. [Indexing and performance](/sql/3-indexing-performance) and
   [query optimization](/sql/5-query-optimization-checklist) — the two most
   likely to come up in a backend round.
10. [Window functions](/sql/2-window-functions-the-senior-differentiator) if they
    are rusty, then
    [practice problems](/sql/practice-sql-problems) to keep them warm.

Before the loop, skim
[what seniors say in interviews](/java/quick-reference-what-seniors-say-in-interviews) —
it is a list of phrasings that signal depth, which is a real and slightly
cynical part of how this round is scored.

**Time budget: three to four weeks as a primary track, then light maintenance.**

## Track C — System design, HLD and LLD

### What it is assessed on

These are two different rounds with two different rubrics, and conflating them is
a common preparation mistake.

**HLD** is assessed on process more than output. There is no correct answer. The
interviewer is scoring whether you scoped the problem, estimated with numbers and
drew conclusions from them, justified each component by the bottleneck it solves,
went one level below the buzzword when pushed, and criticised your own design
before they did.

**LLD and machine coding** are assessed on class modelling under time pressure:
whether your abstractions are right, whether the design extends without
rewriting, whether you know the pattern vocabulary, and — in machine coding —
whether it actually runs.

### Where you stand

**This is the gap, and it is the one that decides the outcome at senior level.**
You have not done system design before, which means you are starting from zero on
roughly a third of the assessment.

There is one large compensating factor: you have built a URL shortener with
Postgres, Redis, JWT and a rate limiter. That is not a toy relative to the
canonical interview question — it *is* the canonical interview question, at small
scale. Most of the concepts in the HLD track already exist in your code, which
makes them far easier to internalise than they are for someone learning them
abstractly.

### What to do, in order

HLD first, because it is the round with more weight:

1. [The framework](/system-design/1-the-framework-how-to-drive-a-45-minute-design-interview) —
   read it twice. Everything else assumes the six phases.
2. [Back-of-the-envelope estimation](/system-design/2-back-of-the-envelope-estimation-numbers-to-memorize) —
   memorise the number set. This is genuinely rote and it pays for itself in
   every design round.
3. Building blocks in order:
   [scaling](/system-design/3-scaling-vertical-vs-horizontal),
   [load balancing](/system-design/4-load-balancing),
   [caching](/system-design/5-caching-your-strongest-topic-you-already-use-redis)
   (start here if you want a confidence win — you already do cache-aside),
   [databases](/system-design/6-databases),
   [CAP and consistency models](/system-design/7-cap-theorem-consistency-models),
   [async processing and queues](/system-design/8-asynchronous-processing-message-queues),
   [rate limiting](/system-design/9-rate-limiting-you-already-have-a-basic-version).
4. [Other building blocks](/system-design/10-other-building-blocks-to-know),
   [consistent hashing](/system-design/13-consistent-hashing),
   [Kafka](/system-design/14-kafka-and-event-streaming) and
   [microservice reliability patterns](/system-design/15-microservice-reliability-patterns)
   as the second layer.
5. [The reference architecture](/system-design/11-a-reference-architecture-how-the-pieces-fit)
   to tie the components together into one picture.
6. Then the case studies, starting with
   [the URL shortener](/system-design/case-1-design-a-url-shortener-your-project-master-this-one)
   because it is yours, through
   [rate limiter](/system-design/case-2-design-a-distributed-rate-limiter),
   [news feed](/system-design/case-3-design-a-news-feed-twitter-instagram-timeline),
   [chat](/system-design/case-4-design-a-chat-system-whatsapp-messenger) and
   [notifications](/system-design/case-5-design-a-notification-system-email-sms-push).
7. [How to practice](/system-design/12-how-to-practice-do-this-dont-just-read) and
   [how to self-grade](/system-design/how-to-self-grade-your-practice) — do not
   skip these. Reading case studies without timed, spoken attempts produces the
   illusion of competence and nothing else.

LLD in parallel or immediately after:

8. [HLD versus LLD](/lld/hld-vs-lld-get-this-clear-first) first, to stop the two
   rounds blurring together.
9. [OOP applied](/lld/part-1-the-foundation-oop-the-4-pillars-applied) and
   [SOLID](/lld/part-2-solid-principles-the-heart-of-every-lld-interview) — SOLID
   is the vocabulary the round is graded in.
10. [Design patterns](/lld/part-3-design-patterns-the-lld-vocabulary), then
    [the method](/lld/part-4-how-to-drive-an-lld-interview-the-method) and the
    [worked parking lot](/lld/part-5-worked-example-design-a-parking-lot-the-canonical).
11. [Practice problems](/lld/part-6-practice-problems-beginner-advanced) with the
    [four-week plan](/lld/part-7-your-4-week-lld-plan-youre-a-beginner-so-build),
    finishing with
    [machine coding survival tips](/lld/part-8-machine-coding-round-survival-tips).

**Time budget: this is the primary track for roughly half of your total
preparation time.** If something has to be cut, cut track A maintenance, not
this.

## Track D — CS fundamentals and behavioral

### What it is assessed on

Two quite different things bundled together because they are both breadth.

**CS fundamentals** appears as five-minute detours rather than a dedicated round:
what happens when you type a URL into a browser, how TCP differs from UDP, what a
deadlock is, what idempotency means. The bar is a clear two-minute answer, not
depth. Fumbling one of these is a small negative that accumulates.

**Behavioral** is assessed on evidence of ownership, conflict handling and
accountability. It is the round most people under-prepare, and — as
[the hiring process lesson](/roadmap/1-how-product-company-hiring-actually-works)
explains — at senior level it is frequently the deciding input.

### Where you stand

Not weak, unprepared. You have the knowledge for most of the fundamentals; what
you lack is the ability to produce a crisp spoken answer. Behavioral is a genuine
build, because it requires writing and rehearsing your own stories.

### What to do, in order

1. Read the four CS fundamentals lessons once each:
   [networking](/cs-fundamentals/1-networking),
   [operating systems and concurrency](/cs-fundamentals/2-operating-systems-concurrency),
   [distributed systems](/cs-fundamentals/3-distributed-systems-concepts),
   [security](/cs-fundamentals/4-security-also-see-your-projects-owasp-requirements).
   The security one connects directly to the hardcoded credentials in your
   project's configuration, which is worth fixing before anyone reads it.
2. Then use [the rapid-fire self-test](/cs-fundamentals/5-rapid-fire-self-test-answer-out-loud)
   as the actual study method. Answer out loud, timed. Anything you cannot answer
   in two minutes goes back on the list.
3. Behavioral: [general behavioral tips](/behavioral/general-behavioral-tips)
   first, then draft your own stories through the four story lessons. Budget an
   evening for the anchor lesson and an hour per story, most of it writing.

**Time budget: two to three hours a week, spread out.** This track responds badly
to cramming and well to repetition.

## What to do with this

Go to [the integrated plan](/roadmap/3-the-integrated-plan-everything-sequenced-your-chart),
which sequences all four into weeks with one primary focus at a time. The
ordering there is not arbitrary: it front-loads track C because it is your gap
and because it is the slowest to improve.
