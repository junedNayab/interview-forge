## The premise

Four tracks, a full-time job, and roughly fifteen to twenty hours a week. That
adds up if you sequence it and does not if you swarm it. Trying to make progress
on Java, SQL, DSA, HLD, LLD, CS fundamentals and behavioral simultaneously
produces seven shallow tracks and the distinct feeling of working hard while
nothing improves.

Three rules make it fit:

**One primary track per phase.** Each phase has a single thing you go deep on —
ninety to a hundred and twenty minutes a day. Everything else is thirty to
forty-five minutes of maintenance, not deep work.

**Let things double-count.** Learning `@Transactional` properly is Java track
work, it is also material for the project deep dive, and it is also the
explanation for a bug in your URL shortener that becomes a behavioral story. The
plan is built to exploit these overlaps rather than treating each track as
separate hours.

**The gap gets the most time.** System design is roughly six of the fifteen
weeks, because you are starting from zero on it and because it is the slowest
track to improve. Everything else is either maintenance or a build you can do
quickly.

## The shape at a glance

| Phase | Weeks | Primary (90–120 min/day) | Secondary (30–45 min) | Running in background |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **0. Setup** | 0 | Resume, diagnosis, environment | — | — |
| **1. Backend depth** | 1–3 | Java internals, Spring/JPA, SQL refresh | DSA weak patterns | Resume out to 5 companies |
| **2. System design (HLD)** | 4–9 | HLD building blocks, then case studies | DSA maintenance | Behavioral drafting, 1 evening/week |
| **3. LLD and machine coding** | 10–12 | OOP, SOLID, patterns, timed builds | DSA maintenance | Applications, CS rapid-fire |
| **4. Integration** | 13–15 | Mocks across all round types | Weak-area patching | Live loops |

Total: roughly 250 to 300 hours. If you have more time per week, compress
proportionally — full-time, this is eight to ten weeks. What does not compress is
phase 2, because design skill accumulates through repeated spoken attempts and
you cannot do twelve of those in a weekend.

## Phase 0 — Setup (week 0, about six hours)

The point of this phase is to get the slow-moving things started before you need
them, and to find out where you actually are rather than where you assume.

**Do these, in this order:**

1. **Rewrite the resume.** Follow
   [resume guidance](/roadmap/6-resume-guidance-projects-skills-sections).
   This comes first because the application pipeline has a four-to-six-week
   delay built into it, and because writing honest project bullets forces you to
   notice what you cannot defend.
2. **Run the DSA diagnostic.** Skim the fifteen pattern lessons and, without
   solving anything, mark the ones whose template you could not write from
   memory. Then solve one timed medium in two different patterns to calibrate
   how rusty you actually are.
3. **Read [the framework](/system-design/1-the-framework-how-to-drive-a-45-minute-design-interview)
   once.** Not to learn it — to see the shape of what you are about to spend six
   weeks on.
4. **Set up the tracker.** See
   [progress tracking](/roadmap/5-progress-tracking). A spreadsheet is fine.
5. **Fix the hardcoded credentials in your project.** The JWT signing secret and
   the database username and password in `application.yml` should come from
   environment variables, and the committed values should be treated as
   compromised and rotated. Do this now, because you are about to start showing
   this repository to people and it is the first thing a senior engineer will
   notice.

**Done looks like:** a resume you would send today, a marked-up list of weak DSA
patterns, a tracker with rows in it, and a project you are not embarrassed to
link.

## Phase 1 — Backend depth (weeks 1 to 3)

**Why first.** It is the fastest track to move, it is the foundation for LLD
(OOP and SOLID), it is what the project deep dive tests, and it produces the raw
material for two or three behavioral stories. Three weeks here makes the next
twelve more productive.

### Week 1 — Core Java

[Collections deep dive](/java/2-collections-framework-deep-dive), then
[concurrency](/java/3-concurrency-senior-must-have) — which is the one to slow
down on, and the one to do by writing code rather than reading. Work
[producer-consumer](/java/practice-producer-consumer) and
[the thread-safe singleton](/java/practice-thread-safe-singleton) by hand.
Finish with [Java 8+ features](/java/4-java-8-features).

Secondary: two weak DSA patterns, one problem each per day, timed and narrated.

### Week 2 — Spring, JPA and your project

[Spring Data JPA and Hibernate](/java/2-spring-data-jpa-hibernate), then
[the N+1 problem](/java/3-the-n-1-problem-most-asked-jpa-interview-question),
then [transaction management](/java/5-transaction-management-transactional) and
[Spring internals](/java/8-spring-internals-ioc-beans-and-aop-proxies).

Then apply it: go through the URL shortener and write down, for each decision,
why it is that way and what you would change. You will find the `@Transactional`
on the redirect path, the synchronous click insert, and the database query that
still runs on every cache hit. **Write these down — they are the substance of
your project deep dive and of two behavioral stories.**

Secondary: two more weak DSA patterns.

### Week 3 — SQL and OOP

[Indexing and performance](/sql/3-indexing-performance),
[query optimization](/sql/5-query-optimization-checklist),
[window functions](/sql/2-window-functions-the-senior-differentiator), and
[practice problems](/sql/practice-sql-problems) until they are automatic.
Then [OOP and design principles](/java/5-oop-design-principles), which is the
bridge into phase 3.

Secondary: remaining weak DSA patterns. Send the resume to five companies at the
end of this week — not your top choices.

**Phase 1 done looks like:**

- You can explain, out loud and without notes, how HashMap handles collisions,
  what `volatile` guarantees, what the persistence context does between two
  repository calls, and why a self-invoked `@Transactional` method does nothing.
- You can write the N+1 problem on a whiteboard and give three fixes with their
  tradeoffs.
- You have a written page of "here is what is wrong with my project and why",
  covering at least four issues.
- Every DSA pattern template is writable from memory.

## Phase 2 — System design (weeks 4 to 9)

**Six weeks, the longest phase, because it is the gap.** Resist the urge to
shorten it. This is also the phase where the temptation to substitute reading for
practice is strongest, and where doing so is most costly.

### Weeks 4 and 5 — The building blocks

Two lessons every three days, in order:
[the framework](/system-design/1-the-framework-how-to-drive-a-45-minute-design-interview)
again (properly this time),
[estimation](/system-design/2-back-of-the-envelope-estimation-numbers-to-memorize),
[scaling](/system-design/3-scaling-vertical-vs-horizontal),
[load balancing](/system-design/4-load-balancing),
[caching](/system-design/5-caching-your-strongest-topic-you-already-use-redis),
[databases](/system-design/6-databases),
[CAP and consistency](/system-design/7-cap-theorem-consistency-models),
[queues](/system-design/8-asynchronous-processing-message-queues),
[rate limiting](/system-design/9-rate-limiting-you-already-have-a-basic-version).

The estimation numbers are rote. Memorise them in week 4 and use them in every
subsequent session; they are the difference between an estimate that sounds like
engineering and one that sounds like arithmetic anxiety.

### Week 6 — The second layer and the synthesis

[Other building blocks](/system-design/10-other-building-blocks-to-know),
[consistent hashing](/system-design/13-consistent-hashing),
[Kafka](/system-design/14-kafka-and-event-streaming),
[microservice reliability patterns](/system-design/15-microservice-reliability-patterns),
and then
[the reference architecture](/system-design/11-a-reference-architecture-how-the-pieces-fit),
which is where the components stop being a list and become a system.

From this week, add **one behavioral evening per week**: read
[general behavioral tips](/behavioral/general-behavioral-tips), then draft one
story a week. By the end of phase 3 you will have six or seven without ever
having spent a whole session on it.

### Weeks 7 to 9 — Case studies, done out loud

Read [how to practice](/system-design/12-how-to-practice-do-this-dont-just-read)
first, and then follow it literally. Each case study is one full session with a
45-minute timer, standing, talking, before you read the answer:

- Week 7: [URL shortener](/system-design/case-1-design-a-url-shortener-your-project-master-this-one)
  and [distributed rate limiter](/system-design/case-2-design-a-distributed-rate-limiter) —
  both of which you have partially built, which makes them the right place to
  start.
- Week 8: [news feed](/system-design/case-3-design-a-news-feed-twitter-instagram-timeline)
  and [chat](/system-design/case-4-design-a-chat-system-whatsapp-messenger).
- Week 9: [notifications](/system-design/case-5-design-a-notification-system-email-sms-push),
  then a second pass on whichever two went worst, then
  [self-grade](/system-design/how-to-self-grade-your-practice) all of them.

Do each case twice, a week apart. The second attempt is where the improvement
actually happens, and the gap between them is the clearest measurement you will
get of whether this is working.

**Phase 2 done looks like:**

- You can drive forty-five minutes on an unfamiliar design problem without being
  prompted, hitting all six phases, with time left for self-critique.
- You produce a capacity estimate in under five minutes and draw an explicit
  conclusion from it.
- For caching you can discuss eviction policy, TTL strategy, cache-aside versus
  write-through, stampede protection and hot keys — not just "add Redis".
- You can name the single points of failure in your own design before being
  asked.
- All five case studies attempted twice, recorded, self-graded.

At the end of week 8, **widen the application pipeline** to fifteen or twenty
companies. Loops will land in weeks 12 to 16, which is when you want them.

## Phase 3 — LLD and machine coding (weeks 10 to 12)

**Week 10 — Foundations.**
[HLD versus LLD](/lld/hld-vs-lld-get-this-clear-first) first so the two rounds
stop blurring, then
[OOP applied](/lld/part-1-the-foundation-oop-the-4-pillars-applied) and
[SOLID](/lld/part-2-solid-principles-the-heart-of-every-lld-interview). Do the
refactoring exercises rather than reading about them.

**Week 11 — Vocabulary and method.**
[Design patterns](/lld/part-3-design-patterns-the-lld-vocabulary) — know eight or
ten genuinely, rather than twenty-three by name. Then
[the method](/lld/part-4-how-to-drive-an-lld-interview-the-method) and the
[worked parking lot](/lld/part-5-worked-example-design-a-parking-lot-the-canonical),
which you should attempt yourself before reading.

**Week 12 — Timed builds.**
Three or four problems from
[the practice set](/lld/part-6-practice-problems-beginner-advanced), each under a
real ninety-minute timer with working code at the end. Then
[machine coding survival tips](/lld/part-8-machine-coding-round-survival-tips).
[The four-week plan](/lld/part-7-your-4-week-lld-plan-youre-a-beginner-so-build)
is the expanded version if this phase needs to be longer.

Background this phase: CS fundamentals on low-energy days — one lesson from
[CS fundamentals](/cs-fundamentals) per week, then
[the rapid-fire self-test](/cs-fundamentals/5-rapid-fire-self-test-answer-out-loud)
answered out loud.

**Phase 3 done looks like:**

- You can take a problem statement to a class diagram with clear responsibilities
  in fifteen minutes.
- You can name the SOLID principle a piece of code violates and show the fix.
- You have built three machine-coding problems inside ninety minutes with code
  that compiles and runs.
- You have a default starting structure you reach for under time pressure rather
  than designing from scratch.

## Phase 4 — Integration (weeks 13 to 15)

The content phase is over. This phase is entirely about performance under
pressure, and the failure mode is going back to reading because reading is
comfortable.

- **Two mocks a week minimum**, ideally with a person. Pramp and interviewing.io
  are the usual routes; see
  [where to actually solve problems](/roadmap/7-where-to-actually-solve-problems).
  Alternate round types.
- **Behavioral finished.** Six to eight stories written, rehearsed out loud,
  timed at four minutes, each surviving eight follow-ups.
- **Project defence rehearsed.** Forty-five minutes of someone asking why you
  built the URL shortener the way you did.
- **One or two timed mediums a day**, still narrated.
- **Patch weak areas** identified by the mocks, and nothing else. Resist starting
  a new topic in week 14.
- **Live loops running.** The first one will be your worst; schedule it
  accordingly.
- Before each onsite, work
  [the day-of checklist](/behavioral/day-of-interview-checklist).

**Phase 4 done looks like:** you are getting through onsites and the feedback,
where you get it, is about specific gaps rather than about structure or
communication.

## If you have less time than this

Compressions in order of least damage:

1. **Cut DSA maintenance to three days a week.** You are strong there; the
   returns are the lowest of anything in the plan.
2. **Cut phase 1 to two weeks** by doing only collections, concurrency, JPA and
   transactions, and skipping JVM internals and generics.
3. **Cut phase 3 to two weeks** if the companies you are targeting weight HLD
   over machine coding — ask the recruiter, because this varies a lot.
4. **Do not cut phase 2.** Six weeks of system design is the reason the plan
   exists. Cutting it returns you to the position you are trying to leave.

And if you fall behind, do not restart the plan. Move the phase boundary and
carry on; the sequence matters much more than the dates.
