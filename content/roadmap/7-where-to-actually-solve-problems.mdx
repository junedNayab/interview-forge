## How to think about external resources

The lessons on this site are a map. They tell you what to learn and in what
order, and they compress a lot of material into a readable form. What they
cannot do is give you volume and they cannot give you another human being. That
is what the outside resources are for.

Two failure modes to avoid. The first is collecting resources: buying three
books, bookmarking four courses, subscribing to two platforms, and using none of
them past chapter two. The second is finishing a resource for its own sake —
working through all 150 problems on a list because it is a list, when the last
sixty added nothing.

**Pick one primary resource per track, use it until it stops teaching you, and
then stop.** The time budgets below are rough guidance for the fifteen-week plan.

## DSA

### LeetCode — the primary, roughly 60 to 80 hours

There is no real alternative for volume, and the problems overlap heavily with
what companies actually ask. You already have several hundred solved, so your
use of it is different from a beginner's: you are maintaining and patching, not
building.

How to use it well at your stage:

- **Solve by pattern, not by list position.** Pick a weak pattern from
  [the DSA track](/dsa), then filter LeetCode by the corresponding tag and do
  six to eight in a row. Consecutive problems in one pattern build recognition
  in a way that shuffled problems do not.
- **Always set a timer.** Twenty-five minutes for a medium, and stop when it
  goes off whether or not you are done. Untimed practice trains a skill you
  will not be allowed to use.
- **Talk out loud.** This feels ridiculous alone at a desk and it is the single
  highest-value modification you can make, because verbalising while thinking is
  the actual bottleneck in a live round.
- **Use the company filter in the last three weeks,** once you have loops
  scheduled. Before that it is a distraction.
- **Re-solve rather than accumulate.** A problem you solved two months ago and
  can re-solve in fifteen minutes is worth more than a new one.

What it is bad for: it teaches nothing about communication, it rewards a
puzzle-solving style that is only loosely related to engineering, and the
discussion sections will happily give you an optimal solution before you have
struggled enough to learn anything.

### Neetcode — the pattern structure, roughly 10 hours

Neetcode's curated lists (the 150, and the roadmap view) are the best freely
available *organisation* of LeetCode problems, and the videos are good at
explaining the intuition behind a pattern rather than just the solution.

Use it as a problem *selector* rather than a curriculum: when you have
identified a weak pattern, its list for that pattern is a better-ordered set
than what you would assemble yourself. Watch a video only after a genuine
attempt, not before.

Do not work through all 150 top to bottom. At your level most of them are
revision you do not need, and the completionist impulse will eat time that phase
2 needs.

## System design

### "System Design Interview" Volumes 1 and 2, Alex Xu — roughly 25 hours

The best match to the format of the interview itself. Each chapter is a case
study worked in the same four-step structure, which is exactly what you need
when you are learning to *drive* the round rather than to be an architect.

Use it after the building-block lessons here, and use it the way
[how to practice](/system-design/12-how-to-practice-do-this-dont-just-read)
describes: attempt the case with a 45-minute timer, out loud, *then* read the
chapter and diff. Reading it first is pleasant and produces the illusion that
you could have done it.

Volume 1 is the higher priority. Volume 2 is broader and less essential.

### "Designing Data-Intensive Applications", Kleppmann — roughly 20 hours, selectively

DDIA is the best book in this space and it is not an interview book. It will
make you genuinely understand replication, partitioning, consistency and
consensus at a depth no interview requires, and it will take you sixty hours to
read properly.

**Read chapters 5, 6, 7 and 9 — replication, partitioning, transactions and
consistency and consensus.** Those four map directly onto the questions
interviewers push on, and they are where your intuition will be weakest coming
from a single-database background. Chapter 1 to 4 are worth skimming; the
stream-processing chapters are excellent and not interview-critical.

Read it during phase 2 in parallel with the case studies, not instead of them.
It deepens answers; it does not teach you to produce one in forty-five minutes.

### ByteByteGo and the system-design-primer repository — as reference, a few hours

ByteByteGo's diagrams are good for revision and the newsletter is a reasonable
way to keep the vocabulary warm. The `system-design-primer` GitHub repository is
a large free compendium, best used for looking things up rather than reading
linearly.

Neither should be a primary. They are both broad and shallow relative to the
case-study work that actually moves the score.

## Java

### "Effective Java", Bloch — roughly 15 hours, selectively

The reference for how experienced Java engineers think, and it reads as a set of
short independent items, which makes it easy to use in twenty-minute chunks. For
interviews, the highest-value chapters are the ones on object methods
(`equals`, `hashCode`, `toString`), generics, enums, and lambdas and streams.

It pairs directly with [OOP and design principles](/java/5-oop-design-principles)
and with the LLD track, because the vocabulary it gives you is what makes design
answers sound senior rather than merely correct.

### "Java Concurrency in Practice", Goetz — roughly 12 hours, chapters 1 to 5 and 10

Old, still the best thing written on the subject, and concurrency is where a
backend candidate most often gets separated from the pack. The first five
chapters cover thread safety, sharing objects, composing objects and the
building blocks, which is essentially the whole interview surface. Chapter 10 on
deadlock is worth adding.

Read it alongside [concurrency](/java/3-concurrency-senior-must-have) and write
the code. Concurrency is not a reading subject — the understanding arrives when
you have written a race condition and watched it happen.

## Mocks — the one most people skip

### Pramp and interviewing.io — roughly 15 to 20 hours

**This is the most under-used resource on the list and it is the one with the
clearest effect on outcomes.** Every other item here improves what you know.
Mocks improve what you can produce while a stranger watches, which is the thing
actually being measured.

**Pramp** is free peer-to-peer. You interview someone and they interview you.
The quality varies with who you draw, and half the value comes from the side
where you are the interviewer — watching someone go silent for four minutes
teaches you more about why silence is penalised than any amount of advice.

**interviewing.io** has professional interviewers, including engineers from the
companies you are targeting, and the feedback is substantially better. It costs
money and it is worth it for two or three sessions, particularly for system
design, where self-assessment is least reliable.

How to use them: start earlier than feels comfortable — week 6 or 7, not week
13. Your first mock will be bad, and it is far better for that to happen in week
6 with a stranger you will never meet again. Do two a week through phase 4, and
alternate round types rather than doing only the one you are best at.

If neither is practical, a colleague or friend with a problem statement and a
timer captures most of the benefit. The essential ingredient is another human
being, not the platform.

## Rough allocation

| Resource | Track | Hours | When |
|---|---|---|---|
| LeetCode | DSA | 60–80 | Throughout, as secondary |
| Neetcode lists | DSA | 10 | Phase 0–1, for pattern selection |
| Alex Xu Vol 1 | System design | 20 | Phase 2, after each attempt |
| DDIA (ch. 5–7, 9) | System design | 20 | Phase 2, in parallel |
| Effective Java | Java / LLD | 15 | Phase 1 and 3 |
| Java Concurrency in Practice | Java | 12 | Phase 1 |
| Pramp / interviewing.io | All | 15–20 | Week 6 onwards |
| ByteByteGo, primer repo | System design | 5 | Reference only |

That is roughly 160 hours of external work against the 250 to 300 in
[the plan](/roadmap/3-the-integrated-plan-everything-sequenced-your-chart) —
the rest being the lessons here and, more importantly, the practice attempts
that no resource can do for you.

## What to skip

- **Paid interview-prep courses that repackage the same material.** You have the
  map already; what you need is repetition, and repetition is free.
- **Certifications.** They do not affect hiring at these companies.
- **Building a second portfolio project.** Going deeper on the URL shortener —
  load testing it, fixing its real bugs, writing up the tradeoffs — produces far
  more interview material per hour than starting something new, because depth is
  what gets interrogated and breadth is not.
- **Reading a fourth system design resource.** If you have Alex Xu, DDIA and the
  case studies here and you still cannot drive a design round, the missing
  ingredient is timed spoken attempts, and no book supplies those.
