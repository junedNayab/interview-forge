## The problem this solves

You can spend three months on this material, finish every lesson, feel
substantially more knowledgeable, and perform exactly as you would have on day
one. This is not a rare outcome; it is the default one, and it happens because
reading produces a feeling of understanding that is almost indistinguishable
from the real thing and costs a tenth as much.

The gap is between **recognition** and **retrieval**. Reading a lesson on
consistent hashing makes the idea recognisable — you will nod along when you see
it again. It does nothing for your ability to produce it, unprompted, while
someone is watching and your hands are shaking. Interviews test retrieval only.

So the tracking that matters is not "did I finish the lesson". It is "can I
produce this".

## The four tests

These are the only four you need. Each one is cheap, each one is unforgiving,
and each one maps onto a different failure mode.

### 1. The out-loud test

**Close everything and explain the topic aloud, from memory, for two minutes, as
if to a colleague.**

This is the single best tool here, and it is brutal in a way that re-reading
never is. You will get thirty seconds in and discover that you know the shape of
the idea and none of the mechanism. That discovery is the point.

Two rules that make it work: no notes visible, and out loud rather than in your
head. Silent explanation is much more forgiving than spoken explanation, because
your internal monologue skips the parts you cannot articulate without noticing.
Recording yourself on your phone makes it harder to cheat and lets you hear the
hesitations.

Apply it after every lesson, and again a week later. **A topic is not learned
until it survives the week-later version.**

### 2. The timed attempt

**Whatever the skill is, do it once under a real timer before you look at any
answer.**

A coding problem in twenty-five minutes. A design case in forty-five, standing,
talking. A machine coding problem in ninety, with code that runs. The timer is
not there to add pressure for its own sake — it is there because unlimited time
lets you avoid the specific skill being tested, which is making decisions
quickly enough to finish.

The critical ordering is attempt first, read the answer second. Reading a worked
case study and then feeling able to do it is the most common self-deception in
system design preparation. Reading it after a failed attempt is genuinely
valuable, because you have something to compare against.

### 3. The query-count check

**For anything in the backend track, verify behaviour rather than asserting it.**

The name comes from the most useful instance of it: turn on SQL logging, make
one request, and count the queries. Not the cache hit rate, not the latency —
the actual number of statements your application sent to the database. This is
how you find that a cache-aside read still queries Postgres on every hit because
something downstream needs the entity, which is exactly the bug in the URL
shortener discussed in
[Story 2](/behavioral/story-2-describe-a-time-you-improved-performance).

Generalised: for every backend claim you intend to make in an interview, find
the observation that would prove it. What does `EXPLAIN` actually say about the
index you think is being used? Does the lazy association load where you think it
does? Does the `@Transactional` you added actually apply, given that you called
it from within the same class?

**The reason this is a progress test and not just good practice is that it
distinguishes people who know the framework's documentation from people who know
the framework's behaviour,** which is precisely what the backend deep-dive round
is designed to separate.

### 4. The week-later design review

**Write a design, put it away, and grade it seven days later.**

Immediately after producing a design you cannot see its holes — you are still
inside the reasoning that produced it. A week later you have lost that context
and you read it roughly the way an interviewer does. Missing capacity numbers,
components with no stated justification, an unaddressed single point of failure
and a data model that does not support the access patterns all become obvious.

Use [how to self-grade your practice](/system-design/how-to-self-grade-your-practice)
as the rubric. Keep the graded versions; the trend across six of them is the
clearest evidence you will get that phase 2 is working.

## What to track

Keep it small. A spreadsheet with a row per week and a handful of columns beats
an elaborate system you abandon in week three.

```
Week | Phase | Sessions with output | Out-loud tests passed | Timed attempts | Notes
```

**"Sessions with output"** is the one to take seriously: sessions in which you
produced something — spoke an answer, wrote code, drew a design, solved a
problem under time. Reading sessions do not count. Four or five a week is a
healthy rate.

Do not track hours. Hours are easy to accumulate, easy to inflate, and
uncorrelated with the thing you are trying to change.

Also keep a running **"cannot answer" list**. Every time the out-loud test fails,
every time a mock exposes a gap, every time an interviewer asks something you
fumble, it goes on the list. That list is your actual curriculum from phase 3
onwards, and it is far better targeted than any plan written in advance.

## Signals per track

Concrete things to check, roughly in order of increasing difficulty. These are
the ones worth testing, not an exhaustive list.

### DSA

- You can name the pattern within three minutes of reading a problem you have
  not seen.
- You can write the template for all fifteen patterns from memory. Check against
  [the pattern lessons](/dsa).
- You state complexity without hesitating, using
  [the cheat sheet](/dsa/complexity-cheat-sheet-memorize-this) knowledge from
  memory.
- You solve a medium in twenty-five minutes while narrating, without going
  silent for more than twenty seconds at a stretch.
- **The real signal:** your failed attempts fail because the problem was hard,
  not because you spent fifteen minutes not knowing where to start.

### Java and SQL

- Two-minute out-loud answers on HashMap internals, `volatile` and happens-before,
  the persistence context lifecycle, and the three fixes for
  [N+1](/java/3-the-n-1-problem-most-asked-jpa-interview-question).
- You can read a piece of Spring code and say whether the `@Transactional` on it
  actually takes effect, and why.
- You can read an `EXPLAIN` output and say which index was used and why.
- **The real signal:** on your own project, you can explain every non-obvious
  decision *and* name what is wrong with it. Four issues, with the fix and the
  tradeoff for each.

### System design

- You drive forty-five minutes without being prompted, hitting requirements,
  estimation, API, high-level, deep dive and bottlenecks.
- Estimates take under five minutes and you state a conclusion from each — "that
  is 40 writes a second, so one Postgres instance, so no sharding."
- On any component you reach for, you can go one level below the name: for a
  cache, the eviction policy, the TTL strategy, stampede protection and hot keys.
- You name your own single points of failure before the interviewer does.
- **The real signal:** your second attempt at a case study, a week after the
  first, is visibly better on the self-grade rubric. If it is not, you are
  reading rather than practising.

### LLD

- Problem statement to a class diagram with clear responsibilities in fifteen
  minutes.
- You can name the SOLID principle a piece of code violates and show the
  refactor.
- Eight to ten design patterns you can implement, not twenty-three you can name.
- **The real signal:** working, compiling code at the end of ninety minutes,
  three times in a row.

### Behavioral

- Six to eight stories, four minutes each, delivered from bullets rather than a
  script.
- Each survives the eight follow-ups listed in its lesson.
- Played back on a recording, your Situation and Task is around twenty percent
  of the runtime and your Action around sixty.
- **The real signal:** at least two stories where you were wrong, and you can
  tell them without either defensiveness or excessive apology.

## The honest check, every fourth week

Once a month, do a short audit rather than a study session:

1. Pick three topics from a month ago. Out-loud test each. How many survive?
2. Look at your "cannot answer" list. Has it shrunk, or only grown?
3. Count sessions with output for the last four weeks. Is the rate holding?
4. Attempt one thing cold from the phase you finished. Does it still work?

If the answers are bad, **the response is to change the method rather than to add
hours**. Almost always the diagnosis is the same: too much reading, not enough
producing. The fix is to convert half of next month's sessions into attempts,
even though attempts are uncomfortable and reading is not.

That discomfort is, unhelpfully, the best available signal that a session was
worth doing.
