## Two hours a day beats eight on Saturday

This is the most important operational fact in the plan, and it is not a
motivational claim — it follows from what you are actually trying to build.

Interview skill is retrieval under pressure. You are not accumulating knowledge
so much as making it reachable quickly while someone watches. Retrieval strength
is built by spacing, not by duration: the same eight hours spread across four
days produces substantially more durable recall than eight hours in one sitting,
because each session forces you to retrieve what the previous one left behind.

There is a second effect, which is that a Saturday-only schedule has no
resilience. One busy weekend and you have lost a week. A daily schedule that you
miss twice still delivers five sessions.

The corollary: **a thirty-minute session you actually do is worth more than a
three-hour session you keep deferring.** On days when the full block is not
happening, do the reduced version rather than nothing. The point is the streak,
not the hours.

## The shape of a weekday

Roughly two and a half hours, split into two unequal blocks. The split matters —
the primary block is deep work on one track and should not be interrupted or
subdivided.

```
Morning, before work        45 min   SECONDARY — DSA or light review
Evening, after dinner       90 min   PRIMARY — the current phase's track
Before sleep                10 min   Recall pass, no new material
```

**The morning block** is short and mechanical, which is what makes it survivable
at that hour. One timed medium problem, narrated out loud. Or, if it is a
maintenance week, fifteen minutes on
[the rapid-fire self-test](/cs-fundamentals/5-rapid-fire-self-test-answer-out-loud)
and twenty minutes reviewing yesterday's notes.

Putting the secondary track in the morning is deliberate. If it goes in the
evening it gets eaten by the primary block, and DSA in particular expands to
fill any available time because it is the most comfortable thing on the list.

**The evening block** is the phase's primary track — Java internals in phase 1,
system design in phase 2, LLD in phase 3. Ninety minutes, one topic, no tab
switching. If you are in phase 2, roughly half of these sessions should involve
talking out loud at a whiteboard or a sheet of paper rather than reading.

**The ten minutes before sleep** is a recall pass and it is much more valuable
than its length suggests. Close everything, and say out loud what you learned
today. No notes. What you cannot reproduce goes on tomorrow's list. This single
habit does more for retention than any amount of re-reading.

If two and a half hours is not available, the priority order is: evening block,
then morning block, then the recall pass. Ninety minutes of primary work is a
complete day.

## The shape of a week

| Day | Primary (90 min) | Secondary (45 min) |
|---|---|---|
| Monday | Current phase — new material | 1 timed DSA medium |
| Tuesday | Current phase — new material | 1 timed DSA medium |
| Wednesday | Current phase — **practice, not reading** | CS fundamentals rapid-fire |
| Thursday | Current phase — new material | 1 timed DSA medium |
| Friday | **Behavioral** — draft or rehearse one story | Light review, or off |
| Saturday | **Long session** — 2 to 3 hours, one full mock or case study | Review the week |
| Sunday | Off, or one light session | Update the tracker, plan the week |

Four observations about this shape:

**Wednesday is a practice day by design.** Mid-week is when the temptation to
read rather than do is strongest, because reading feels like progress and costs
nothing. Making one weekday explicitly a doing day — a case study attempted
before reading the answer, a pattern implemented from scratch, a design spoken
out loud — is what keeps the phase honest.

**Friday is behavioral.** It is the lightest cognitive load of the week, it fits
at the end of a work week better than concurrency does, and one story a week for
six weeks gives you a complete story bank without ever having dedicated a phase
to it.

**Saturday is the long session, not the whole plan.** Two to three hours, and
the right use of it is the thing that does not fit on a weekday: a full
forty-five-minute design case with a timer, a ninety-minute machine coding
problem, or a mock interview with another person. Do not use it to catch up on
reading.

**Sunday is off, or nearly.** Take it. Fifteen weeks is long enough that a
seven-day schedule will break somewhere around week five, and it will break by
collapsing rather than by tapering. Spend thirty minutes updating
[the tracker](/roadmap/5-progress-tracking) and deciding what the coming week's
primary track is, then stop.

## When work eats the week

It will, several times. Two rules that keep this from turning into an abandoned
plan:

**Protect the primary block, sacrifice the secondary.** If you have forty-five
minutes, spend them on the phase's primary track, not on a DSA problem. The
secondary track is maintenance and it tolerates gaps; the primary track is where
the actual movement is.

**Move the phase boundary, do not restart.** If you lose a week, phase 2 now ends
in week 10 instead of week 9. Nothing is wrong. The instinct to start the plan
over from the beginning because it "got messy" is the single most common way
these plans die.

## Low-energy days

Some evenings you will sit down and have nothing. This is normal, particularly
in weeks four through eight of a fifteen-week stretch, and the correct response
is not to force a deep session — you will get forty minutes of low-quality work
and reinforce the feeling that studying is unpleasant.

Do one of these instead. All of them are genuinely useful, and none require much:

- **Rapid-fire self-test.** Fifteen minutes of
  [answering CS fundamentals out loud](/cs-fundamentals/5-rapid-fire-self-test-answer-out-loud).
  Low effort, high value, and it is the kind of thing that otherwise never gets
  done.
- **Re-solve a problem you have already solved.** Not a new one. The point is
  fluency, and the low cognitive load is exactly right for a tired evening.
- **Read one case study you have already attempted** and diff it against what
  you produced. Passive, and still useful because you have the contrast.
- **Rehearse a behavioral story out loud.** Four minutes, from bullets. You can
  do this while walking, which is sometimes the only way it happens.
- **Skim a quick reference.** [What seniors say in interviews](/java/quick-reference-what-seniors-say-in-interviews)
  or [the complexity cheat sheet](/dsa/complexity-cheat-sheet-memorize-this).
  Pure review, zero construction.
- **Clean up your project or your notes.** Fix one thing in the URL shortener,
  or write up one of the issues you found as a behavioral story outline.
  Mechanical work that produces a real artifact.

**Twenty minutes of one of these beats a skipped day, and it beats a forced deep
session.** What it must not become is the default — if three consecutive days are
low-energy days, the problem is sleep or the schedule, not motivation, and the
fix is to shorten the plan rather than to push through it.

## The thing to measure

Not hours. Hours are easy to accumulate and tell you very little — an hour of
reading system design lessons and an hour of attempting a design out loud both
count as an hour, and only one of them is preparation.

Count sessions where you produced something: a spoken answer, written code, a
drawn design, a solved problem under time. Aim for four or five such sessions a
week. [Progress tracking](/roadmap/5-progress-tracking) covers how to tell
whether they are working.
