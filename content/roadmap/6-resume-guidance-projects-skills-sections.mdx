## The rule that governs everything else

**Everything on your resume is interview surface area.** A line that says
"Kafka" buys you a fifteen-minute conversation about consumer groups and
partition rebalancing. A line that says "optimised database performance" buys
you "which query, and how did you measure it?"

This cuts both ways, and the useful direction is the one people ignore: you can
*choose* what you get asked about. A resume is partly a list of the topics you
would like to spend the interview on. If you write three lines about caching and
one about Kubernetes, you will mostly be asked about caching. That is a lever,
and it is worth using deliberately.

The corollary is the hard rule: **do not list anything you cannot defend for ten
minutes.** Not "have heard of". Not "used once in a tutorial". Ten minutes of
someone competent asking why. The cost of being caught is much higher than the
benefit of the keyword, because it converts a knowledge gap into a credibility
problem, and credibility problems contaminate everything else you said.

## Bullets: action, technology, outcome

Every bullet should have three parts. Most bad bullets are missing the third,
and some are missing the first two as well.

```
[Action verb] + [what, with which technology] + [measurable outcome]
```

Compare:

> **Weak:** "Worked on improving API performance using Redis caching."
>
> **Strong:** "Cut p99 latency on the redirect endpoint from 180 ms to under
> 15 ms by caching the URL record and moving click tracking off the request
> path, raising sustained throughput roughly tenfold."

The second version names the specific endpoint, the specific change, the
mechanism, and two numbers measured the same way. It is also a complete
behavioral answer in compressed form, which is not a coincidence — the bullets
on your resume and your
[story bank](/behavioral/general-behavioral-tips) should be the same material.

A few mechanics that matter more than they should:

**Lead with the verb, in past tense, and vary it.** Built, designed, cut,
migrated, instrumented, diagnosed, automated. Avoid "responsible for" and
"worked on", which describe a position rather than an action.

**Numbers, even approximate ones.** Latency, throughput, query count, data
volume, users, cost, time saved, defects prevented. If you genuinely have no
metrics, use a scale or scope number instead — "across three services" or "for a
dataset of 40 million rows" — which is at least concrete. An unquantified bullet
is not fatal; a resume where *no* bullet is quantified reads as someone who has
never measured anything.

**One line each, two at most.** Three-line bullets are not read.

**Put the interesting one first.** Within each role or project, the first bullet
gets a disproportionate share of the attention. Do not lead with "developed REST
APIs using Spring Boot", which describes several million people.

## Presenting the url-shortener

A URL shortener is, on its face, an unimpressive project — it is a common
tutorial, and a screener has seen dozens. That is fine, because **what makes a
project section work is not the novelty of the product, it is the visible
engineering judgement.** A URL shortener with real caching, real rate limiting
and an honest account of its failure modes reads far better than an ambitious
system nobody could defend.

You have two advantages here worth exploiting. First, it is genuinely the
canonical system design interview question, so the work you have already done
maps directly onto
[Case 1](/system-design/case-1-design-a-url-shortener-your-project-master-this-one).
Second, you know what is wrong with it, which is a much rarer quality in a
candidate than having built it.

A defensible version:

> **URL Shortener Service** — Java 17, Spring Boot, PostgreSQL, Redis, Docker
> *(github.com/your-handle/url-shortener)*
>
> - Built a redirect service with Base62 short-code generation and cache-aside
>   reads through Redis, handling around N requests per second in load testing
>   at a p99 of M milliseconds.
> - Diagnosed a cache-hit path that still queried Postgres on every request
>   because click tracking loaded the entity for a foreign key; restructured the
>   cached value and moved click recording off the hot path, cutting p99 from
>   180 ms to under 15 ms.
> - Found and fixed a correctness bug where link expiry was only evaluated on
>   the database fallback path, so cached entries kept redirecting for up to
>   24 hours past expiry; bounded the cache TTL by the remaining link lifetime.
> - Implemented Redis-backed per-IP rate limiting with `INCR` and `EXPIRE`,
>   failing open with an alert on limiter degradation rather than rejecting
>   traffic on a Redis outage.
> - Secured endpoints with stateless JWT authentication; externalised all
>   credentials and signing keys to environment configuration.

Substitute your real numbers, and only include the bullets that describe work
you actually did. Note the shape of the middle three: each one names a problem,
a diagnosis and an outcome, which is what makes them read as engineering rather
than as a feature list. Note also that the last one is the least interesting and
therefore last.

<Callout type="pitfall" title="Before you link the repository">
Someone will open it. The `application.yml` in that project currently contains a
hardcoded JWT signing secret and hardcoded database credentials, and a committed
secret is the first thing a senior reviewer notices — it turns a good project
into a conversation about your security judgement.

Move both to environment variables or a secrets manager, make the application
fail at startup when they are missing rather than falling back to a default,
rotate the committed values, and add a `.env.example` documenting what is
required. It is an hour of work and it removes a guaranteed negative.
</Callout>

**Include a README that states the tradeoffs.** A short section headed "known
limitations and what I would change" is unusual enough to be memorable, and it
pre-empts the interviewer's best questions by answering them first. It is also
the single clearest signal of seniority you can put in a repository.

## The skills section

Its main job is getting through keyword filters, so it should be scannable and
grouped. Its secondary job is telling the interviewer which questions to ask,
which means it needs to be honest about depth.

```
Languages       Java 17, SQL, Python (working knowledge)
Frameworks      Spring Boot, Spring Data JPA, Hibernate, JUnit
Data            PostgreSQL, Redis
Tools           Docker, Git, Maven, Postman
Concepts        REST API design, caching strategies, JWT authentication,
                concurrency, query optimisation
```

Rules:

**Group by category.** A single 40-item comma-separated list is unreadable and
signals nothing except that you have used a computer.

**Mark honest depth where it is uneven.** "Python (working knowledge)" is a
useful qualifier that costs you almost nothing and protects you from a Python
interview. What you must not do is list something at full strength that you
would fail a basic question on.

**Cut anything you would not choose to be asked about.** If you put Kubernetes
on the list because it appeared in a job description, you have volunteered for a
Kubernetes conversation you will lose. Fewer, defensible entries outperform a
long list every time.

**No proficiency bars or star ratings.** They are self-assessed, nobody believes
them, and "4 out of 5 stars in Java" is a claim you will be asked to justify.

**Include the concepts row.** It is the one most people omit, and it is the one
that signals you think in terms of problems rather than tools.

## Prepare the defence, not just the document

When the resume is finished, do this before you send it. For every bullet, write
down the three follow-up questions you would ask if you were interviewing
yourself, and answer them in writing.

For the latency bullet above: how did you measure it, what was the p50, why did
you choose to move click tracking asynchronous rather than just caching the ID,
what did that cost you in durability, and what was the bottleneck afterwards.
If any of those has no answer, either get the answer or delete the bullet.

**This exercise is where most of the value of this lesson is.** A resume you can
defend line by line changes how the deep-dive round goes, because you stop
hoping they skip a topic and start hoping they ask about it.

## Format and length

One page if you have under about eight years of experience. Two only if the
second page earns itself.

Plain single-column layout, standard fonts, no photo, no graphics, no columns —
resume parsers handle multi-column layouts badly and silently, and you will not
know it happened. Send PDF unless asked otherwise. Name the file with your name
in it.

Put the most relevant thing highest. For someone moving into a product company
on the strength of a project, that ordering is usually: a two-line summary,
skills, experience, projects — unless the project is genuinely stronger evidence
than the job, in which case move it above experience and accept that this is a
slightly unconventional choice.

Drop the objective statement, the photograph, the date of birth, the marital
status and the full postal address. A city, an email, a phone number, a GitHub
link and a LinkedIn link is the complete header.
