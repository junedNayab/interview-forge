## Why the process is worth understanding

Because the rounds are scored independently, and you cannot compensate. A
brilliant coding round does not rescue a weak design round — they go into
separate boxes on separate scorecards and get read by people who were not in the
other room. Understanding the shape of the funnel tells you where your effort
actually converts into offers, which is rarely where it feels most productive.

The other reason: much of what feels arbitrary about these loops is not. A
"no signal" outcome is a rejection, interviewers are writing evidence rather than
opinions, and the final decision is often made by people who never met you. Once
you know that, several otherwise puzzling pieces of advice — write down every
question you were asked, think out loud, always have questions — become obviously
correct.

## The pipeline

### 1. Resume screen

Usually a recruiter with a few hundred applications, spending well under a minute
each, plus an automated keyword filter in many cases. What survives: relevant
technology keywords in the right places, recognisable companies or products,
quantified outcomes, and no unexplained gaps.

This is the highest-leverage and least-worked-on stage. Nothing else in this
roadmap matters if you do not get past it, and it is the cheapest thing to fix.
See [resume guidance](/roadmap/6-resume-guidance-projects-skills-sections).

### 2. Recruiter screen — 20 to 30 minutes

Not technical. The recruiter is checking that you exist as described, that you
are interested, that your compensation expectations are plausible, and that you
can hold a conversation.

Prepare three things: a two-minute summary of your background, a clean answer to
"why are you looking?", and a compensation range. Discuss the range as what you
are seeing in the market rather than as a number you will accept. Rejections
here are usually about level mismatch or location rather than ability.

You should also *ask* things here: how many rounds, what each round is, and what
level the role is mapped to. Recruiters answer this readily and it determines
what you prepare.

### 3. Online assessment — 60 to 120 minutes

Common at larger companies and at volume-hiring times. Typically two to four
algorithmic problems on HackerRank, CodeSignal or an internal platform, sometimes
with a debugging section or a short work-styles questionnaire.

Scored almost entirely on **test cases passed**. Nobody reads your code unless
you are borderline. This is the one stage where elegance is worthless and
correctness under time pressure is everything — which makes it the stage that
rewards raw problem volume more than any other. Some companies also record how
long you took and whether you left the tab.

### 4. Technical phone screen — 45 to 60 minutes

One or two algorithmic problems with a live interviewer, in a shared editor,
usually without execution. Roughly medium difficulty.

Scored on: did you reach a working solution, did you reach the optimal complexity,
did you communicate while doing it, and did you handle the edge cases. The
communication component is real — a correct silent solution frequently scores
below a correct narrated one, because the interviewer has to write evidence and
silence produces none.

### 5. The onsite loop — 4 to 6 rounds

Usually one day, sometimes split across two. A typical backend loop at mid to
senior level:

| Round | Length | What is actually scored |
|---|---|---|
| **DSA 1** | 45 min | Pattern recognition, optimal complexity, edge cases, clean code |
| **DSA 2** | 45 min | Same, usually harder or a different pattern family |
| **System design (HLD)** | 45–60 min | Scoping, estimation, tradeoff reasoning, depth on one component, self-critique |
| **LLD or machine coding** | 60–120 min | Class modelling, SOLID, extensibility, working code under time |
| **Behavioral / hiring manager** | 45–60 min | Ownership, conflict, failure, collaboration, motivation |
| **Backend deep dive** *(varies)* | 45 min | Java, concurrency, JPA, SQL, and your own project under interrogation |

Not every company runs all six. Indian product companies and startups weight
machine coding heavily; the large global companies weight HLD and behavioral
more. Ask the recruiter which you are getting.

Two things worth knowing about the loop. First, **the deep-dive and behavioral
rounds are where your resume gets tested** — the project you listed is fair game
for forty-five minutes. Second, at least one interviewer is usually assigned the
behavioral signal explicitly, which means it is not a chat, whatever it feels
like.

### 6. Hiring manager round

Sometimes merged with behavioral. This person is deciding whether they want you
on their team specifically, and they are the one who has to justify the hire. The
content is behavioral plus role fit plus, often, a genuine conversation about
what you would work on.

This is the round where your questions matter most, because you are being
assessed on judgement rather than knowledge, and the questions you ask are
evidence of judgement. See
[general behavioral tips](/behavioral/general-behavioral-tips) for four that do
real work.

### 7. Hiring committee or bar raiser

At the larger companies, a group that did not interview you reads the written
feedback and decides. This matters more than most candidates realise, because it
means **your interviewers' notes are the entire input.** The committee cannot see
that you were charming or that the problem was unusually hard. They see written
evidence against a rubric.

Practical consequences: anything you did not say out loud did not happen; a round
with no strong negative but no specific evidence is usually recorded as "no
signal", which functions as a soft no; and consistency across rounds is checked —
if you described yourself as owning something in one round and as a contributor
in another, someone will notice.

## How levels change the weighting

Most companies map to something like L3/SDE-1 (entry), L4/SDE-2 (mid, roughly
two to five years), L5/senior (roughly five and up, though the band is wide).
The transition you are likely targeting is mid to senior, and the weighting
shifts sharply across it:

- **At entry and mid level**, DSA dominates. A strong coding performance can
  carry a mediocre design round. Design questions are often scoped down or
  replaced with LLD.
- **At senior level, system design and behavioral start to dominate.** The coding
  bar does not go away but it stops being the differentiator, because everyone
  who reaches the loop clears it. What decides the outcome is whether you can
  drive an ambiguous design problem and whether the behavioral evidence shows
  ownership.

This is why "I'm strong at DSA" is a weaker position than it feels at senior
level. The returns on your five-hundred-and-first LeetCode problem are close to
zero; the returns on your first ten system design practice runs are enormous.

Down-levelling is common and worth being prepared for: a candidate interviewed
for senior who performs well on coding and weakly on design is frequently offered
the level below rather than rejected. If that happens, it is almost always the
design round that did it.

## Realistic timelines

From first contact to offer, assuming nothing goes wrong:

- Recruiter screen to online assessment or phone screen: one to two weeks.
- Phone screen to onsite: one to three weeks, mostly scheduling.
- Onsite to decision: three days to two weeks; longer where a committee meets on
  a fixed cadence.
- Offer to negotiation closed: one to two weeks.

**Six to ten weeks end to end is normal.** Twelve is not unusual. Plan for this:
start applying while you are still preparing rather than after, because the
pipeline delay means your first loop will happen four to six weeks after you
begin, which is roughly when you want it to.

Run loops in parallel rather than sequentially, for two reasons: competing offers
are the only real leverage in compensation negotiation, and the first loop is
always the worst one. If there is a company you badly want, do not interview
there first.

Most companies have a cooling-off period after a rejection — typically six months
to a year — so an early attempt at your top choice is expensive.

## How referrals change the funnel

A referral does one thing well: it gets your resume read by a human instead of
filtered. That is a large effect at the top of the funnel and roughly zero effect
after it. Referred candidates are not graded more leniently, and at companies
with a hiring committee the referral is often not visible at the decision stage.

Getting one is more tractable than it seems. Former colleagues are the obvious
source. Beyond that, a short, specific message naming the team or role, with
your resume attached and an easy way to say no, has a much better hit rate than
a generic request — and most referral programmes pay a bonus, so you are asking
for something the other person is mildly incentivised to give.

What a referral cannot do is compensate for a resume that does not support the
level. If you get referred and screened out anyway, the problem is the resume,
not the referral.

## What this means for how you prepare

Three conclusions follow from the above, and they drive
[the four tracks](/roadmap/2-the-four-tracks) and
[the plan](/roadmap/3-the-integrated-plan-everything-sequenced-your-chart):

1. **Your weakest track sets your ceiling,** because rounds are scored
   independently and a weak round is rarely rescued.
2. **At senior level, design and behavioral carry more weight than additional
   DSA volume,** which is the opposite of how most people allocate their time.
3. **Everything on your resume is interview surface area.** The project you list
   will be interrogated for forty-five minutes by someone who is good at this.
