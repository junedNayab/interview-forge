## What this is

This is a study site for one specific transition: a Java backend engineer with
solid fundamentals moving from a services environment to a product company. It
is not a general programming course. Every lesson is written against what
actually gets scored in an interview loop, which is a narrower and stranger thing
than being good at the job.

There are about a hundred lessons across seven sections. The depth varies
deliberately: the [system design](/system-design) and [LLD](/lld) tracks are long
and detailed because that is where most people in this situation are weakest,
while [SQL](/sql) is a tighter refresher because most people in this situation
already know it.

## The four tracks

Interview loops at these companies test four separable things, and you are
scored on each one independently. Being excellent at three and weak at one is
how strong engineers get rejected.

1. **DSA and coding.** Pattern recognition under time pressure.
   → [DSA and coding patterns](/dsa)
2. **Backend depth — Java and SQL.** Language semantics, concurrency, JPA and
   query behaviour, all of which get probed against your own project.
   → [Java](/java) and [SQL](/sql)
3. **System design, both high and low level.** Distributed systems design in one
   round, object-oriented design or machine coding in another. These are
   different skills with different rubrics.
   → [System design](/system-design) and [LLD](/lld)
4. **Breadth and behavioral.** Networking, operating systems, distributed systems
   concepts and security, plus the round where you talk about yourself.
   → [CS fundamentals](/cs-fundamentals) and [Behavioral](/behavioral)

[The four tracks](/roadmap/2-the-four-tracks) goes through each one properly:
what it is assessed on, how much of the outcome it decides, and the exact
lessons to do in order.

## How deep the material goes

Worth knowing before you plan your time, because it determines how long this
takes.

The system design track runs from a 45-minute interview framework through
estimation, caching, databases, consistency, queues, rate limiting and
consistent hashing, and then five full case studies worked end to end — starting
with [a URL shortener](/system-design/case-1-design-a-url-shortener-your-project-master-this-one),
which is the project you already built.

The LLD track covers OOP, SOLID and the design-pattern vocabulary, then a full
worked parking lot and a set of practice problems, ending in
[machine coding survival tips](/lld/part-8-machine-coding-round-survival-tips).

The Java track goes well past syntax into
[JVM internals](/java/1-jvm-internals),
[concurrency](/java/3-concurrency-senior-must-have),
[the N+1 problem](/java/3-the-n-1-problem-most-asked-jpa-interview-question) and
[Spring's proxy behaviour](/java/8-spring-internals-ioc-beans-and-aop-proxies) —
the things interviewers use to separate people who have used a framework from
people who understand it.

The DSA track is fifteen patterns rather than a problem list, on the theory that
you have already solved a lot of problems and what you need is retrieval, not
volume.

## How to use it

**Do not read it front to back.** It is a reference organised by topic, and
reading it linearly is the most reliable way to spend three months feeling
productive and improving at nothing. The roadmap exists to give you a path
through it.

Three rules that matter more than the specific plan:

**One primary track at a time.** Each week has one thing you go deep on.
Everything else is thirty to forty-five minutes of maintenance. Studying four
tracks in parallel produces four shallow tracks.

**Output, not input.** A lesson is done when you can explain it out loud without
looking, or have written the code, or have drawn the design. Reading it does not
count. This is the single most common way preparation fails, and
[progress tracking](/roadmap/5-progress-tracking) is about making it visible.

**Protect the weakest track.** For most people making this move, that is system
design, and it is also the track that feels least urgent because nothing breaks
when you skip it. [The integrated plan](/roadmap/3-the-integrated-plan-everything-sequenced-your-chart)
weights it accordingly.

## Read the roadmap in this order

1. **[How product-company hiring actually works](/roadmap/1-how-product-company-hiring-actually-works)** —
   the pipeline, what each round scores, and how levels change the weighting.
   Read this first; it explains why the tracks are weighted the way they are.
2. **[The four tracks](/roadmap/2-the-four-tracks)** — what to train and where
   you currently stand.
3. **[The integrated plan](/roadmap/3-the-integrated-plan-everything-sequenced-your-chart)** —
   the week-by-week sequence.
4. **[Your weekly rhythm](/roadmap/4-your-weekly-rhythm-the-shape-of-a-day)** —
   what a realistic day looks like alongside a full-time job.
5. **[Progress tracking](/roadmap/5-progress-tracking)** — how to tell whether
   any of this is working.
6. **[Resume guidance](/roadmap/6-resume-guidance-projects-skills-sections)** —
   do this early, because the resume is what gets you the loop at all.
7. **[Where to actually solve problems](/roadmap/7-where-to-actually-solve-problems)** —
   the external resources and how much time each deserves.
8. **[Start here, today](/roadmap/8-start-here-today)** — five concrete things to
   do in the next two hours.

That last page is the one to open when you finish reading this. Everything above
is orientation; that page is the first day.

## A note on honesty

This material is blunt in places, because encouragement is not useful here.
Where the advice is uncertain or a matter of taste, it says so. Where something
is a hard constraint — like the fact that you cannot fake a project story under
follow-up questions — it says that too.

The realistic shape of this transition, for someone with a full-time job and a
genuine system design gap, is three to four months at around fifteen to twenty
hours a week. It can be done faster with more hours and it is routinely done
slower. What does not work is three weeks.
