## The premise

You cannot read your way into passing an LLD round. This is the single most
important thing to accept before you plan your time.

> **Reading about the Strategy pattern teaches you its shape. Implementing a
> parking lot, then being told "now add electric-vehicle charging spots",
> teaches you why the shape exists** — because you either touch one new file or
> you touch six existing ones, and the difference is physical rather than
> theoretical.

So the plan below is weighted roughly 70% typing, 30% reading. If you find
yourself three days into a week with no code written, the week has already
failed regardless of how much you understood.

**The honest time budget:** eight to ten hours a week, which is about ninety
minutes on weekdays plus one longer weekend session. Less than six hours a week
and four weeks will not be enough — extend to six or eight weeks rather than
compressing, because the bottleneck is repetitions, not information.

### The exercise that does most of the work

Every week ends with the same drill, and it is the highest-value thing in this
entire track:

1. Implement a problem end to end, with a runnable `main`.
2. Close the file. Write down a new requirement on paper, without looking at
   your design.
3. Implement it. **Count the files you touched.**

One or two files means your abstractions were on the right axes. Five means they
were on the wrong ones, and now you know exactly which guess was wrong and why.
No amount of reading produces that feedback, and it happens to be the precise
question the interviewer will ask you — so you are rehearsing the answer with
real evidence behind it.

## Week 1 — Foundations, and your first refactor

**Read:** [Part 1 — the OOP pillars](/lld/part-1-the-foundation-oop-the-4-pillars-applied)
and [Part 2 — SOLID](/lld/part-2-solid-principles-the-heart-of-every-lld-interview).
Read Part 2 twice; it is the load-bearing lesson and one pass will not stick.

**Build, in this order:**

- **A `BankAccount` with an enforced invariant.** Twenty minutes. Private
  balance, validation in the constructor, a `withdraw` that rejects overdrafts,
  and a test that proves you cannot reach a negative balance from outside.
  Trivial, and it makes encapsulation concrete rather than verbal.
- **Refactor a `switch` into polymorphism.** Write a deliberately bad
  `ShippingCalculator` with an `if`-chain over three shipping methods, get it
  working, then refactor it to an interface with three implementations. Do both
  halves — the point is feeling the difference, and you only feel it if you
  wrote the bad one first.
- **Find a real SRP violation in your own code.** Open a project you have
  actually shipped and find one class that changes for two different reasons.
  Write down who owns each reason. This gives you an honest answer to "give me
  an example from your own work", which is asked constantly and which nobody
  prepares.

**Readiness check:** can you explain all five SOLID principles with a
before-and-after example each, out loud, in under six minutes, including one
place where you would deliberately *not* apply one? If the last clause is hard,
re-read the closing section of Part 2 — it is the part that signals judgement.

## Week 2 — Patterns, one tiny program each

**Read:** [Part 3 — design patterns](/lld/part-3-design-patterns-the-lld-vocabulary).

**Build a minimal working demo of seven patterns.** Twenty to thirty minutes
each, one file each, with a `main` that prints something. Do not build elaborate
examples; the goal is muscle memory for the shape.

| Pattern | The demo to write |
|---|---|
| Strategy | Three pricing rules behind one interface, swapped at runtime |
| Factory | An enum-to-class mapping producing three `Vehicle` subtypes |
| Builder | A `Pizza` with four optional toppings and validation in `build()` |
| Singleton | The holder idiom and the enum form, plus a note on why you would rather inject |
| Observer | A publisher with two listeners, one of which throws |
| State | A three-state turnstile: locked, unlocked, and a coin that does nothing when already unlocked |
| Decorator | Coffee with milk and sugar, and a second version wrapping `java.io` streams |

The Observer demo has a deliberate cruelty in it: make one listener throw and
watch the second one never run. That is the pitfall from Part 3, learned in
thirty seconds instead of in production.

**Readiness check:** for each of the seven, can you state in one sentence the
*problem* it solves without using the pattern's name? "Behaviour depends on
which lifecycle stage the object is in" is the answer for State. If you can only
describe the structure and not the problem, you will be unable to justify the
pattern when pushed, which is the failure mode Part 3 warns about.

## Week 3 — Your first two full problems

This is the week the real learning happens. Two problems, each built twice.

**Read:** [Part 4 — how to drive an LLD round](/lld/part-4-how-to-drive-an-lld-interview-the-method)
and [Part 5 — the parking lot](/lld/part-5-worked-example-design-a-parking-lot-the-canonical).

**Build:**

1. **Parking lot, attempted before reading the solution.** Give yourself ninety
   minutes and a timer. Then read Part 5 and diff it against what you wrote —
   not to feel bad, but to find the specific decisions you did not know were
   decisions. Most beginners discover they put pricing inside the lot, which is
   exactly the lesson.
2. **Parking lot, extended.** Add electric-vehicle charging spots and monthly
   passes to *your* version. Count the files. This is the drill, and it is the
   most valuable ninety minutes of the four weeks.
3. **Vending machine, from scratch.** Ninety minutes. Model the states
   explicitly — idle, has-money, dispensing, out-of-service — and implement the
   refund path, because the refund path is where a boolean-based design falls
   apart and a state machine pays for itself.

**Readiness check:** with no notes, can you produce a runnable parking lot with
pluggable pricing in under seventy-five minutes, including a `main` that
demonstrates it? Time yourself honestly. If you are at a hundred minutes, the
gap is typing speed and Java fluency rather than design knowledge, and the fix
is more repetitions, not more reading.

## Week 4 — Concurrency, and a timed mock

**Read:** [Part 8 — machine-coding survival tips](/lld/part-8-machine-coding-round-survival-tips)
and, if concurrency is shaky,
[Java concurrency](/java/3-concurrency-senior-must-have).

**Build:**

1. **The two-cars-one-spot test.** Take your week 3 parking lot and write a test
   with a `CountDownLatch` that releases two threads at the same spot and
   asserts exactly one succeeds. Then break it deliberately — remove the
   `synchronized` — and run it a few hundred times in a loop until it fails.
   **Watching a race condition actually happen is worth more than any
   explanation of one**, and it gives you a real story to tell when asked.
2. **Elevator system.** Ninety minutes. It forces you to separate scheduling
   from movement, which is the intermediate-level skill.
3. **A timed 90-minute mock, out loud, recorded.** Pick a problem you have not
   seen — BookMyShow or the ATM — set a timer, narrate your reasoning aloud as
   if someone were listening, and record it. Then watch it back.

The playback is uncomfortable and it is where the round is actually won. Check
three things: did you scope out anything explicitly in the first five minutes,
did you state an axis of change before introducing an interface, and did you
reserve time at the end for concurrency and testing? Almost everyone fails the
third one on their first attempt.

**Readiness check:** you are ready when you can take an unseen intermediate
problem, drive it end to end in ninety minutes to something that compiles and
runs, name the axes of change before you abstract, and answer "how would you add
X?" with a specific file count.

## If you have eight weeks

Do not compress; use the room. Spend two weeks on each of the four above, and
use the extra time as follows:

- **Weeks 1–2:** the same foundation work, plus rewriting one real class from a
  project you shipped with the principles applied. Real code you know well makes
  a much better interview story than a toy.
- **Weeks 3–4:** patterns, plus the library management system and the logging
  framework — both teach modelling distinctions (title versus copy, formatter
  versus appender) that the starred problems do not.
- **Weeks 5–6:** parking lot, vending machine, elevator, and the extension drill
  on each.
- **Weeks 7–8:** BookMyShow, Splitwise, and two timed mocks a week. Interleave
  with [system design](/system-design) on alternate days so both rounds stay
  warm.

<Callout type="pitfall" title="How this plan usually fails">
- **Reading Part 3 five times and writing no code.** Patterns feel learnable by
  reading because the explanations are short. They are not. Seven tiny programs
  beat seven re-reads.
- **Attempting the parking lot only after reading the solution.** You get the
  comfortable feeling of understanding and none of the feedback. Attempt first,
  always.
- **Never running a timer.** The interview is a timed exercise and time pressure
  changes what you can do. A design you can produce in three hours is not
  evidence about a ninety-minute round.
- **Skipping the extension drill because the problem "already works".** The
  drill is the whole point. A design is only good relative to the changes it
  survives.
- **Practising silently.** The bottleneck in a real round is talking while
  thinking. If you have never done both at once, the first time will be in the
  interview.
- **Treating week 4's concurrency as optional.** It is the most common
  differentiating follow-up in the entire round, and it is also the one
  beginners most reliably skip.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="A realistic day-to-day shape">
Weekdays, ninety minutes: twenty minutes reading, seventy typing. Weekend, one
three-hour block: a full timed problem plus the extension drill.

Keep one file called `lld-notes.md` with two lists — patterns you have
implemented and the problem each solves, and mistakes you made and what you
would do differently. Re-read it for fifteen minutes the morning of the
interview. It is far more useful than re-reading any lesson, because it is
written in your own words about your own gaps.
</Callout>
