## Layer 1 — The mental model

A beginner treats an LLD round as a design problem to solve. It is better
understood as a *performance* to deliver, with a script you have rehearsed.

> **The interviewer is not comparing your design to an ideal one. They are
> deciding whether they would trust you to own an ambiguous feature.** The
> design is the medium; the thing being recorded is whether you scoped before
> designing, named what varies before choosing an abstraction, and could defend
> a decision when pushed.

That reframing has a practical consequence: the biggest scoring gains are
available in the first ten minutes and the last ten, and those are precisely the
parts beginners rush through on the way to writing code.

Six phases, with times for a 45–60 minute round. Adjust proportionally for a
90-minute machine-coding round — the phases are the same, the coding phase
absorbs the extra time.

```
1. CLARIFY & SCOPE          5 min   requirements in, non-requirements out
2. ENTITIES & RESPONSIBILITIES  5 min   the nouns, and one sentence of job each
3. CLASS DIAGRAM & INTERFACES  10 min   the skeleton, interfaces on the axes of change
4. CODE THE CORE            20 min   the 3–4 classes that carry the design
5. WALK A SCENARIO           5 min   trace one request end to end, out loud
6. EXTENSIONS, CONCURRENCY, TESTS  5 min   critique your own design first
```

The single habit that separates strong candidates: **you drive.** You announce
each phase, you state its output, you move on. An interviewer who has to prompt
you to start coding has already written something on the scorecard.

## Layer 2 — The mechanism

### Phase 1 — Clarify and scope (5 minutes)

The problem statement is deliberately underspecified. Your first job is to make
it specific enough to design, and small enough to finish.

Ask four or five questions, no more. The ones that always earn their keep:

- **"What are the core use cases you want working?"** Get them to name two or
  three. Everything else is out of scope by agreement.
- **"What is likely to change or be extended later?"** This is the highest-value
  question in the round and almost nobody asks it. The answer tells you where to
  put interfaces.
- **"Is this single-process and in-memory, or do I need persistence?"** In a
  machine-coding round the answer is nearly always in-memory. Ask anyway, so it
  is agreed rather than assumed.
- **"Is this concurrent — multiple users or gates acting at once?"** The answer
  determines whether you need synchronisation, and asking it signals you know it
  matters.
- **"How much of this should I actually code versus design?"** Legitimate and
  useful. It tells you how to split your time.

Then do the thing beginners skip: **say what you are not building.**

> *"So: park and unpark a vehicle, compute a fee on exit, support multiple
> floors and spot types. I'm going to treat payment processing, user accounts
> and reservations as out of scope and assume they're handled elsewhere — is
> that reasonable?"*

This is not dodging work. It is the clearest available signal of someone who has
shipped under a deadline, and it protects the forty minutes you have left. The
equivalent move in system design is described in
[the 45-minute framework](/system-design/1-the-framework-how-to-drive-a-45-minute-design-interview),
and it scores for exactly the same reason.

<Callout type="tip" title="Ask about the axes of change, explicitly">
The question *"which of these rules is most likely to change — the pricing, the
spot allocation, or the vehicle types?"* does three things at once. It gets you
the information that selects your abstractions. It tells the interviewer you
think about designs in terms of cost of change rather than aesthetics. And it
pre-loads your answer to the follow-up they were already planning to ask, which
is almost always "now add X".

Whatever they name, that is where your interface goes. Whatever they do not
name, keep concrete and say why: *"I'll keep the floor layout as a plain class —
you mentioned it doesn't vary, so an abstraction there would be cost with no
benefit."*
</Callout>

### Phase 2 — Entities and responsibilities (5 minutes)

Pull the nouns out of the requirements. They are your candidate classes. Then do
the part that matters: **give each one a one-sentence job description, out
loud.**

> *"`ParkingSpot` owns whether it's occupied and by what. `ParkingFloor` owns a
> collection of spots and can find a free one. `Ticket` is an immutable record
> of an entry. `ParkingLot` is the entry point that coordinates floors,
> allocation and pricing."*

If you cannot state a class's job in one sentence without using "and", it is
doing too much. That is the cheapest Single Responsibility check available and
it takes four seconds per class.

Two habits that pay off here:

- **Discard nouns that are not classes.** Not every noun earns a class. "Fee" is
  a value; `Money` might be a class, `Fee` probably is not. Saying "I'll model
  fee as a value on the ticket rather than its own type" shows discrimination.
- **Name the verbs and place them.** Park, unpark, allocate, price. Each verb
  belongs to the class that owns the data it touches. A verb whose data lives
  somewhere else is a sign you have put the method on the wrong class.

### Phase 3 — Class diagram and interfaces (10 minutes)

Sketch boxes and arrows. Do not draw formal UML; nobody wants it. Do name the
relationships as you draw — "the lot *composes* floors, so floors don't outlive
the lot" — because that is the part being listened to.

The rule for this phase: **write the interfaces before their implementations.**
Say why as you do it.

```java
// Declared first, deliberately. These two are the axes of change the
// interviewer named, so everything else will depend on these, not on classes.
public interface SpotAllocationStrategy {
    Optional<ParkingSpot> allocate(List<ParkingFloor> floors, Vehicle vehicle);
}

public interface PricingStrategy {
    Money fee(Ticket ticket, Instant exitTime);
}
```

Writing the interface first forces you to decide what a component *is* before
you get lost in how it works, and it gives the interviewer something to react to
early — which is good, because a correction at minute twelve is cheap and one at
minute forty is not.

Before leaving this phase, run the extensibility check on yourself, out loud:

> *"If I had to add electric-vehicle charging spots tomorrow, I'd add an enum
> constant and one allocation rule — `ParkingLot` doesn't change. If I had to
> add weekend surge pricing, that's one new `PricingStrategy` implementation.
> That's the property I was designing for."*

You have just pre-answered the deep-dive question. Interviewers notice.

### Phase 4 — Code the core (20 minutes)

Write the three or four classes that carry the design. Not all of them — you do
not have time, and completeness is not what is being measured.

What to write, in priority order:

1. The interfaces on the axes of change, with real signatures.
2. The core entity that owns the tricky invariant — the one with the
   `synchronized` block or the validation.
3. The coordinator that ties them together, showing the dependencies arriving
   through the constructor.
4. One implementation of each interface, so the design is demonstrably real.

What to stub, with a comment and a spoken note: everything else.

```java
final class ParkingLot {
    private final List<ParkingFloor> floors;
    private final SpotAllocationStrategy allocation;      // injected: DIP
    private final PricingStrategy pricing;                // injected: swappable

    ParkingLot(List<ParkingFloor> floors,
               SpotAllocationStrategy allocation,
               PricingStrategy pricing) {
        this.floors = List.copyOf(floors);
        this.allocation = allocation;
        this.pricing = pricing;
    }

    Ticket park(Vehicle vehicle) {
        ParkingSpot spot = allocation.allocate(floors, vehicle)
                .orElseThrow(() -> new NoSpotAvailableException(vehicle.type()));
        if (!spot.tryOccupy(vehicle)) return park(vehicle);   // lost the race, retry
        return Ticket.issue(vehicle, spot, Instant.now());
    }

    // Deliberately stubbed — same shape as park(), releases the spot and
    // delegates the fee to the pricing strategy. I'll say this rather than
    // spend three minutes typing it.
    Money unpark(Ticket ticket) { throw new UnsupportedOperationException("TODO"); }
}
```

Two habits while typing:

- **Narrate the non-obvious lines.** "`List.copyOf` because I don't want a
  caller mutating my floors after construction." "The retry on a failed
  `tryOccupy` is because allocation and occupation aren't atomic together — I'll
  come back to that in the concurrency discussion." Silent typing is wasted
  time; an interviewer cannot score code they do not understand the reason for.
- **Keep the names boring and precise.** `tryOccupy` rather than `doIt`.
  `NoSpotAvailableException` rather than `RuntimeException`. Naming is explicitly
  on most scorecards and it is free.

### Phase 5 — Walk a scenario (5 minutes)

Take one concrete case and trace it through your code, out loud, naming each
call.

> *"A car arrives at gate 1. The gate calls `lot.park(car)`. The lot asks the
> allocation strategy for a spot; the nearest-first strategy scans floors in
> order and returns the first spot where `canFit` is true. The lot calls
> `tryOccupy`, which is synchronized on the spot, so exactly one of two
> competing gates wins. On success we issue a `Ticket` with the entry time. On
> failure we retry allocation, because another gate took it."*

This phase does three things. It proves the design actually works, which
catches your own gaps — most missing methods are discovered here. It
demonstrates you can reason about your code rather than just produce it. And it
sets up the concurrency discussion naturally, because tracing two simultaneous
cars is the obvious next sentence.

Trace the happy path first, then one failure: the lot is full, or the ticket is
already used.

### Phase 6 — Extensions, concurrency and testing (5 minutes)

Reserve this time. It is disproportionately valuable and it is the phase that
gets eaten when you do not watch the clock.

**Extensions.** Offer them before being asked: *"Three things I'd expect to be
asked for next: electric-vehicle charging spots, which is an enum constant plus
an allocation rule; monthly passes, which is a new `PricingStrategy` plus a
lookup at entry; and multiple lots, which changes this from a single-process
design to a distributed one and moves allocation to a shared store."* You have
demonstrated the extensibility you designed for, in fifteen seconds.

**Concurrency.** Name the race before they do. *"Two gates can call `park` at the
same instant. Allocation is a read of free spots and occupation is a write, so
between them another thread can take the spot. I handled it by making
`tryOccupy` synchronized on the spot and retrying on failure, which keeps the
lock narrow. The alternative is a single lock around the whole allocation, which
is simpler to reason about but serialises every gate."* Two options, a choice, a
reason.

**Testing.** Answer in terms of seams: *"`ParkingLot` takes both strategies
through the constructor, so I can test allocation with a fake pricing strategy
that returns zero, and test pricing with no lot at all. The concurrency case I'd
test with a `CountDownLatch` releasing two threads at the same spot and
asserting exactly one succeeds."* That last sentence is specific enough that
the interviewer knows you have actually written a concurrency test.

### Recovering when things go wrong

**You are running out of time.** Do not accelerate and produce garbage. Stub the
method and state the intent: *"I'm going to leave `unpark` as a stub — it's the
mirror of `park`: release the spot, then delegate to the pricing strategy. I'd
rather spend the remaining five minutes on the concurrency discussion, which I
think is more interesting. Does that work?"* Naming the trade and asking is a
senior move, and it is far better than a half-written method you never explain.

**You forget a pattern's name.** Describe the shape. *"I want something that
sits in front of the real object with the same interface and does caching
before delegating — I can't remember what it's called, but that's the
structure."* Almost every interviewer will supply the word, and describing the
mechanism correctly was the thing worth points. **Pattern names are vocabulary,
not knowledge; being unable to recall a word is not the same as being unable to
design.**

**You realise your design is wrong halfway through.** Say so and fix it. *"I've
put pricing inside `ParkingLot` and it's already awkward — adding weekend rates
would mean editing this method. Let me pull it out behind an interface."*
Catching your own mistake and correcting it scores higher than a design that was
right by luck, because it is direct evidence of the review skill they are hiring
for.

**The interviewer pushes back twice on a decision.** They are telling you
something. *"That's fair — if the allocation rule changes per floor rather than
per lot, my design is wrong and the strategy should live on the floor. Let me
move it."* Defending a choice past its expiry costs far more than changing it.

**You go blank.** Return to the requirements. *"Let me re-read the
requirements and make sure I've got the entities right"* is always a legal move
and it buys you thinking time while looking deliberate.

## Layer 3 — Interview surface

<Callout type="interview" title="Follow-ups to rehearse out loud">
1. **"Any questions before you start?"** Always yes. Core use cases, what will
   change, in-memory or persisted, concurrent or not, code or design.
2. **"Why did you put an interface there and not there?"** Because that is the
   axis of change they named. Say it in those words.
3. **"How would you add feature X?"** Walk the files you would touch, and count
   them. One new file is the answer you designed for.
4. **"What happens if two users do this simultaneously?"** Identify the
   check-then-act window, name your fix, and state the alternative you rejected
   and why.
5. **"How would you test this?"** Answer in seams: which dependencies arrive
   through constructors and can therefore be faked. Then name one specific test.
6. **"Isn't this over-engineered?"** Possibly. Name the abstraction you would
   drop first and the condition under which you would keep it.
7. **"You're running low on time."** Stub, state the intent, and propose what to
   spend the remaining minutes on.
8. **"Why this class and not that one?"** Whichever owns the data the behaviour
   touches. Behaviour follows data.
9. **"What's the weakest part of your design?"** Have an answer ready. Naming
   your own weak point unprompted is one of the highest-value things you can say.
10. **"Would you use a database here?"** Not unless asked. A `Map` behind a
    repository interface keeps the option open at zero cost.
11. **"You used a pattern — why that one?"** Lead with the problem, not the
    name. If you cannot state the problem, do not use the name.
12. **"How long would this take you to build for real?"** They are checking
    whether you have shipped. Be honest, and mention what you would add that the
    interview version skipped: validation, logging, persistence, tests.
</Callout>

<Callout type="pitfall" title="Things that bite in practice">
- **Coding in minute two.** Without scoping, you will design the wrong thing
  well. Five minutes of questions saves twenty of rework.
- **Designing every class.** Fifteen classes sketched and none written loses to
  four classes written and the rest named. Depth beats breadth here.
- **Silence while thinking.** An interviewer cannot score thoughts. If you need
  twenty seconds, say "let me think about how to structure this" and take them.
- **No time left for concurrency or testing.** Those last five minutes carry
  more signal per minute than any other part. Watch the clock from minute
  thirty.
- **Arguing instead of adapting.** Two pushes on the same point is feedback.
  Take it.
- **Treating the stub as a failure.** A clearly stated stub with the intent
  spoken aloud reads as prioritisation. An unexplained gap reads as running out
  of ability.
- **Forgetting the interviewer is a person.** Ask "does this direction make
  sense before I code it?" at the end of phase three. A ten-second check-in can
  save the whole round.
</Callout>
