## How to use this list

Each problem below is broken into five fields, and the one that matters most is
**the real test** — the single design decision the problem exists to probe. A
problem statement is a costume; underneath, "design a vending machine" is a
question about state machines and "design Splitwise" is a question about whether
you model the balance or recompute it.

Work them in the order given. Do not read a solution before attempting the
problem — the value is entirely in the attempt. And follow the rule from
[Part 7](/lld/part-7-your-4-week-lld-plan-youre-a-beginner-so-build): after each
one, invent a new requirement and count the files you have to touch.

Starred problems are the highest-frequency. **If you do the four starred ones
properly, you can handle the majority of LLD rounds**, because the underlying
decisions repeat.

## Beginner — one clear decision each

These have a small entity set and one obvious design axis. The goal is to finish
them cleanly rather than cleverly.

### 1. Parking lot (worked in full)

- **Entities:** `ParkingLot`, `ParkingFloor`, `ParkingSpot`, `Vehicle`, `Ticket`, `Gate`
- **The real test:** can you find the axes of change — pricing and allocation —
  and put interfaces on exactly those, leaving everything else concrete?
- **Expected pattern:** Strategy for both axes, Factory for vehicles and spots,
  Facade for the lot itself.
- **Classic follow-up:** two cars arrive at the last free spot simultaneously.
- **The trap:** putting fee calculation inside `ParkingLot`. It fails the first
  follow-up and it is by far the most common version of this design.
- Full solution: [Part 5](/lld/part-5-worked-example-design-a-parking-lot-the-canonical).

### 2. Vending machine

- **Entities:** `VendingMachine`, `Slot`, `Product`, `Coin`, `Inventory`, plus a state type
- **The real test:** do you model the lifecycle as explicit states, or as a pile
  of booleans? This is the purest state-machine problem in the set.
- **Expected pattern:** State. Idle, has-money, item-selected, dispensing,
  out-of-service, each knowing which operations it permits.
- **Classic follow-up:** the user cancels mid-transaction, or the machine cannot
  make exact change.
- **The trap:** three booleans (`hasMoney`, `isDispensing`, `isSoldOut`) give
  you eight representable states of which four are legal, and the illegal ones
  are reachable. Also: the refund path is where the state machine earns its
  keep, so do not skip it.

### 3. Tic-tac-toe

- **Entities:** `Game`, `Board`, `Cell`, `Player`, `Move`, `WinStrategy`
- **The real test:** can you separate the rules from the board? A board that
  knows how to detect a win is a board that cannot be reused for Connect-4.
- **Expected pattern:** Strategy for the win condition, Command if you support
  undo.
- **Classic follow-up:** generalise to an `n` by `n` board, or to Connect-4.
- **The trap:** hardcoding the eight winning lines. It works, it is fast to
  write, and it dies the moment the follow-up arrives. Detect a win by scanning
  the row, column and diagonals of the last move placed.

### 4. Snake and ladders

- **Entities:** `Game`, `Board`, `Cell`, `Jump`, `Player`, `Dice`
- **The real test:** do you notice that a snake and a ladder are the same thing?
  Both are a jump from one cell to another; only the sign of the difference
  differs.
- **Expected pattern:** almost none, deliberately. This problem rewards good
  modelling and punishes pattern-forcing.
- **Classic follow-up:** multiple dice, or a rule that rolling a six grants
  another turn.
- **The trap:** separate `Snake` and `Ladder` classes with duplicated logic. If
  an interviewer sees you unify them unprompted, that is a strong signal.

### 5. Logging framework

- **Entities:** `Logger`, `LogLevel`, `LogRecord`, `Appender`, `Formatter`
- **The real test:** two independent axes vary — where the log goes (console,
  file, network) and how it is formatted (plain, JSON). Do you recognise that
  they are independent, or do you create `JsonFileLogger` and
  `PlainConsoleLogger`?
- **Expected pattern:** Chain of Responsibility for level filtering, Strategy
  for the formatter, Observer for multiple appenders.
- **Classic follow-up:** make it thread-safe and asynchronous without losing
  messages.
- **The trap:** class explosion from crossing the two axes. Composition gives
  you three formatters plus three appenders; inheritance gives you nine classes.

## Intermediate — multiple interacting decisions

These have more entities and at least one genuine concurrency or consistency
question.

### 6. Elevator system

- **Entities:** `ElevatorSystem`, `Elevator`, `Request` (internal and external),
  `SchedulingStrategy`, `Direction`, `DoorState`
- **The real test:** can you separate *which* elevator answers a call
  (scheduling) from *how* an elevator services its own queue (movement)? These
  are two different algorithms and beginners fuse them.
- **Expected pattern:** Strategy for scheduling, State for the elevator's own
  lifecycle (idle, moving up, moving down, doors open).
- **Classic follow-up:** four elevators and a hall call on floor 7 — which one
  goes, and why? Then: what changes during a fire alarm?
- **The trap:** modelling a request as just a floor number. An external request
  has a floor *and* a direction; an internal one has only a destination. Merging
  them makes the scheduler impossible to write correctly.

### 7. ATM

- **Entities:** `ATM`, `Card`, `Account`, `CashDispenser`, `Transaction`,
  `CashCassette`
- **The real test:** two things at once — the session lifecycle as a state
  machine, and cash dispensing as a denomination problem where you must not
  commit the debit unless the dispense succeeds.
- **Expected pattern:** State for the session, Chain of Responsibility for
  dispensing (the 2000 handler passes the remainder to the 500 handler, and so
  on).
- **Classic follow-up:** the cash jams after the account is debited.
- **The trap:** debiting before dispensing with no compensation path. Say out
  loud that these two steps are not atomic and that you would either reserve the
  cash first or record a reversal.

### 8. Library management system

- **Entities:** `Library`, `Book`, `BookItem` (a physical copy), `Member`,
  `Loan`, `Reservation`, `Catalog`
- **The real test:** do you distinguish a *book* (a title, an ISBN) from a *book
  item* (one physical copy with a barcode)? Everything else in the problem
  depends on that split, and missing it makes reservations unmodellable.
- **Expected pattern:** Observer for reservation notifications, Strategy for
  fine calculation, Repository for the catalog.
- **Classic follow-up:** two members reserve the last copy at the same instant.
- **The trap:** one `Book` class with a `quantity` field. It cannot express
  which copy is overdue, and it makes the reservation queue meaningless.

### 9. Splitwise / expense sharing

- **Entities:** `User`, `Group`, `Expense`, `SplitStrategy`, `BalanceSheet`,
  `Settlement`
- **The real test:** do you store *balances* or recompute them from the expense
  log? The log is the source of truth and the balance is a derived view, and
  candidates who store only balances cannot answer "why do I owe this?".
- **Expected pattern:** Strategy for the split (equal, exact amounts,
  percentage, shares), Observer for notifications.
- **Classic follow-up:** simplify debts so that three people owing each other in
  a cycle settle with the fewest transfers.
- **The trap:** floating-point money. Splitting 100 rupees three ways with
  `double` loses a paisa and the balances never reach zero. Use minor units as a
  `long`, and decide explicitly who absorbs the remainder.

### 10. Rate limiter

- **Entities:** `RateLimiter` interface, one class per algorithm, `Bucket`,
  `Clock`
- **The real test:** can you make the algorithm pluggable behind a one-method
  interface, and can you do refill without a background thread?
- **Expected pattern:** Strategy. Token bucket, leaky bucket, fixed window,
  sliding window log, sliding window counter.
- **Classic follow-up:** two requests for the same key arrive on different
  threads — and then, is your clock injectable so this is testable?
- **The trap:** a background thread refilling every bucket. Compute the refill
  lazily from the elapsed time on each call instead; it is simpler, it scales to
  millions of keys, and it is testable with a fake clock. The distributed
  version is in
  [design a distributed rate limiter](/system-design/case-2-design-a-distributed-rate-limiter).

### 11. Notification service

- **Entities:** `NotificationService`, `Notification`, `Channel`,
  `UserPreferences`, `RetryPolicy`, `Template`
- **The real test:** do channels stay independent of the dispatch logic, so
  adding WhatsApp is one file?
- **Expected pattern:** Strategy or Observer for channels, Decorator for retry
  and rate limiting wrapped around a channel, Factory to build a channel from a
  preference.
- **Classic follow-up:** one channel is down — do you fail the whole
  notification, and how do you retry without duplicating?
- **The trap:** a `switch` on channel type inside `send`. Also: building retry
  into every channel rather than decorating one retry implementation over all of
  them.

## Advanced — concurrency, consistency, and larger entity sets

These are where L5 loops go. Each has a genuine correctness question, not just a
modelling one.

### 12. BookMyShow / movie ticket booking

- **Entities:** `Movie`, `Show`, `Screen`, `Seat`, `SeatLock`, `Booking`,
  `Payment`, `PricingStrategy`
- **The real test:** **seat locking.** Two users select seat A5 at the same
  moment; one must get it, the other must get a clear failure, and a user who
  abandons payment must not hold the seat forever.
- **Expected pattern:** a `SeatLockProvider` with an expiry, Strategy for
  pricing, State for the booking lifecycle (created, locked, paid, confirmed,
  expired).
- **Classic follow-up:** the user locks the seats and then never pays. Then:
  what if the payment succeeds *after* the lock expired?
- **The trap:** locking at the booking level rather than the seat level, or
  locking with no timeout. A lock without an expiry is a permanently lost seat.
  This problem is really a distributed-lock question wearing an LLD costume, and
  saying so scores.

### 13. Chess

- **Entities:** `Game`, `Board`, `Square`, `Piece` hierarchy, `Move`,
  `MoveValidator`, `GameState`
- **The real test:** where do the movement rules live? Per-piece is the
  answer — `Bishop` knows how a bishop moves — but check and checkmate are
  board-level rules that no single piece can evaluate.
- **Expected pattern:** Strategy or polymorphism per piece for movement, Command
  for move and undo, State for the game status.
- **Classic follow-up:** implement undo, then castling and en passant — both of
  which depend on history, not just the current board.
- **The trap:** a giant `isValidMove` switching on piece type; and modelling
  the board as a flat array with no `Square` abstraction, which makes en passant
  and castling nearly unwriteable.

### 14. Food delivery (Swiggy/Zomato-style)

- **Entities:** `Restaurant`, `MenuItem`, `Cart`, `Order`, `DeliveryPartner`,
  `AssignmentStrategy`, `OrderState`, `PricingEngine`
- **The real test:** the order lifecycle is long and every stage has legal and
  illegal transitions — placed, accepted, preparing, picked up, delivered,
  cancelled — and cancellation rules differ by stage.
- **Expected pattern:** State for the order, Strategy for partner assignment and
  for pricing/surge, Observer for status updates to the customer.
- **Classic follow-up:** two orders are assigned the same delivery partner, or
  a restaurant rejects after the customer has paid.
- **The trap:** an `OrderStatus` enum with a `setStatus` method. Any status can
  then be set from any other, so "delivered" to "placed" is representable.
  Transitions need to be validated in one place.

## A note on how to actually practise these

<Callout type="tip" title="The exercise that teaches the most">
Implement one problem end to end. Then, without looking at your design, write
down a new requirement — "add a second pricing rule", "add a third player",
"make it work with two machines" — and implement it while counting the files you
touch.

**If the count is one or two, your abstractions were on the right axes. If it is
five, they were on the wrong ones, and that is the most useful feedback
available anywhere in LLD preparation.** It is also exactly the question the
interviewer will ask, so you are rehearsing the answer with real evidence
behind it.
</Callout>

Two practical rules for your practice sessions:

**Time-box.** Ninety minutes for an intermediate problem, no extensions. An
unfinished design under a timer teaches you more about your bottleneck than a
perfect one over three evenings, because the interview is the timed version.

**Write a runnable `main` every time.** A design you cannot run is a design you
have not tested against reality. Most of the gaps in a class diagram surface in
the first ten lines of the driver.

<Callout type="pitfall" title="Patterns that repeat across the whole list">
Once you notice these, new problems stop feeling new:

- **Anything with pricing, scheduling, matching or splitting is a Strategy.**
  Parking fees, ride matching, elevator scheduling, expense splitting, rate
  limiting.
- **Anything with a lifecycle is a State machine.** Vending machine, ATM
  session, order, elevator, booking.
- **Anything with a shared limited resource is a locking problem.** Parking
  spots, cinema seats, library copies, delivery partners. The question is always
  where the lock goes and whether it expires.
- **Anything with notifications is an Observer**, and the follow-up is always
  about a slow or failing listener.
- **Anything with money needs a `Money` type over integer minor units.** Every
  time. `double` will be challenged and you will lose the exchange.
</Callout>
