## What this track is assessed on

People assume the coding round is a pass-or-fail test of whether the solution works. It is
not, and believing it is produces behaviour that costs the round: going silent for eight
minutes, coding before there is a plan, treating a hint as an accusation.

**What is scored is how you get to the solution; the solution is evidence rather than the
thing itself.** Interviewers record specific signals: did the candidate clarify the
constraints, state a brute-force approach before optimising, explain the trade-off, write
code that compiles and reads cleanly, test an edge case unprompted, and state the complexity
correctly. Someone who reaches the optimal answer in silence and cannot explain why it works
scores below someone who narrates a clean path to a slower solution and then spots the
improvement themselves.

The second thing assessed, and the reason this track is organised around patterns rather
than problems, is recognition speed. Roughly 90 percent of questions are variations on about
fifteen shapes, and nobody derives a monotonic stack from first principles under a 30-minute
timer. A strong candidate reads "next greater element" and recognises the shape in twenty
seconds, which buys the rest of the time for the parts that are actually scored.

So the goal is not to teach you algorithms you do not know. It is to make the recognition
automatic and the narration fluent.

## Where you stand, and what to prioritise

You have solved several hundred problems, and DSA is one of your two strong tracks alongside
SQL. **This is a refresh, not a grind, and treating it like a from-scratch course is the main
way to waste time here.**

Your bottleneck is retrieval latency and verbalisation, not knowledge. You have written a BFS
before; the question is whether you can write it from memory in four minutes while explaining
it, without pausing to remember whether the visited check goes before or after the enqueue.
So structure the work around two things.

**Timed reps, not reading.** Two or three problems per pattern with a 25-to-35 minute timer
is enough to reload a pattern you already know. Read the lesson, close it, solve.

**Talking while you code.** This is genuinely new for most self-taught practice, because
nobody narrates alone — and it is the part being graded. It feels absurd for the first few
sessions and then becomes automatic, which is why it has to be practised rather than planned.

The patterns most likely to feel rusty are dynamic programming, backtracking and tries, since
they are least used in backend work; graphs and heaps come back fastest. Flag whatever feels
slow and redo it after three days and again after seven.

## The order to work through it

The two meta-lessons come first. They are about the round rather than about
algorithms, and they change how every subsequent practice rep should be run.

1. [How to approach ANY coding problem (say this out loud in the interview)](/dsa/how-to-approach-any-coding-problem-say-this-out-loud-in-the)
   — first, because it defines the script you will use on every problem after
   this. Without it, your practice reps train the wrong habit.
2. [Complexity cheat sheet (memorize this)](/dsa/complexity-cheat-sheet-memorize-this)
   — short, and worth having in recall before you start, since you will state a
   complexity at the end of every single problem.

Then the fifteen patterns, in order. The sequence is deliberate: each one reuses
machinery from an earlier one, so working through it in order means you are always
adding one idea rather than several.

3. [Pattern 1 — Arrays & Hashing](/dsa/pattern-1-arrays-hashing) — the trade you
   make in every other pattern, memory for time.
4. [Pattern 2 — Two Pointers](/dsa/pattern-2-two-pointers) — the first structured
   way to avoid a nested loop.
5. [Pattern 3 — Sliding Window](/dsa/pattern-3-sliding-window) — two pointers with
   state carried between steps.
6. [Pattern 4 — Binary Search](/dsa/pattern-4-binary-search) — including search on
   the answer space, which is the version that actually gets asked.
7. [Pattern 5 — Stack](/dsa/pattern-5-stack) — monotonic stacks, the highest
   recognition-value shape in the list.
8. [Pattern 6 — Linked List](/dsa/pattern-6-linked-list) — pointer manipulation,
   and the foundation for the LRU cache question in the
   [Java track](/java/practice-lru-cache).
9. [Pattern 7 — Trees (DFS & BFS)](/dsa/pattern-7-trees-dfs-bfs) — recursion
   discipline, and the prerequisite for graphs.
10. [Pattern 8 — Tries (Prefix Trees)](/dsa/pattern-8-tries-prefix-trees) — a tree
    with a different node shape; usually the rustiest of the tree patterns.
11. [Pattern 9 — Heaps / Priority Queue](/dsa/pattern-9-heaps-priority-queue) —
    top-K and streaming problems, and directly useful in design rounds.
12. [Pattern 10 — Backtracking](/dsa/pattern-10-backtracking) — after trees,
    because it is a DFS over a decision space rather than over nodes.
13. [Pattern 11 — Graphs (BFS/DFS/Union-Find)](/dsa/pattern-11-graphs-bfs-dfs-union-find)
    — the largest single lesson here, and the one with the widest question
    surface.
14. [Pattern 12 — Dynamic Programming](/dsa/pattern-12-dynamic-programming) —
    after backtracking, because memoised recursion is backtracking plus a cache,
    and framing it that way is what makes DP tractable rather than mysterious.
15. [Pattern 13 — Greedy](/dsa/pattern-13-greedy) — after DP, so that you can
    articulate why a greedy choice is safe, which is the real question being
    asked.
16. [Pattern 14 — Intervals](/dsa/pattern-14-intervals) — sorting plus a sweep;
    short, and very commonly asked.
17. [Pattern 15 — Bit Manipulation & Math](/dsa/pattern-15-bit-manipulation-math)
    — last, because it is the least transferable and mostly appears as a single
    trick question.

Then the two lessons that turn all of that into a schedule and a performance.

18. [6-week DSA sprint (pairs with the Master Roadmap, Phase 1-2)](/dsa/6-week-dsa-sprint-pairs-with-the-master-roadmap-phase-1-2)
    — the week-by-week plan; use it to pace the patterns above rather than doing
    them all in one push.
19. [Coding-round survival tips](/dsa/coding-round-survival-tips) — read a few
    days before an actual interview: what to do when you are stuck, how to take a
    hint well, and how to manage the clock.

Solve everything in Java. It is your strongest language and it is what you will
use in the interview, and the muscle memory for `Deque`, `PriorityQueue` and
`Map.computeIfAbsent` is worth building deliberately.

## What "ready" looks like

Pick a medium problem you have not seen, set a 30-minute timer, record yourself,
and check all of these were true.

- Within two minutes you restated the problem, asked about input constraints, and
  confirmed an edge case.
- You stated a brute-force approach and its complexity before writing code.
- You said which pattern you recognised, and why, out loud.
- You narrated continuously, with no silence longer than about fifteen seconds.
- The code compiled on the first or second attempt.
- You traced an example by hand unprompted and checked an empty or single-element
  input.
- You stated time and space complexity correctly, including the recursion stack.

The knowledge bar underneath: you can write BFS, DFS, binary search on an answer
space, a monotonic stack template, union-find with path compression, and a
one-dimensional DP from memory in under five minutes each, without looking anything
up. Finishing the starred problems in each pattern, roughly 75, makes you
interview-ready; all 150 makes you comfortable rather than merely prepared.

## How long this track takes

About 55,000 words across the fifteen patterns, but the reading is not where the time goes.
Budget six hours on the patterns and between 40 and 60 hours of timed problem solving, which
is what the six-week sprint is sized for.

Because this is your strong track, the correct instinct is to protect time for
[system design](/system-design) rather than over-invest here. Keep DSA at two or three
problems every weekday as maintenance and spend weekend blocks on design. Sharpness here
decays slowly once reloaded; design skill has to be built from zero.
