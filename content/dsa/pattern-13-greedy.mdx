## Layer 1 — When you reach for this

> Greedy is the easiest pattern to code and the hardest to justify. The
> algorithm is usually "sort by the right key, then one pass" — five lines. The
> difficulty is entirely in the word *right*. **A wrong greedy passes the sample
> inputs, because the sample inputs are small and friendly, and then fails on a
> test you never see.**

So the recognition question for greedy is not "does a greedy rule exist?" — one
always exists, you can always invent a rule. It is "is this greedy rule
*provably* optimal?"

Signals that greedy is worth trying:

- **The problem asks for a maximum or minimum count of something**, and the items
  have an obvious ordering key — deadlines, end times, sizes, ratios,
  frequencies.
- **Scheduling and interval questions.** Maximum non-overlapping meetings,
  minimum rooms, minimum removals; see
  [Pattern 14 — Intervals](/dsa/pattern-14-intervals).
- **The structure is a matroid-like "independent set" problem** — if a selection
  is valid, every subset of it is valid, and any smaller valid selection can be
  extended. Minimum spanning tree is the archetype, which is why both Kruskal
  and Prim are greedy and both are correct.
- **Reachability and coverage questions**: can you reach the end, what is the
  minimum number of jumps, the fewest arrows.
- **Each decision is genuinely local** — the choice you make now does not change
  what the remaining problem looks like beyond removing the item you took.

Signals that greedy is wrong and you need
[dynamic programming](/dsa/pattern-12-dynamic-programming):

- **Taking the best item now can lock you out of a better combination later.**
  That is the 0/1 knapsack signature: a single heavy, valuable item may block
  two lighter ones that together beat it.
- **The problem has a capacity, budget, or exact-target constraint** that couples
  the choices together.
- **You can construct a counterexample in thirty seconds.** This is the test, and
  it is the most useful habit in this entire pattern.

**The framing to carry into the interview: greedy commits, DP explores.** A
greedy algorithm makes one choice at each step and never revisits it, which is
why it is fast and why it needs a proof. DP considers every choice at each step
and keeps the best, which is why it is slower and why it needs no proof beyond
the recurrence being right. If you cannot prove the commit is safe, do not
commit.

## Layer 2 — The mechanism

### The exchange argument

There is one proof technique for greedy, and naming it out loud in an interview
immediately changes how the conversation goes.

**The exchange argument: take any optimal solution, and show that it can be
transformed step by step into the greedy solution without ever getting worse.
It follows that the greedy solution is itself optimal.**

The shape of the argument is always the same. Suppose the optimal solution `OPT`
and the greedy solution `G` first differ at position `k`. Greedy chose `g`; the
optimal chose `o`. Show that you can swap `o` for `g` in `OPT` and still have a
valid solution that is at least as good. Now `OPT` agrees with `G` in one more
position. Repeat. After finitely many swaps `OPT` has become `G` and has never
got worse, so `G` is optimal.

A related and sometimes easier variant is the **"greedy stays ahead"** argument:
show by induction that after each step, the greedy partial solution is at least
as good as any other partial solution by some measure — it has scheduled at least
as many meetings, or it has finished no later.

### Worked proof: interval scheduling, and why end time is the key

**Problem:** given intervals, select the maximum number that do not overlap.

The greedy rule is **sort by end time, and repeatedly take the earliest-ending
interval compatible with what you have already taken.**

Why end time? Because the only thing a chosen interval costs you is the part of
the timeline it consumes going forward. **Among all intervals available right
now, the one that ends earliest leaves the largest possible remaining timeline,
and a larger remaining timeline can never admit fewer future intervals.** That
sentence is the intuition; here is the exchange argument that makes it a proof.

Let `G = g1, g2, ...` be the greedy selection in order and `O = o1, o2, ...` any
optimal selection in order. Suppose they agree on the first `k - 1` choices and
differ at `k`. Greedy picked `gk`, the compatible interval with the smallest end
time, so `end(gk) <= end(ok)`. Replace `ok` with `gk` in `O`. The result is still
valid: `gk` starts after `g(k-1)` ends, because greedy only considers compatible
intervals, and everything after `ok` in `O` starts after `end(ok)`, which is at
or after `end(gk)`. The count is unchanged. So `O` with the swap is still optimal
and agrees with `G` one step further. Induct.

Now the two tempting alternatives, and why they fail — being able to produce
these counterexamples on demand is the real skill:

- **Sort by start time.** Counterexample: one interval `[0, 100]` and two
  intervals `[1, 2]` and `[3, 4]`. Earliest start takes the long one and gets 1;
  the answer is 2.
- **Sort by duration, shortest first.** Counterexample: `[0, 10]`, `[9, 11]`,
  `[10, 20]`. The shortest is `[9, 11]`, which conflicts with both of the others,
  so you get 1; the answer is 2.
- **Sort by fewest conflicts.** This is more subtle and still wrong; there are
  standard constructions where it loses by one. Mentioning that you know it fails
  is enough.

Only end time survives, and the reason survives with it.

```java
// Maximum non-overlapping intervals.
Arrays.sort(iv, (a, b) -> Integer.compare(a[1], b[1]));   // by END time
int count = 0, lastEnd = Integer.MIN_VALUE;
for (int[] x : iv) {
    if (x[0] >= lastEnd) {        // compatible with everything chosen so far
        count++;
        lastEnd = x[1];           // commit — never reconsidered
    }
}
```

Non-overlapping Intervals — "minimum removals to make the rest disjoint" — is
this same algorithm, reported as `n - count`.

### Worked example: Jump Game, and the invariant

**Problem:** each `nums[i]` is the maximum jump length from index `i`. Can you
reach the last index?

```java
int reach = 0;                         // furthest index reachable so far
for (int i = 0; i < nums.length; i++) {
    if (i > reach) return false;       // a gap we can never cross
    reach = Math.max(reach, i + nums[i]);
}
return true;                           // O(n) time, O(1) space
```

**The invariant: after processing index `i`, `reach` is the furthest index
reachable using only positions `0` through `i`.** The correctness argument is
that reachability here is an interval — if you can reach index `j`, you can reach
every index below it, since you can always jump shorter. So the reachable set is
always a prefix, and a single number describes it completely. That is why no
search is needed and why there is nothing to backtrack over.

Jump Game II, asking for the *minimum* number of jumps, extends the same idea
with a level structure: track the end of the current jump's range, and when `i`
reaches it, increment the jump count and extend the range to the furthest point
seen. **That is breadth-first search on the reachability intervals, with each
"level" being one jump** — which is why the answer is a shortest path and why
greedy gets it right.

### Worked example: Gas Station, and why the reset works

**Problem:** given `gas[i]` and `cost[i]` around a circle, find the unique start
index from which you can complete the circuit, or `-1`.

```java
int total = 0, tank = 0, start = 0;
for (int i = 0; i < n; i++) {
    int gain = gas[i] - cost[i];
    total += gain;
    tank  += gain;
    if (tank < 0) { start = i + 1; tank = 0; }    // reset
}
return total >= 0 ? start : -1;
```

Two separate claims, and you should state them separately.

**A solution exists if and only if the total gain is non-negative.** If the total
is negative you run out of fuel somewhere no matter where you start, because the
sum around the whole loop is fixed. If it is non-negative, a valid start exists.

**If the tank goes negative somewhere between `start` and `i`, then no index in
`start..i` can be a valid start.** This is the part people recite without
understanding. Suppose some `j` strictly between `start` and `i` worked. Starting
at `start`, the tank was non-negative at every point up to `j` — otherwise we
would have reset earlier — so arriving at `j` from `start` we had at least as
much fuel as starting fresh at `j` with zero. Therefore if we failed by `i`
starting from `start`, we would also fail by `i` starting from `j`, with less
fuel. So every candidate in the failed prefix is eliminated at once, which is why
one linear pass suffices rather than `n` simulations.

Notice the shape: **the greedy is fast because one failure eliminates a whole
block of candidates, not because one candidate is obviously best.** That is a
different flavour of greedy argument and it is worth having in your vocabulary.

### The other classic correct greedies

**Kadane's algorithm** for maximum subarray. At each index the running sum either
extends the previous subarray or restarts at the current element, `cur =
max(nums[i], cur + nums[i])`. The greedy commit is the restart: **a prefix with a
negative sum can never help any extension, so discard it immediately.** That is a
one-line exchange argument. It is also legitimately DP with a one-variable state,
which is a nice thing to point out.

**Huffman coding.** Repeatedly merge the two least-frequent symbols into one node
and push it back — a heap-driven greedy. The exchange argument: in any optimal
prefix code, the two least-frequent symbols can be swapped down to be siblings at
the deepest level without increasing the encoded length, because moving a less
frequent symbol deeper and a more frequent one shallower can only help. So some
optimal solution contains the greedy's first merge, and induction finishes it.

**Minimum spanning tree.** Kruskal adds the globally cheapest edge that does not
form a cycle; Prim grows a tree by its cheapest outgoing edge. Both rest on the
**cut property**: for any partition of the vertices into two sets, the cheapest
edge crossing the partition is in some MST. That is an exchange argument —
inserting the cheapest crossing edge into any spanning tree creates a cycle, and
removing any other crossing edge from that cycle leaves a spanning tree no more
expensive. See [Pattern 11](/dsa/pattern-11-graphs-bfs-dfs-union-find) for the
union-find that implements Kruskal.

### The classic greedy failures

Knowing these cold is what stops you committing to a wrong greedy.

**0/1 knapsack.** The natural greedy is "take items in decreasing order of
value per unit weight." It is correct for the *fractional* knapsack, where you
may take a piece of an item, and provably so by an exchange argument: any
solution not using the best ratio first can be improved by swapping in a sliver
of it. It is wrong for 0/1. Capacity 10, and items `(weight 6, value 12)` with
ratio 2, `(weight 5, value 9)` with ratio 1.8, and `(weight 5, value 9)`. Greedy
takes the first for 12 and cannot fit anything else. The optimum is the two
smaller items for 18. **Indivisibility is what breaks the exchange argument,
because you cannot swap in a fraction.**

**Coin change with arbitrary denominations.** With coins 1, 3 and 4 and an
amount of 6, greedy takes 4, then 1, then 1 — three coins. The optimum is 3 plus
3, two coins. Greedy is correct for *canonical* coin systems, which include real
currencies like `1, 5, 10, 25`, and that is exactly why the wrong intuition is so
durable: it works every day at the till. Determining whether an arbitrary system
is canonical is itself a hard problem, so in an interview the answer is always
DP. **This is the single best counterexample to have memorised, because it is
short and it refutes the most confident wrong answer in the pattern.**

**Longest path, travelling salesman, graph colouring, set cover.** All NP-hard,
all with plausible greedies that are merely approximations. Set cover's greedy is
worth knowing positively: picking the set covering the most uncovered elements is
within a `ln n` factor of optimal, and that bound is tight.

### The interview habit

When a greedy rule occurs to you, say this out loud: **"Let me check whether
greedy is safe here — I'll try to build a counterexample first."** Then actually
try, for thirty seconds, with three or four small items and a tight capacity. One
of two things happens. You find a counterexample, and you have saved yourself
from a wrong solution while demonstrating exactly the judgement being tested. Or
you fail to find one, and you can then articulate why — usually "taking the
earliest-ending item leaves the most room", or "a negative prefix can never
help" — which is the exchange argument in informal dress. That sentence
distinguishes a candidate who knows greedy algorithms from one who knows *when*
to use them.

### Edge cases and traps

- **Sorting by the wrong key.** Start time instead of end time is the most
  common, and it produces plausible wrong answers.
- **Comparator overflow.** `(a, b) -> a[1] - b[1]` breaks on large or
  mixed-sign values; use `Integer.compare`.
- **Ties in the sort key** where the tie-break matters.
- **Touching boundaries.** Whether `[1,2]` and `[2,3]` overlap changes the
  comparison from `>` to `>=`. Ask.
- **Assuming the greedy answer is unique.** Often many selections achieve the
  optimum; if the problem wants a specific one, it needs a tie-break rule.
- **Greedy plus a heap without realising it is greedy.** Meeting Rooms II and
  Task Scheduler are greedy rules implemented with a
  [heap](/dsa/pattern-9-heaps-priority-queue); the heap is the data structure,
  not the justification.

## Layer 3 — Interview surface

<Callout type="interview" title="Follow-ups to rehearse out loud">
1. **"How do you know greedy is correct here?"** The exchange argument: any
   optimal solution can be converted into the greedy one, one swap at a time,
   without getting worse.
2. **"Why sort by end time and not start time?"** The earliest-ending compatible
   interval leaves the most remaining timeline, and more room never admits fewer
   future intervals. Sorting by start fails on one long interval covering two
   short ones.
3. **"Why not sort by duration?"** Counterexample: `[0,10]`, `[9,11]`,
   `[10,20]`. The shortest interval conflicts with both others.
4. **"When is greedy wrong?"** When a local commitment can block a better
   combination — capacity constraints, exact targets, indivisible items.
5. **"Give me a coin system where greedy fails."** Coins 1, 3, 4 and amount 6.
   Greedy gives three coins, the optimum is two.
6. **"Fractional versus 0/1 knapsack?"** Fractional is greedy by value density
   and provably optimal, because you can swap in a fraction. 0/1 is DP, because
   you cannot.
7. **"Greedy or DP — how do you decide?"** Try a counterexample for thirty
   seconds. If one exists, DP. If not, state the exchange argument informally and
   commit.
8. **"Complexity of your greedy?"** Almost always `O(n log n)` dominated by the
   sort, with `O(1)` or `O(n)` extra space. If the input arrives sorted, it drops
   to `O(n)`.
9. **"What if the input were a stream?"** Rules that need a global sort cannot
   stream. Rules with a running invariant can: Kadane and Jump Game both work on
   a stream in `O(1)` space, which is a strong point to volunteer.
10. **"What if the data is too big to sort in memory?"** External merge sort
    first, then the single pass — the pass itself needs only constant state, so
    the sort is the only memory pressure. For interval scheduling specifically
    you can also process in end-time order straight off a sorted file.
11. **"Prove Kadane's."** A prefix with a negative sum can only reduce any
    extension, so discarding it is always safe. Equivalently it is DP with the
    state "best subarray ending here".
12. **"Why does Gas Station's reset skip the whole prefix?"** Arriving at any
    intermediate index from the earlier start, you had at least as much fuel as
    starting there fresh. If the earlier start failed by index `i`, so would any
    of them.
13. **"Is Dijkstra greedy?"** Yes — it commits to the smallest tentative distance
    and never revisits. The proof is the same finality argument, and it is
    exactly why negative edges break it.
14. **"Can you always convert a greedy into a DP?"** Yes, and the DP will be
    correct whether or not the greedy was; it just costs more. That is a
    reasonable safety move when you cannot settle the proof under time pressure.
</Callout>

<Callout type="pitfall" title="Things that bite in practice">
- **Committing to a greedy because it passes the two sample cases.** The samples
  are chosen to be readable, not adversarial.
- **Sorting by start when you meant end**, which is a one-character bug that
  produces a plausible wrong number.
- **Subtraction comparators overflowing.**
- **Forgetting that "minimum removals" is "total minus maximum kept"** and
  writing a whole second algorithm.
- **Mishandling touching endpoints** because you never asked whether they count
  as overlapping.
- **Mutating the input array by sorting it** when the caller needed the original
  order. Say it aloud, or copy.
- **Confusing "a heap is involved" with "this is a heap problem".** The heap
  implements the greedy rule; the rule still needs its own justification.
- **Asserting a greedy proof you cannot actually give.** Interviewers push on
  this exact point, and "I would verify it with a DP" is a far better answer than
  a hand-wave.
</Callout>

### Problems to drill

**Easy**

- **Assign Cookies** — two-pointer greedy after sorting both arrays; the
  smallest possible exchange argument.
- **Best Time to Buy and Sell Stock II** — take every upward step, and be able
  to say why summing all the rises is optimal.
- **Lemonade Change** — greedy on which bills to give back, and why the larger
  denomination goes first.

**Medium**

- **Jump Game** and **Jump Game II** — the reachability invariant and the
  level-based minimum.
- **Gas Station** — the reset argument, the most interesting proof in this list.
- **Maximum Subarray** — Kadane's, and the DP framing of it.
- **Non-overlapping Intervals** — interval scheduling restated as removals.
- **Minimum Number of Arrows to Burst Balloons** — the same algorithm again, so
  you can recognise it in disguise.
- **Partition Labels** — greedy over last-occurrence positions, where the sort
  key is not obvious.
- **Task Scheduler** — greedy with a heap, and the closed-form alternative.
- **Hand of Straights** — greedy from the smallest remaining card, with a
  `TreeMap` of counts.
- **Queue Reconstruction by Height** — sorting on two keys, where most people's
  greedy intuition breaks down.

**Hard**

- **Candy** — two passes in opposite directions, because one greedy pass cannot
  satisfy both neighbour constraints.
- **Minimum Number of Refueling Stops** — greedy with a max-heap of skipped
  stations, against the DP alternative.
- **Course Schedule III** — take everything, then greedily drop the longest
  course when a deadline is violated. The "undo" flavour of greedy.
- **IPO** — two heaps implementing a greedy that is correct only because capital
  requirements are monotone.
