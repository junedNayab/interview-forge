## Layer 1 — When you reach for this

A heap is the data structure you choose when you need **the best remaining
element, repeatedly, and never anything else**. That restriction is the whole
point. A sorted array gives you every element in order and charges you
`O(n log n)` for the privilege. A heap gives you only the extreme — smallest or
largest — but hands it over in `O(1)` and repairs itself in `O(log n)`.

> If you find yourself thinking "I'll sort this and then look at the front,"
> stop and ask whether you ever look past the front. **If you only ever need
> the front, you are paying for order you never read, and a heap is the
> structure that charges you for exactly what you use.**

The phrases that should make you reach for a heap:

- **"Top K"**, **"K-th largest"**, **"K closest"**, **"K most frequent"**. Any
  time K is small relative to n, a heap of size K turns `O(n log n)` into
  `O(n log k)`.
- **"From a stream"**, **"as elements arrive"**, **"running median"**,
  **"maintain the current best"**. A heap is inherently online: it accepts new
  elements without reprocessing what it already holds.
- **"Merge k sorted"** anything — lists, arrays, files. The heap holds one
  candidate per source and always knows which source wins next.
- **"Schedule by priority"**, **"process the earliest deadline"**, **"the room
  that frees up soonest"**. Here the heap is literally modelling a priority.
- Dijkstra and Prim. The heap is the frontier; the algorithm is "repeatedly
  take the cheapest unexplored thing."

The anti-signals matter just as much, because a heap is a tempting wrong answer:

- **You need the full sorted order.** Then sort. Popping every element out of a
  heap is `O(n log n)` with worse constants than `Arrays.sort`.
- **You need to search for an arbitrary element, or delete one by value.** A
  heap has no index; `contains` and `remove(Object)` are both `O(n)` on
  `PriorityQueue`. If you need "the best" *and* "find by key", you want a
  `TreeMap`, or a heap paired with a hash map of positions.
- **You need the k-th largest once, offline, with no streaming.** Quickselect
  does that in `O(n)` average. The heap earns its place only when you also want
  the other `k - 1` elements, or when the data arrives over time.
- **K is close to n.** A heap of size `k` where `k` is around `n` is a slow sort.
- **You need range queries or a successor** — "the smallest element greater
  than x". That is a balanced BST. A heap orders parents against children and
  says nothing about siblings.

## Layer 2 — The mechanism

### The invariant

A binary min-heap maintains exactly one property, and it is worth saying out
loud because everything else follows from it:

**Every node is less than or equal to both of its children.**

That is all. Notice what it does *not* say: nothing about the relationship
between siblings, nothing about the relationship between cousins, nothing about
the order within a level. The heap is a *partial* order, and its cheapness
comes from how little it promises. The one thing the invariant does guarantee,
by transitivity along any root-to-node path, is that **the root is the minimum
of the entire structure** — which is precisely the one question a heap answers.

The second structural property is that the tree is **complete**: every level is
full except possibly the last, which fills left to right. This is what keeps
the height at `floor(log2 n)` and what makes the array representation possible.

### The implicit array

A heap needs no pointers and allocates no nodes. It lives in a flat array where
the tree structure is arithmetic:

- node at index `i` has children at `2i + 1` and `2i + 2`
- node at index `i` has parent at `(i - 1) / 2` using integer division

This is a consequence of completeness — with no gaps, level-order traversal and
array order are the same thing. Two benefits follow. There is no per-node object
header, no child references, no allocation per insert, so the footprint is
roughly one machine word per element instead of four or five. And the whole
structure is contiguous, so the upper levels — the ones every operation
touches — stay resident in cache. **The array layout is why a heap beats a
pointer-based balanced tree in practice even when the asymptotics match.**

### Sift-up and sift-down

Two operations restore the invariant after it is locally broken.

**Sift-up** (used by insert): append the new element at the end of the array,
which keeps completeness but may violate the ordering against its parent. Swap
it with its parent while it is smaller. Each swap moves up one level, so this
costs at most the height, `O(log n)`.

**Sift-down** (used by extract-min): the root is removed, so move the last
element into the root slot — completeness is preserved — and then swap it with
its **smaller** child while it is larger than that child. Swapping with the
smaller child is not an arbitrary choice: swapping with the larger one would
place a value above its sibling that is bigger than it, breaking the invariant
on the other branch. Again at most the height, `O(log n)`.

### Why heapify is O(n), not O(n log n)

Building a heap from an unordered array by inserting `n` elements one at a time
costs `O(n log n)`. But Floyd's method — start at the last internal node,
`n/2 - 1`, and sift-down every node walking backwards to the root — is `O(n)`,
and interviewers like this because the naive analysis gives the wrong answer.

The naive analysis says: `n` nodes, each sift-down is `O(log n)`, so
`O(n log n)`. The correction is that **almost no node is at the top.** Sift-down
from a node at height `h` costs `O(h)`, and in a complete tree there are at most
`n / 2^(h+1)` nodes at height `h`. Half the nodes are leaves and cost nothing.
A quarter are at height 1 and cost one swap. So the total is the sum over `h` of
`h * n / 2^(h+1)`, and the series `h / 2^h` converges to 2. The total work is
bounded by `2n`. **The cost is dominated by the cheap nodes because that is
where nearly all the nodes are.**

This is why heapsort's build phase is free, and why constructing a
`PriorityQueue` from a collection is cheaper than offering elements one by one.

### The canonical template

```java
// Top-K largest with a MIN-heap of size k.
// PriorityQueue in Java is a MIN-heap by default.
static int findKthLargest(int[] nums, int k) {
    PriorityQueue<Integer> heap = new PriorityQueue<>();   // min-heap
    for (int n : nums) {
        heap.offer(n);                 // O(log k) — heap never exceeds k+1
        if (heap.size() > k) {
            heap.poll();               // evict the SMALLEST of the k+1 held
        }
    }
    return heap.peek();                // the smallest survivor = k-th largest
}
```

### Why the k-th *largest* uses a *min*-heap

This is the single most counter-intuitive thing in the pattern, and being able
to explain it cleanly is worth more than memorising the code.

You are keeping a running set of "the k best I have seen." When a new element
arrives and the set is full, you must throw one away — and the one to throw away
is **the weakest member of the set**, because it is the one most likely to be
displaced. To find the weakest of your k largest, you need the *minimum* of
those k, so the structure holding them must be a min-heap. Its root is the
current k-th largest, and it is also exactly the element you evict.

A max-heap would give you the largest of your k largest, which is a number you
never need and cannot act on. The mnemonic that survives interview pressure:
**the heap's root is the bouncer at the door — it must be the weakest thing
inside, so you know instantly whether the newcomer deserves to get in.**

Symmetrically, k-th smallest uses a max-heap of size k.

### Cost, and the alternatives

The loop runs `n` times and each heap operation is `O(log k)` because the heap
never holds more than `k + 1` elements. So **`O(n log k)` time and `O(k)`
space**, versus `O(n log n)` and `O(n)` for a full sort. With `k` of 10 and `n`
of a million, `log k` is about 3 against `log n` of 20 — and the space
difference is usually the bigger win, because it is what lets you handle a
stream at all.

The third option is **quickselect**: partition around a pivot as in quicksort,
but recurse into only the side containing position `k`. Expected `O(n)` because
the work halves each time and `n + n/2 + n/4 + ...` is `2n`; worst case `O(n^2)`
on adversarial pivots, fixed by a random pivot. Quickselect wins when the whole
array is in memory and you need the answer once. It loses when the data streams,
when you must not mutate the input, or when you need all k in order.

### Variants of the template

**A comparator instead of natural order.** For "K closest points to origin" you
want to evict the *farthest* of your k closest, so the heap is a max-heap by
distance:

```java
PriorityQueue<int[]> heap =
    new PriorityQueue<>((a, b) -> dist(b) - dist(a));   // max-heap by distance
```

For plain reverse order, `new PriorityQueue<>(Comparator.reverseOrder())` is
clearer than a lambda. When comparing by a numeric field, prefer
`Comparator.comparingInt(...)` over `a - b` subtraction, which overflows if the
values span the integer range.

**Heap of entries for frequency problems.** Top K Frequent Elements is a
`HashMap` count pass followed by a size-k min-heap keyed on the count:
`O(n + m log k)` where `m` is the number of distinct values. Bucket sort is the
`O(n)` follow-up — counts are bounded by `n`, so index an array of lists by
frequency and read it from the back.

**Heap over sources, for merging.** Merge K Sorted Lists seeds the heap with the
head of each list, then repeatedly polls the minimum and pushes that node's
successor. The heap stays at size `k`, so the cost is `O(N log k)` for `N` total
nodes — better than the `O(N k)` of scanning all k heads each time, and better
than concatenate-then-sort because it preserves the work the sorted inputs
already did.

**Two heaps, for a running median.** This is the hard application and it is
asked often. Keep a max-heap `lo` holding the smaller half and a min-heap `hi`
holding the larger half. The invariants are: every element in `lo` is at most
every element in `hi`, and the sizes differ by at most one. Then the median is
either the root of the larger heap or the average of the two roots. The trick in
the insertion is to push through one heap into the other, which enforces the
ordering invariant without any comparison logic:

```java
PriorityQueue<Integer> lo = new PriorityQueue<>(Comparator.reverseOrder()); // low half, max-heap
PriorityQueue<Integer> hi = new PriorityQueue<>();                          // high half, min-heap

void addNum(int num) {
    lo.offer(num);            // always enter through the low side
    hi.offer(lo.poll());      // hand the low side's largest to the high side
    if (hi.size() > lo.size()) lo.offer(hi.poll());   // rebalance, favour lo
}

double findMedian() {
    return lo.size() > hi.size() ? lo.peek() : (lo.peek() + hi.peek()) / 2.0;
}
```

Insert is `O(log n)`, median is `O(1)`. The two-line insert is worth memorising
because deriving it live under pressure is error-prone.

### Worked example: Task Scheduler

Given tasks labelled A–Z and a cooldown `n` between two runs of the same task,
find the minimum number of intervals. The greedy insight is that **you should
always run the task with the most remaining occurrences that is currently
allowed**, because the most frequent task is the bottleneck and every idle slot
you fill with it is a slot you do not waste later. That is a "best remaining"
query, so it is a heap: build a max-heap of counts, then in each round pop up to
`n + 1` distinct tasks, decrement them, and push back the ones that still have
occurrences left. Cost is `O(total * log 26)`, effectively linear.

The adaptation to notice: the heap holds *counts*, not tasks, and the cooldown
is handled by holding popped tasks aside for one full round rather than by
anything inside the heap.

### Worked example: Meeting Rooms II

Given intervals, find the minimum number of rooms. Sort by start time and keep a
min-heap of **end times of the meetings currently running**. For each meeting in
start order, if the earliest-ending running meeting has finished, poll it — that
room is reused — then push the new end time. The answer is the maximum heap size
reached, at `O(n log n)` dominated by the sort.

The adaptation is the choice of what goes in the heap. You are not asked about
end times; you are asked about rooms. But the only question you ever ask of the
running set is "has the earliest one finished yet", and the heap answers that in
`O(1)`. **Choosing what the heap holds is the actual design decision in every
heap problem.** See [Pattern 14 — Intervals](/dsa/pattern-14-intervals) for the
sweep-line alternative that solves the same problem without a heap.

### Edge cases and traps

- **Iteration order is not sorted.** `for (int x : pq)` walks the backing array,
  which is heap order, not sorted order. The same applies to `toString()`,
  `toArray()`, and streaming the queue. Only repeated `poll()` gives you sorted
  output. This silently passes small tests and fails large ones.
- **`remove(Object)` and `contains` are `O(n)`.** There is no lookup structure.
  The standard fix for "decrease-key" style problems, including Dijkstra, is
  **lazy deletion**: push the new entry, and when you poll, skip any entry whose
  recorded value is stale.
- **`peek()` and `poll()` return `null` on an empty queue**, they do not throw.
  With `PriorityQueue<Integer>` that null unboxes into a
  `NullPointerException` somewhere else entirely, which is a nasty debug.
- **Comparator subtraction overflows.** `(a, b) -> a - b` is wrong whenever the
  values can be far apart in sign. Use `Integer.compare(a, b)`.
- **Mutating an element after it is in the heap** silently corrupts the
  invariant, exactly as mutating a `HashMap` key does. Remove, mutate, re-insert.
- **`PriorityQueue` is unbounded and grows**, so "top K" code that forgets the
  eviction line still produces the right answer on small inputs while using
  `O(n)` memory.
- **Ties.** A binary heap is not stable, so equal priorities come out in an
  arbitrary order. If the problem cares, put a sequence number in the comparator
  as a tiebreaker.

## Layer 3 — Interview surface

<Callout type="interview" title="Follow-ups to rehearse out loud">
1. **"Why a min-heap for the k-th largest?"** Because you evict the weakest
   member of your running best-k set, and the weakest of the k largest is their
   minimum. The root is both the current answer and the eviction candidate.
2. **"Complexity of your top-K?"** `O(n log k)` time, `O(k)` space. The heap
   never exceeds `k + 1` elements, so each operation is `log k`, not `log n`.
3. **"Can you do better than `O(n log k)`?"** Quickselect: expected `O(n)`,
   worst case `O(n^2)`, mitigated by a random pivot. It needs the full array in
   memory and it mutates it.
4. **"What if the input is a stream you cannot store?"** That is exactly where
   the heap wins and quickselect cannot compete — `O(k)` memory, and the answer
   is correct after every single element.
5. **"What if k is so large the heap does not fit in memory?"** Then it is not a
   top-K problem any more. Use external merge sort, or an approximate sketch
   such as Count-Min plus a small heap if the query is top-K by frequency.
6. **"Why is heapify `O(n)`?"** Cost is proportional to height, and the node
   count halves as height rises. The sum of `h / 2^h` converges to 2, so the
   total is bounded by `2n`.
7. **"How is a heap stored?"** A flat array, children of `i` at `2i + 1` and
   `2i + 2`. No pointers, no per-node allocation, and the hot upper levels stay
   in cache.
8. **"Why does sift-down swap with the smaller child?"** Swapping with the
   larger one would leave a value above its sibling that exceeds it, breaking
   the invariant on the other branch.
9. **"Heap or balanced BST?"** Heap if you only ever need the extreme; BST if
   you need ordered traversal, range queries, or a successor. A heap says
   nothing about siblings.
10. **"Running median from a stream?"** Two heaps — max-heap for the low half,
    min-heap for the high half. Push through one into the other, then rebalance.
    `O(log n)` insert, `O(1)` query.
11. **"Median with a sliding window instead of a stream?"** The two-heap version
    needs deletion, so either use lazy deletion with a "to remove" count map, or
    switch to a `TreeMap` of counts with a pointer to the middle.
12. **"Merge k sorted lists — why a heap?"** It keeps one candidate per list and
    finds the next output in `log k` instead of scanning all `k` heads.
    `O(N log k)` for `N` total elements.
13. **"How would you implement `PriorityQueue` yourself?"** Array plus sift-up
    on insert and sift-down on extract. Write the parent and child index
    arithmetic out; that is what they are checking.
14. **"Is `PriorityQueue` thread-safe?"** No. `PriorityBlockingQueue` is the
    concurrent version, and it is unbounded, which matters for back-pressure.
</Callout>

<Callout type="pitfall" title="Things that bite in practice">
- **Iterating a `PriorityQueue` and expecting sorted output.** It is heap order.
  Only `poll()` is sorted.
- **Forgetting the eviction line** in the top-K loop. Correct answer, wrong
  complexity, and reviewers miss it too.
- **`a - b` comparators** overflowing on large or mixed-sign values.
- **Using `remove(Object)` inside a loop**, turning an `O(n log n)` solution
  into `O(n^2)`. Use lazy deletion instead.
- **Unboxed `null`** from `poll()` on an empty queue.
- **Building a heap with a loop of `offer`** when you have all the data —
  `new PriorityQueue<>(collection)` heapifies in `O(n)`.
- **Two-heap median where the sizes drift** because you rebalanced in only one
  direction. Push through one heap, then rebalance once; that is the whole
  invariant.
- **Reaching for a heap when you need order statistics repeatedly by rank.**
  That is an indexed tree or a BST with subtree sizes.
</Callout>

### Problems to drill

**Easy**

- **Kth Largest Element in a Stream** — the size-k min-heap in its purest form,
  and it forces you to see the streaming case.
- **Last Stone Weight** — a max-heap with re-insertion; good for getting
  `Comparator.reverseOrder()` into your fingers.

**Medium**

- **Kth Largest Element in an Array** — the canonical one. Solve it with a heap,
  then again with quickselect and be ready to compare.
- **Top K Frequent Elements** — heap over a frequency map, plus the bucket-sort
  `O(n)` follow-up.
- **K Closest Points to Origin** — the heap direction is decided by what you
  evict, not by what the question says.
- **Task Scheduler** — the "most remaining first" greedy and why it is correct.
- **Meeting Rooms II** — heap of end times; compare against the sweep line.
- **Reorganize String** — the same greedy shape with an impossibility condition.
- **Kth Smallest Element in a Sorted Matrix** — heap over rows, and the binary
  search alternative that beats it.

**Hard**

- **Find Median from Data Stream** — the two-heap template; memorise the insert.
- **Merge k Sorted Lists** — heap over sources, against divide-and-conquer
  merging at the same complexity.
- **Sliding Window Median** — two heaps plus deletion, where lazy removal earns
  its keep.
- **IPO** — two heaps together, one on capital and one on profit; a test of
  whether you can choose what each heap holds.
