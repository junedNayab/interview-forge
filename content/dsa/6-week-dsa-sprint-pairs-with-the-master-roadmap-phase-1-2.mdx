## The principle this plan is built on

> **Understanding 75 problems deeply beats skimming 300.** Deeply means you can
> re-solve it from scratch a week later, name the pattern, and explain why the
> approach is correct — not that you recognise it.

That distinction is the whole design of this plan, because recognition is a
trap. Reading a solution produces a strong feeling of understanding that
disappears completely under interview conditions. The only reliable test is
**re-solving from scratch after enough delay that you've forgotten the code**,
which is why spaced repetition is built into every week below rather than left
as a suggestion.

The plan assumes roughly **8–10 hours a week** — about 1.5 hours on weekdays
and a longer weekend session. If you have less, stretch it to eight or ten
weeks rather than cutting the review, because the review is where the learning
actually happens.

| 7+ | Weak patterns, hards, timed mocks | ongoing | ongoing |

Two things about the ordering. **Arrays and hashing come first** because
practically every later pattern uses a hash map inside it. **Dynamic
programming comes last** because it's the hardest to build intuition for and
because several DP problems are easier once you've internalised recursion from
the trees and backtracking weeks.

The review column is not optional. **It is the part that converts practice into
retained skill**, and it's the first thing people cut.

## The weekly rhythm

```
Mon   Read the pattern lesson. Solve 2 easy problems in it.
Tue   3 mediums. Timer on.
Wed   3 mediums. Timer on.
Thu   2 mediums + 1 hard attempt (30 min, then read the solution).
Fri   Light: re-solve 2 problems from earlier weeks, from scratch.
Sat   Longer session: 3 mediums under timed conditions, out loud.
Sun   Off, or write up the week's pattern in your own words.
```

That Sunday write-up is worth more than it looks. **Explaining a pattern in
your own words, in writing, exposes the parts you only think you understand** —
it's the same mechanism as the out-loud test, applied to the pattern rather
than to a single problem.

### How to run one problem

1. **Timer on: 25 minutes for a medium.** Untimed practice trains a skill you
   don't get to use.
2. **Solve it out loud**, alone, as if someone were listening. This is the
   single highest-return habit and almost nobody does it.
3. **If you're stuck at 25 minutes**, take a targeted look — the approach only,
   not the code — and then close it and implement it yourself.
4. **If you're still stuck at 40 minutes**, read the full solution, then
   **close it and reimplement from scratch.** Reading is not practice.
5. **Write one line in your mistake log**: the problem, the pattern, and what
   specifically went wrong.
6. **Add it to the review queue** for one week out.

### The mistake log

One line per problem, in a plain text file:

```
Sep 14  Longest Substring No Repeat  sliding window
        forgot to move left past the previous index, moved by 1
Sep 15  Koko Eating Bananas          binary search on answer
        didn't recognise the answer space was monotone — wasted 15 min
```

Read it before every session. Within two weeks the patterns in **your own**
errors become obvious, and they're far more actionable than a problem count.
Most people find their errors cluster into two or three recurring habits — an
off-by-one in the shrink condition, or failing to spot that the answer space is
searchable — and fixing a habit is worth twenty problems.

## What "done" looks like each week

Don't advance on the calendar. Advance on the check.

| Week | You're done when you can, from scratch |
|---|---|
| 1 | Write the complement-hash-map solution and the prefix-sum-plus-map solution without reference, and explain why the prefix sum works |
| 2 | Write the variable sliding-window template and explain the amortised `O(n)` argument; write all three binary search forms |
| 3 | Reverse a linked list iteratively, explain why Floyd's second phase finds the cycle start, and write all four tree traversals |
| 4 | Write the backtracking choose/explore/un-choose template and explain the duplicate-skip condition; explain why k-th largest uses a min-heap |
| 5 | Write BFS and DFS on a grid, do a topological sort both ways, and state the difference between directed and undirected cycle detection |
| 6 | Take an unfamiliar DP problem and correctly state the state definition and recurrence, even if you can't finish the code |

**If you fail the check, repeat the week.** Advancing on schedule while failing
the check is how people arrive at week six having learned nothing durable.

## Week 7 and beyond

The sprint builds coverage. What follows builds reliability:

- **Timed full mocks.** Two problems in 45 minutes, with another person.
  [Coding-round survival tips](/dsa/coding-round-survival-tips) covers the
  tactics; this is where you practise them under real pressure.
- **Your weakest two patterns**, from the mistake log. Almost always DP and
  graphs.
- **Hard problems**, but only in patterns where your mediums are solid. Hards in
  a weak pattern teach you nothing except that it's weak.
- **Re-solve the full set on a spaced schedule** — anything you got wrong at one
  week, two weeks, one month.

<Callout type="tip" title="The honest signal that you're ready">
Not a problem count. This:

**Pick a random medium in a pattern you covered four weeks ago. Solve it out
loud, on a timer, in a plain editor, without hints, in under 25 minutes —
including stating the complexity and testing it yourself.**

Do that successfully three times in a row across three different patterns and
you are ready for the coding round. If you can't, the number of problems you've
seen is not the missing ingredient.
</Callout>

<Callout type="pitfall" title="How this plan usually fails">
- **Skipping the review column** because new problems feel more productive.
  They aren't; review is where retention comes from.
- **Reading solutions instead of solving.** Reading produces recognition, which
  evaporates under pressure.
- **Practising untimed and silently**, then being surprised by the interview.
- **Chasing a problem count.** 300 skimmed leaves you worse off than 75
  understood, and it takes longer.
- **Advancing on the calendar rather than the check.**
- **Starting with dynamic programming** because it's scary. It's last for a
  reason.
- **Stopping DSA entirely once you move to system design.** Two problems a week
  maintains it; zero loses it in a month.
</Callout>
