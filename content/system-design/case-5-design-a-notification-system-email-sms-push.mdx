## Layer 1 — The mental model

Every other case study in this track is about scaling *your* infrastructure.
This one is different: **the bottleneck and the failure modes both live outside
your system**, in Twilio, SES, APNs and FCM.

That inverts the usual design. You don't optimise your own throughput — you
build machinery to absorb someone else's unreliability:

> Accept the request instantly, durably queue it, and let workers fight the
> providers on their own time.

Which makes the whole shape fall out of one decision:

```
Caller ──► Notification API ──► Queue ──► Workers ──► Provider
        (fast, returns 202)   (durable) (retry,      (slow, flaky,
                                         backoff)     rate-limited)
```

The three things that make this hard, and the three things the interview is
actually about:

1. **You cannot get exactly-once.** A provider can send the SMS and then time
   out before acking. Retrying sends it twice; not retrying may send it zero
   times. Everything about dedupe flows from this.
2. **Not all notifications are equal.** An OTP is worthless after 60 seconds; a
   marketing email is fine tomorrow. One queue for both means the OTP waits.
3. **"Notify all users" is a fan-out problem** hiding inside what looks like a
   simple send.

<Callout type="tip" title="Why this one is worth over-preparing">
Notification systems are asked at every level because they're deceptively
simple — the naive answer ("put it on a queue") is *correct*, which means the
interviewer will spend the whole session on the follow-ups. You can't coast on
the high-level diagram here the way you can on a URL shortener. The depth is the
entire signal.
</Callout>

## Layer 2 — Driving it with R-E-A-H-D-B

### R — Requirements

**Functional:** send notifications over push, SMS and email; respect per-user
per-channel preferences and opt-outs; support templates; no duplicate sends;
track delivery status.

**Non-functional:**

- **Reliable** — a queued notification is never silently dropped.
- **Low latency for the notifications that need it.** Say this precisely:
  transactional (OTP, password reset) under a few seconds; marketing can take
  minutes.
- High throughput, very bursty (a product launch email is 10M sends in one
  request).
- **Out of scope:** in-app notification inbox UI, the analytics pipeline, the
  content/marketing tooling.

The single most valuable clarifying question here: **"Are we treating
transactional and promotional notifications the same?"** Asking it shows you
already know prioritization is the core problem, and it buys you the constraint
you need later.

### E — Estimate

```
Users:     10M
Push:      10M × 5/day  = 50M/day  ≈  580/sec   (peak ~3,000/sec)
Email:     10M × 2/day  = 20M/day  ≈  230/sec
SMS:       10M × 0.1/day = 1M/day  ≈   12/sec   (expensive! ~$0.01 each)
                                          → $10K/day if unchecked
Burst:     one marketing campaign = 10M sends in minutes
```

Two conclusions worth saying out loud, because they shape the design:

- **Steady-state throughput is unremarkable; the bursts are the problem.** A
  system sized for 580/sec dies on a campaign. That argues for a queue that
  absorbs the burst and workers that drain at a *controlled* rate — the queue is
  a shock absorber, not just a decoupler.
- **SMS costs real money.** Notification systems are one of the few designs
  where cost is a first-class constraint, and mentioning it separates you from
  candidates reciting a template. It also justifies budget caps and a
  cheapest-channel-first fallback.

### A — API

```
POST /v1/notifications
  Idempotency-Key: <uuid>          ← caller-supplied, see below
  {
    "userId": "u_123",
    "type": "ORDER_SHIPPED",        ← not a channel: the event
    "templateData": { "orderId": "A-42", "eta": "Friday" },
    "priority": "transactional" | "promotional"
  }
  → 202 Accepted { "notificationId": "n_789" }

GET /v1/notifications/{id}          → per-channel delivery status
POST /v1/users/{id}/preferences     → channel opt-in/out, quiet hours
```

Three deliberate choices to defend:

**`202 Accepted`, not `200 OK`.** You have accepted responsibility for the
notification; you have not sent it. Returning 200 would be a lie, and it also
means the caller blocks on Twilio's latency. This is the API expressing the
async design.

**The caller names an event type, not a channel.** `ORDER_SHIPPED`, not
`send_email`. The *system* decides the channels from user preferences. If
callers pick channels, every service has to know about preferences and opt-outs,
and GDPR/TCPA compliance leaks across your whole codebase.

**The caller supplies an idempotency key.** The order service may retry its
HTTP call after a timeout; without a key you send two "your order shipped"
emails. This is the same
[idempotency key](/system-design/15-microservice-reliability-patterns) pattern as
everywhere else, applied at the front door.

### H — High-level design

```
                        ┌────────────────────────────────────────┐
 Order svc ─┐           │  preferences  │  templates  │ dedupe   │
 Auth svc  ─┼─► API ───►│         (Postgres / Redis)             │
 Billing   ─┘   │       └────────────────────────────────────────┘
                │ 202
                ▼
        ┌───────────────────────────────────────┐
        │  Kafka  (partitioned by userId)       │
        │  ├── topic: transactional   (drained first)
        │  └── topic: promotional               │
        └───────┬───────────┬───────────┬───────┘
                ▼           ▼           ▼
           Push wkrs    SMS wkrs    Email wkrs      ← independent scaling
                │           │           │            + per-provider rate limits
                ▼           ▼           ▼
            APNs/FCM     Twilio       SES           ← retry, circuit-break,
                │           │           │              fail over
                └───────────┴───────────┴──► delivery-status events ──► DB
                                                   + dead-letter queue
```

**The API does almost nothing.** Validate, assign an ID, check the idempotency
key, write to the queue, return 202. Every slow or failure-prone operation is
behind the queue. If the API starts resolving preferences and rendering
templates synchronously, its latency becomes the sum of its dependencies.

**Separate topics per priority, not one queue with a priority field.** This is
the detail interviewers wait for. With a single queue, 10 million campaign
messages sit ahead of the OTP, and a priority field doesn't help because
consumers read in order. Separate topics with separate worker pools (or strictly
weighted draining) mean the OTP path is physically isolated from the campaign.
**A shared queue means your OTP is as slow as your slowest bulk job.**

**Separate workers per channel** because the channels have nothing in common
operationally: email is high-volume and cheap, SMS is low-volume and expensive,
push has two entirely different provider APIs. Independent pools let you scale
and rate-limit each against its provider's quota, and a Twilio outage doesn't
back up email.

### D — Storage

| Data | Store | Why |
|---|---|---|
| User preferences | Postgres, cached in Redis | Small, relational, read on every send |
| Templates | Postgres / object storage | Versioned, rarely written |
| Dedupe keys | Redis, TTL 24–48 h | `SET NX` is the whole mechanism |
| Delivery status | Cassandra | High write volume, queried by notification or user |
| In-app inbox | Postgres / Cassandra | Read by user, paginated |

The interesting one is **delivery status**, because the volume is larger than
the notification volume: every send produces `queued → sent → delivered →
opened/clicked`, and providers post those back asynchronously via webhooks.
Four-plus writes per notification, append-only, queried by ID — the same
argument for a wide-column store as in
[the chat system](/system-design/case-4-design-a-chat-system-whatsapp-messenger).

### Deduplication — the honest version

Say this plainly, because the honest answer scores better than a confident wrong
one: **you cannot achieve exactly-once delivery through a third party.** The
provider can dispatch the SMS and then fail to ack. You get at-least-once and
you shrink the duplicate window.

Two layers:

```
1. At the API      — Idempotency-Key from the caller
                     SET notif:idem:{key} = notificationId NX EX 86400
                     Already exists? Return the original notificationId.

2. At the worker   — before calling the provider
                     SET notif:sent:{notificationId}:{channel} NX EX 172800
                     Already exists? Skip. Another worker has it.
```

Then the residual race, which you should volunteer rather than wait to be
caught on: a worker sets the key, calls the provider, and crashes before
recording the result. Did the SMS go out? You genuinely don't know. Your choices
are to retry (risking a duplicate) or not (risking a miss), **and the right
answer depends on the notification type** — retry an OTP, don't retry a
"payment received" receipt. Naming that trade-off instead of pretending it
doesn't exist is the strongest thing you can do in this section.

A third, product-level layer is worth a sentence: **collapse and digest.** Ten
"someone liked your post" events in a minute should become one notification, not
ten. That's a different kind of deduplication — semantic, not technical — and
mentioning it shows product judgement.

### Fighting the providers

This is where the design earns its keep, and it's
[reliability patterns](/system-design/15-microservice-reliability-patterns)
applied end to end:

- **Retry with exponential backoff and jitter** — and only on retryable errors.
  A 500 or a 429 is retryable; "invalid phone number" is not, and retrying it
  burns quota forever.
- **Respect `Retry-After`.** Providers tell you when to come back. Ignoring it
  gets you rate-limited harder.
- **Circuit breaker per provider.** When Twilio starts failing, stop calling it
  — every request during an outage is a thread blocked for a 30-second timeout,
  and that's how one dead provider consumes your entire worker pool.
- **Provider failover.** Two SMS vendors; when the breaker on the primary opens,
  route to the secondary. This is worth building precisely because you don't
  control provider uptime, and it's the answer to "what if Twilio goes down?"
- **A dead-letter queue** after N attempts, with alerting and a replay tool.
  A DLQ nobody looks at is just a slower way to drop messages, so say that the
  DLQ has an owner and a dashboard.
- **Rate-limit yourself** to each provider's quota with a
  [token bucket](/system-design/case-2-design-a-distributed-rate-limiter) shared
  across workers in Redis. Getting throttled by the provider is worse than
  pacing yourself.

<Callout type="pitfall" title="Timeouts are the failure that takes the system down">
The naive worker calls Twilio with the client's default timeout — often none, or
60 seconds. During a provider slowdown, every worker thread is parked waiting,
the pool is exhausted, and the queue backs up. **Nothing has errored**, so your
error-rate alerts stay green while the system is effectively dead.

Always set an aggressive timeout (a few seconds), always pair it with a circuit
breaker, and alert on **queue depth and consumer lag**, not just error rate.
Queue depth is the metric that catches this class of failure, and naming it is a
strong operational signal.
</Callout>

### Fan-out: "notify all 10 million users"

The trap is doing it synchronously in the request. The fix is a two-stage
fan-out, which mirrors [the news feed](/system-design/case-3-design-a-news-feed-twitter-instagram-timeline):

```
POST /campaigns  ──► one "campaign" record, return 202 immediately
                          │
                     Fan-out workers: page through the audience
                     in batches of ~1,000, filtering by preference
                          │
                     ──► enqueue individual notification jobs
                          (promotional topic, drained at a paced rate)
```

Details that matter: make the fan-out **resumable** (checkpoint the last user ID
per batch, so a crashed fan-out worker doesn't restart from zero or duplicate a
million sends), **pace the drain** so you don't blow your provider quota or
melt your own database with 10M preference lookups, and **filter before
enqueueing** rather than enqueueing 10M jobs that mostly get discarded.

### Preferences, quiet hours, and the compliance layer

Easy to skip, and skipping it is a real-world red flag:

- **Per-channel, per-type preferences** — a user can want push for orders and
  nothing for marketing.
- **Quiet hours in the user's timezone.** An OTP ignores quiet hours; marketing
  respects them. Scheduling into the next allowed window is a small feature with
  real product value.
- **Global opt-out is legally binding.** CAN-SPAM, GDPR and TCPA make an ignored
  unsubscribe a fine, not a bug. This check belongs at the worker, immediately
  before the send, so it catches a message that was queued before the user opted
  out.
- **Frequency caps** — at most N promotional notifications per user per day.
  This protects the one resource you can't buy more of: the user's willingness
  to leave notifications enabled.

### B — Bottlenecks and failure modes

- **Provider outage** → circuit breaker plus failover to a secondary vendor;
  degrade to a cheaper or slower channel rather than failing.
- **Provider rate limits** → self-throttle with a shared token bucket; honour
  `Retry-After`.
- **Queue backlog** → alert on consumer lag; autoscale channel workers
  independently; shed or defer promotional traffic first.
- **Poison message** kills a worker repeatedly → cap retries, DLQ it, keep
  moving.
- **Preference DB hot on fan-out** → cache in Redis, and batch the lookups
  during campaigns.
- **Cost blowout** → per-tenant budget caps and anomaly alerts. A bug that loops
  on SMS is a five-figure incident, not a page of stack trace.
- **Thundering herd of retries** — everything fails at once during an outage and
  then retries in lockstep when it recovers. Jitter, always.

## Layer 3 — Interview surface

<Callout type="interview" title="Follow-ups to rehearse out loud">
1. **"Why a queue?"** Providers are slow and unreliable; the queue decouples the
   caller from them and absorbs bursts. The API returns 202 in milliseconds.
2. **"How do you stop an OTP queuing behind 10M marketing emails?"** Separate
   topics and separate worker pools per priority. A priority *field* on one
   queue does not work, because consumers read in order.
3. **"How do you prevent duplicates?"** Caller idempotency key at the API, a
   Redis `SET NX` guard per notification-and-channel at the worker — and the
   honest admission that exactly-once is impossible through a third party.
4. **"A worker crashes after calling Twilio but before recording it?"** You
   can't know whether it sent. Retry for an OTP, don't for a receipt. The
   trade-off is the answer.
5. **"What if Twilio is down?"** Circuit breaker opens, fail over to a secondary
   SMS provider, DLQ what still fails. Aggressive timeouts so workers aren't
   parked.
6. **"How do you send to all 10 million users?"** Two-stage fan-out: accept the
   campaign, then resumable batch workers enqueue individual jobs at a paced
   rate, filtering by preference first.
7. **"Which errors do you retry?"** 5xx and 429 yes; 4xx validation errors no.
   Retrying an invalid phone number burns quota forever.
8. **"How do you track delivery?"** Provider webhooks post status back;
   append-only status events in Cassandra keyed by notification ID.
9. **"How do you know the system is unhealthy?"** Queue depth and consumer lag,
   not error rate — the dangerous failure is everything *slow*, not everything
   erroring.
10. **"How do you respect user preferences?"** Per-channel, per-type prefs plus
    quiet hours and frequency caps, checked at the worker just before sending so
    a late opt-out still wins.
11. **"Why does the caller send an event type instead of a channel?"** So
    preferences and compliance live in one service instead of leaking into every
    caller.
12. **"How do you control cost?"** SMS is ~$0.01 a message. Budget caps, anomaly
    alerts, prefer push over SMS where the user allows it.
13. **"Scheduled and recurring notifications?"** A scheduler writes due jobs into
    the same queue; the send path doesn't change.
</Callout>

<Callout type="pitfall" title="Things that bite in practice">
- **One queue for everything.** Your OTP is now as slow as your slowest bulk
  job.
- **No timeout on the provider call.** Workers park, queue backs up, error rate
  stays green.
- **Retrying non-retryable errors.** Infinite retries on a bad phone number.
- **Claiming exactly-once.** You can't have it through a third party; say so and
  describe the window instead.
- **Checking opt-out only at enqueue time.** A message queued before the
  unsubscribe still goes out — and that's the one that becomes a complaint.
- **Non-resumable fan-out.** A crash mid-campaign either skips millions of users
  or re-sends to them.
- **A dead-letter queue with no owner.** A slower way to drop messages.
</Callout>
