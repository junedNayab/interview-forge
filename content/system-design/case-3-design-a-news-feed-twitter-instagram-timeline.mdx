## Layer 1 — The mental model

A feed is **a merge of sorted lists.** You follow 200 people, each has a
chronological list of posts, and your timeline is those lists merged by time and
truncated to the newest 20.

That's the whole problem. The only real question is **when you do the merge:**

- **At read time** ("fan-out on read", pull) — query your 200 followees when you
  open the app, merge, return. Writes are trivial, reads are expensive.
- **At write time** ("fan-out on write", push) — when someone posts, immediately
  append that post into the precomputed timeline of every one of their followers.
  Reads become a single lookup, writes become expensive.

Everything else in this design — the storage, the queues, the caches — follows
from that one choice. And the answer is neither, because each breaks at a
different extreme. Getting to *why* the hybrid exists, rather than just naming
it, is the whole interview.

```
Fan-out on READ                        Fan-out on WRITE
 you open app                           alice posts
      │                                      │
      ├─► alice's posts ─┐              ┌────┴────┬─────────┐
      ├─► bob's posts   ─┼─► merge      ▼         ▼         ▼
      ├─► carol's ...   ─┘              bob's   carol's   dave's
      └─► (×200)                        timeline timeline timeline
  expensive read, cheap write          cheap read, expensive write
```

## Layer 2 — Driving it with R-E-A-H-D-B

### R — Requirements

**Functional:** post; view a feed of people you follow, newest-first; follow and
unfollow.

**Non-functional:**

- **Extremely read-heavy.** Feed loads vastly outnumber posts.
- Feed load under about 200 ms at p99.
- **Eventual consistency is acceptable** — a post showing up a few seconds late
  is invisible to users. Say this explicitly; it licenses the entire
  asynchronous design that follows.
- **Except for your own posts**, which must appear immediately. That's a
  read-your-own-writes requirement, and it's the one consistency exception worth
  calling out early.
- Highly skewed follower distribution: most accounts have a handful of
  followers, a few have tens of millions. This skew *is* the design problem.

**Out of scope:** ML ranking internals, ads, direct messages, search, notifications,
trending topics. Name these — the feature list here is bottomless and scoping is
being scored.

### E — Estimate

```
Users:      100M DAU
Feed loads: 100M × 10/day  = 1e9/day   ÷ 86,400 ≈ 11,600 reads/sec  (peak ~35K)
Posts:      20M/day                    ÷ 86,400 ≈    230 writes/sec (peak ~700)

Fan-out writes (the number that actually matters):
            20M posts × 200 avg followers = 4e9 timeline writes/day
                                          ÷ 86,400 ≈ 46,000 writes/sec
```

**The conclusion to draw out loud:** the post rate is trivial — 230/sec is
nothing. But fan-out multiplies it by the average follower count, so the *real*
write load is 46,000/sec, two orders of magnitude larger than the user-visible
write rate. **Fan-out amplification is the defining cost of this system**, and
naming that is the single most valuable sentence in the estimate phase.

Storage for timelines:

```
Keep ~800 post IDs per timeline × 8 bytes = ~6.4 KB/user
× 20M genuinely active users ≈ 128 GB      → a modest Redis cluster
```

Note we store **IDs, not content** — more on why in a moment.

### A — API

```
POST /v1/posts          { text, mediaIds[] }    → 201 { postId }
GET  /v1/feed?cursor=<opaque>&limit=20          → 200 { items[], nextCursor }
POST /v1/users/{id}/follow                      → 204
```

**Cursor-based pagination, not offset** — and here the reason is sharper than
usual. The feed is a *moving target*: new posts arrive while the user scrolls.
With `offset=20`, three new posts arriving means items shift down and the user
sees three duplicates. The cursor encodes "everything older than
`(timestamp, postId)`", which is stable regardless of what arrives.

### The fan-out decision

**Option 1 — Fan-out on read (pull).**

On feed load: look up who you follow, query recent posts from each, merge,
truncate. Posting is one insert.

Costs: 200 lookups plus a merge per feed load. At 11,600 feed loads/sec that's
**2.3 million post-list lookups per second.** Latency is bounded by the slowest
of 200 queries, so your p99 is a fan-out tail-latency problem. Not viable as the
primary path.

It does have real virtues, which is why it survives inside the hybrid: no write
amplification, no storage duplication, and no stale precomputed state to
maintain.

**Option 2 — Fan-out on write (push).**

On post: append the post ID to each follower's timeline list. On read: one
lookup.

Reads become trivially cheap and cacheable. The cost is write amplification
equal to your follower count, and it fails in two distinct ways:

- **The fan-out storm.** An account with 100 million followers generates 100
  million timeline writes from one post. That's not just expensive, it's bursty —
  and if several large accounts post simultaneously you have a queue backlog
  measured in minutes.
- **Latency skew.** Those 100M writes take time, so the first follower sees the
  post in milliseconds and the last sees it minutes later. For a live event,
  that's a visibly broken product.

There's a third, quieter problem: **wasted work.** Most accounts are dormant. If
70% of your registered users haven't opened the app in a month, 70% of your
fan-out writes are building timelines nobody will read.

**Option 3 — Hybrid. This is the answer.**

Pick a follower threshold (Twitter historically used a value in the tens of
thousands; take ~100K as a defensible number):

- **Below the threshold** — fan out on write. Covers virtually every account, so
  virtually every feed read is a single lookup.
- **Above the threshold** — do *not* fan out. Mark the account as "celebrity" and
  fetch their posts at read time.
- **At read time** — take the precomputed timeline, pull recent posts from the
  handful of celebrities you follow, merge the two, truncate.

The merge is cheap because you follow few celebrities, and each celebrity's
recent-posts list is hot in cache (every one of their followers reads the same
list, so the hit rate is essentially 100%).

<Callout type="interview" title="Two refinements that separate a good answer from a great one">
**1. Don't fan out to inactive users.** Skip anyone who hasn't opened the app in
30 days and rebuild their timeline on demand at next login. Given typical
dormancy this removes the majority of all fan-out writes — often a bigger saving
than the celebrity optimisation, and almost nobody mentions it.

**2. Store post IDs, not post content.** If timelines held content, one celebrity
post would be duplicated 100 million times, and an edit would require rewriting
100 million copies. Timelines hold 8-byte IDs; a **hydration** step then batch-fetches
the actual posts from a cache keyed by post ID.

Hydration is what makes the whole design work, and it's the step candidates
forget:

```
GET /feed
  1. LRANGE timeline:{userId} 0 19        → 20 post IDs
  2. MGET post:{id} × 20                  → content (one round trip)
  3. merge in celebrity posts, re-sort
  4. filter: deleted, blocked, muted
  5. return
```

Because every follower of a post hydrates the *same* cache entry, the post cache
hit ratio is extremely high. One copy of the content, read many times.
</Callout>

### D — Data model

**Posts** — sharded by `post_id`. Append-only, immutable-ish, and the hot access
pattern is a primary-key multi-get, so this shards trivially.

**The social graph** — and here's a detail worth raising, because it catches
people: you need the edge indexed **in both directions.**

```sql
follows (follower_id, followee_id, created_at)
  -- "who do I follow?"    → index/shard on follower_id   (read path, merge step)
  -- "who follows alice?"  → index/shard on followee_id   (write path, fan-out)
```

Fan-out needs "who follows alice?" and the read-time merge needs "who does bob
follow?". One index can't serve both efficiently at scale, so you maintain two —
which means the follow-write is itself a dual write, and
[the outbox pattern](/system-design/15-microservice-reliability-patterns) applies.

**Timelines** — a Redis sorted set per user, scored by timestamp, **capped at
~800 entries** (`ZADD` then `ZREMRANGEBYRANK`). The cap matters: it bounds memory
per user, and 800 entries is far more than anyone scrolls. Requests past the cap
fall back to fan-out-on-read, which is rare enough to be acceptable.

### H — High-level design

```
WRITE PATH
  Client → Post service → posts DB
                            │
                            └─→ Kafka "posts" topic
                                    │
                          Fan-out workers
                                    │  (skip if celebrity or inactive)
                                    ▼
                            Redis timelines (ZADD, capped)

READ PATH
  Client → Feed service ─→ Redis timeline (ZREVRANGE)          ─┐
                        ├─→ celebrity recent-posts (cached)     ├─ merge
                        └─→ post cache MGET (hydration)        ─┘
                                    │
                                    └─→ filter → 20 items + cursor
```

Media never touches this path: uploads go to object storage and are served by
CDN, with the feed carrying only URLs.

**Why a queue between posting and fan-out** is the key structural point. The user
gets a `201` as soon as the post is durable; the 46,000/sec of timeline writes
happen asynchronously. Without it, posting latency would depend on follower
count, which is absurd — a popular account would take minutes to post.

Since [Kafka gives at-least-once](/system-design/14-kafka-and-event-streaming),
fan-out workers must be idempotent. `ZADD` with the post ID as member is
naturally idempotent — a replayed event overwrites the same member rather than
duplicating it. That's a satisfying answer to give.

### The follow-ups that decide the interview

**"Your own post doesn't appear immediately. Fix it."**

Fan-out is asynchronous, so there's a window where you've posted and your feed
doesn't show it. Users read this as data loss. Two fixes, and offering both is
better than one: write to *your own* timeline synchronously in the request path
(one extra write, trivially cheap), or merge your own recent posts at read time
the same way celebrity posts are merged. This is
[read-your-own-writes](/system-design/7-cap-theorem-consistency-models) from the
consistency ladder, applied.

**"A user deletes a post. It's in 100 million timelines."**

Do **not** try to remove it from 100 million timelines. Leave the stale ID and
**filter at hydration time** — the post lookup returns a tombstone or nothing, and
the feed service drops it. Same mechanism handles blocks, mutes, and
account suspensions. Accepting cheap stale pointers plus a filter, rather than an
expensive purge, is exactly the kind of tradeoff being probed for. (The
consequence to acknowledge: a feed page can return 18 items instead of 20, so
over-fetch slightly and top up.)

**"Someone with 50 million followers unfollows... I mean, someone follows a
celebrity. What happens to their timeline?"**

Nothing immediately — a new follow doesn't retroactively backfill. Either
backfill asynchronously from the followee's recent posts, or rely on the
read-time merge to include them going forward. Worth stating that a brand-new
account's timeline is empty until either backfill or a recommendation source
fills it (the cold-start problem).

**"Chronological or ranked?"**

This changes the architecture, which is why it's a good question. A ranked feed
can't be a single precomputed list, because the score depends on the viewer, on
recency, and on a model that changes. The standard shape is **two-stage
retrieval**:

1. **Candidate generation** — cheap, high-recall. Pull a few hundred candidates
   from the precomputed timeline plus other sources (followed accounts, popular
   in your network, recommendations).
2. **Ranking** — expensive, high-precision. Score those few hundred with the
   model and return the top 20.

The precomputed timeline doesn't disappear; it becomes a candidate source. That's
the answer that shows you understand why both layers exist.

### B — Bottlenecks and failure modes

- **Fan-out backlog is the thing that pages you.** A burst of large-account posts
  builds queue lag, and timelines go stale. Mitigate with partitioning by
  followee, priority lanes (small accounts complete fast and shouldn't queue
  behind a celebrity's 100M writes), and lag-based alerting measured in *time*,
  not message count.
- **Hot celebrity post.** One post ID read by tens of millions
  simultaneously — a single hot cache key that
  [consistent hashing can't fix](/system-design/13-consistent-hashing). Replicate
  the key across nodes and add a short-TTL local cache in each feed service
  instance.
- **Redis is the timeline store, so its durability matters.** Losing a node loses
  those timelines. Acceptable, because they're **derived data** — rebuild by
  fan-out-on-read from the posts database. Saying "this is a cache of a
  computation, not a system of record" is the right framing.
- **Thundering herd after a deploy or cache flush.** Millions of cold timelines
  rebuilding at once. Mitigate with request coalescing (single-flight per key)
  and staggered warming.
- **The graph service is a SPOF for both paths.** Replicate it, and cache
  follower lists aggressively since they change slowly.

## Layer 3 — Interview surface

<Callout type="interview" title="Follow-ups to rehearse out loud">
1. **"Push or pull?"** Hybrid: push below a follower threshold, pull for
   celebrities, merge at read. Then explain what breaks at each extreme.
2. **"What's the real write load?"** Not 230 posts/sec — 46,000 timeline
   writes/sec after fan-out amplification. Posts × average followers.
3. **"Why does pure push fail?"** Fan-out storm (100M writes per celebrity post)
   and latency skew (last follower sees it minutes later).
4. **"Why does pure pull fail?"** ~200 lookups per feed load, 2.3M lookups/sec,
   and p99 hostage to the slowest of 200 queries.
5. **"What do you store in a timeline?"** Post IDs only, capped around 800.
   Content lives once in a post cache and is hydrated on read.
6. **"How do you avoid wasted fan-out?"** Skip users inactive for 30 days;
   rebuild on demand at login. Usually the largest single saving.
7. **"Why the queue?"** So posting latency is independent of follower count.
8. **"Fan-out workers get a duplicate event."** `ZADD` keyed by post ID is
   idempotent — the replay overwrites the same member.
9. **"Your own post is missing from your feed."** Read-your-own-writes: write
   your own timeline synchronously, or merge your recent posts at read time.
10. **"A post is deleted — how do you purge 100M timelines?"** You don't. Leave
    the ID, filter at hydration, over-fetch to top up.
11. **"Chronological vs ranked?"** Ranked needs two-stage retrieval: cheap
    candidate generation, then expensive scoring of a few hundred candidates.
12. **"Redis loses a node with a million timelines."** They're derived data —
    rebuild by fan-out-on-read from the posts store.
13. **"Why cursors, not offsets?"** The feed shifts under the user as posts
    arrive; offsets produce duplicates and skips.
14. **"How do you shard the graph?"** Both directions: by `follower_id` for the
    read-time merge, by `followee_id` for fan-out. Two indexes, one dual write.
</Callout>

<Callout type="pitfall" title="Things that bite in practice">
- **Storing post content in timelines.** Turns one celebrity post into 100M
  copies and makes edits impossible. IDs plus hydration, always.
- **Unbounded timelines.** Without a cap, a user following active accounts for
  years accumulates millions of entries. `ZREMRANGEBYRANK` on every write.
- **Treating the timeline store as a system of record.** It's a materialised
  view. If you can't rebuild it from the posts database, you've designed a
  liability.
- **One fan-out queue for all accounts.** A celebrity's 100M writes block
  everyone else's posts behind them. Partition, and give small accounts a fast
  lane.
- **Alerting on queue depth in messages.** 10M messages of lag is meaningless
  without throughput; alert on lag ÷ consumption rate.
- **Forgetting the unfollow path.** Unfollowing doesn't purge their posts from
  your timeline; you filter at hydration, same as deletes.
</Callout>
