## Layer 1 — The mental model

**There is no correct answer, and the interviewer is not looking for one.**

They have 45 minutes and a scorecard. They are trying to answer one question:
*would I trust this person to own an ambiguous system at my company?* The design
is just the medium. What they record is whether you scoped the problem, justified
your choices with numbers, noticed your own bottlenecks, and could go deep when
pushed.

This reframes everything. A "simpler" design that you drive confidently and
critique honestly outscores a Kafka-and-Kubernetes diagram you can't defend.
Which leads to the single most common failure:

<Callout type="pitfall" title="The #1 mistake">
Jumping straight to boxes. A candidate hears "design Twitter", says "so we'll
have a microservices architecture with an API gateway, Kafka, Cassandra and a
CDN" — and has now failed, in minute two, before drawing anything.

Why it fails: complexity with no stated justification reads as pattern-matching
rather than engineering. The interviewer can't tell whether you understand the
components or just know their names, and the follow-up ("why Cassandra?") has no
good answer because you never established a requirement it satisfies.

**Start with a monolith and one database. Add each component only when you have
just named the bottleneck it solves.** Every addition should sound like: *"At
12,000 reads per second this database is the constraint, so I'll put a cache in
front of it — and here's what happens on a cache miss."*
</Callout>

**R-E-A-H-D-B.** Memorise it. When you're nervous and your mind goes blank, the
mnemonic is the thing that keeps you moving.

```
1. REQUIREMENTS   (5 min)  functional, non-functional, and what's OUT of scope
2. ESTIMATE       (5 min)  DAU, read/write QPS, storage/year, bandwidth
3. API            (5 min)  the handful of endpoints
4. HIGH-LEVEL     (10 min) client → LB → service → cache → DB, and the data flow
5. DEEP DIVE      (15 min) they pick 1–2; this is where your level is decided
6. BOTTLENECKS    (5 min)  SPOFs, hot spots, what you'd do next
```

## Layer 2 — Running each phase

### 1. Requirements (5 min)

Open by asking questions, not by answering. Three or four good ones, then
summarise your understanding and **get explicit agreement before moving on.**

The questions that always earn their keep:

- "Who are the users, and how many?"
- "Which two or three features are we designing? Should I treat the rest as out
  of scope?"
- "Is this read-heavy or write-heavy?"
- "Does this need to be strongly consistent, or is eventual fine for some parts?"
- "Global or single-region?"

Then split what you heard:

**Functional** — what it *does*, limited to two or three core features. "Post a
tweet, view a timeline, follow a user." Resist listing ten; you cannot design ten
in 45 minutes and trying to is a scoping failure.

**Non-functional** — the properties that actually shape the architecture: scale,
latency target, availability, consistency, durability. These are the constraints
you'll cite later when justifying every component.

**Out of scope — say it out loud.** "I'm not going to design the ML ranking model
or the ads system; I'll assume ranking is a service I call." This is not
dodging. It is the single clearest signal of someone who has shipped under a
deadline, and it protects your remaining 40 minutes.

Finish the phase by restating: *"So: 100 million daily users, read-heavy at
roughly 100 to 1, timelines can be a few seconds stale, tweet creation must be
durable. Sound right?"* If you misheard something, you've just saved the
interview.

### 2. Estimate (5 min)

Do the arithmetic **out loud and visibly**, and round aggressively. Nobody cares
whether it's 38 or 42 writes per second; they care whether you can turn "100
million users" into "this fits on one machine" or "this needs 200 machines."

The pattern:

```
DAU × actions/user/day ÷ 86,400         = average QPS
average QPS × 2–3                       = peak QPS
QPS × bytes/record × 86,400 × 365       = storage/year
read QPS × response size                = egress bandwidth
```

Two shortcuts worth memorising: **86,400 seconds/day ≈ 10^5**, and 1 million
requests/day ≈ 12 QPS. See
[back-of-the-envelope estimation](/system-design/2-back-of-the-envelope-estimation-numbers-to-memorize)
for the full number set.

**The estimate is worthless unless you draw a conclusion from it.** This is the
step candidates skip, and it's most of the value:

- "40 writes per second — that's one Postgres instance, so I won't shard."
- "600 GB per year — a single node holds years of data. Sharding would be
  premature."
- "12,000 reads per second against a 2 GB hot set — that fits in memory, so a
  cache should take almost all of it."

Each of those sentences pre-justifies a later decision. When you then say "no
sharding," it lands as a judgement rather than an omission.

### 3. API design (5 min)

Five endpoints at most, with the shape of the request and response:

```
POST /v1/tweets        { text, mediaIds[] }         → 201 { tweetId }
GET  /v1/feed?cursor=  &limit=20                    → 200 { items[], nextCursor }
POST /v1/users/{id}/follow                          → 204
```

This phase is short but disciplines the design: it forces you to decide what the
client actually needs, which determines your data model. Two details that reliably
earn credit — **cursor-based pagination** rather than offset (offset drifts and
gets slower the deeper you go), and **idempotency** for anything that creates or
charges.

### 4. High-level design (10 min)

Draw the skeleton, narrate the data flow, keep it boring:

```
Client → CDN → Load Balancer → API service ─→ Cache ─→ Database
                                    │                      │
                                    └──→ Queue → Workers ──┘
```

Then walk one request end to end, out loud: *"A read comes in, hits the load
balancer, the service checks Redis, on a miss it reads the primary, populates the
cache, returns. A write goes to the database and publishes an event; the worker
does the fan-out asynchronously."*

Tracing a concrete request is what makes a diagram a design. And when you reach
for a component, attach the justification in the same breath — the cache exists
because of the read QPS you calculated, the queue exists because fan-out is slow
and the user shouldn't wait for it.

### 5. Deep dive (15 min)

**This is the longest phase and the one that sets your level.** The interviewer
picks one or two areas and drills. Typically: the data model, sharding strategy,
the caching layer, a consistency question, or "what's your bottleneck?"

How to handle it well:

- **Offer a menu.** *"I could go deeper on the fan-out strategy, the sharding
  scheme, or how I'd keep the cache coherent — which is most useful?"* You look
  organised and you steer toward ground you know.
- **Give the tradeoff, not the answer.** "Fan-out on write makes reads trivial
  but is brutal for celebrity accounts; fan-out on read is the reverse. I'd do
  hybrid: precompute for normal users, read-time merge for the few thousand
  accounts above a follower threshold." Two options plus a decision plus a reason
  is the shape of every good answer.
- **Go one level below the buzzword.** Saying "I'd use a cache" is table stakes.
  The level shows in: which eviction policy, what TTL, cache-aside or
  write-through, what happens on a miss stampede, how you handle a hot key.
- **Numbers over adjectives.** Not "that would be fast" but "that's one memory
  lookup, so sub-millisecond, versus a cross-region round trip at 80."

<Callout type="tip" title="When you don't know, say so — then reason">
"I haven't worked with that directly" followed by real reasoning scores *far*
better than confident invention, because interviewers catch invention almost
every time and it costs you trust for the rest of the loop.

The recovery move: **reason from properties you do know.** "I haven't used Druid,
but this is a high-cardinality aggregation over append-only events, so I want a
columnar store with pre-aggregation rather than a row store — whichever product
does that." You've demonstrated the thinking that matters, which is the thing
being scored.

And if you're genuinely stuck, go back to the data: *"Let me re-check the write
volume, because that decides this."* Returning to the numbers is always a legal
move and it buys you thinking time.
</Callout>

### 6. Bottlenecks and tradeoffs (5 min)

Close by critiquing your own design, before they do. Reserve time for this — it
is disproportionately valuable because **senior engineers are expected to know
where their design is weak.**

Run the checklist out loud:

- **Single points of failure.** What breaks everything if it dies? For each:
  replicate, fail over, or degrade gracefully.
- **Hot spots.** Celebrity users, viral items, a single trending key. Note that
  sharding does not fix a hot *key*.
- **What breaks at 10×?** Which component hits its ceiling first, and what
  replaces it.
- **What did I trade away?** "I chose eventual consistency for the feed, so a
  user might briefly not see their own post — I'd fix that specific case by
  reading their own writes from the primary."

Then state what you'd build first if this were real, and what you'd defer. That
answers the "would I trust them to own this?" question directly.

## Layer 3 — The rubric

Interviewers at product companies are usually scoring roughly these axes. Knowing
them tells you where to spend effort:

| Signal | What earns it |
|---|---|
| **Problem framing** | Clarifying questions, scoping down, stating what's out of scope |
| **Quantitative reasoning** | Estimates done out loud, and *conclusions drawn from them* |
| **Technical depth** | One level below the buzzword: eviction policies, quorums, index choice |
| **Tradeoff reasoning** | Two options, a decision, and the reason — not a single assertion |
| **Self-critique** | Naming your own SPOFs and hot spots before being asked |
| **Communication** | Driving the conversation without needing to be prompted |

<Callout type="interview" title="Phrases that do real work">
- *"Before I design, let me confirm the scope…"* — opens on framing.
- *"I'll treat X as out of scope."* — earns the scoping signal explicitly.
- *"That's about 40 writes a second, which means…"* — quantitative, with a
  conclusion.
- *"Let me start simple and add complexity where it's needed."* — pre-empts the
  over-engineering trap.
- *"Two options here. A gives us X at the cost of Y; I'd pick A because…"* — the
  tradeoff shape.
- *"The weak point in what I've drawn is…"* — self-critique, unprompted.
- *"Is it more useful to go deeper here, or move to the data model?"* — hands
  them control while showing you're tracking time.
</Callout>

<Callout type="pitfall" title="Red flags to avoid">
- **Silence while thinking.** Narrate. An interviewer cannot score thoughts.
- **Designing ten features.** You'll finish none. Scope to two or three.
- **Naming technologies as answers.** "Use Cassandra" is not a design; the access
  pattern that makes Cassandra right is.
- **Ignoring the numbers you just calculated.** Sharding a 600 GB dataset after
  estimating 600 GB reads as if you weren't listening to yourself.
- **Defending a choice past its expiry.** If the interviewer pushes twice,
  they're usually telling you something. "That's fair — if writes dominate, my
  choice is wrong, and I'd switch to…" is a strong recovery, not a loss.
- **Running out of time in the deep dive** with no bottleneck discussion. Watch
  the clock and reserve the last five minutes.
</Callout>

### How to practise this

Reading this lesson does approximately nothing on its own. The framework only
becomes usable under pressure once it's automatic:

1. Pick a case study. Set a **45-minute timer**.
2. Talk **out loud**, standing at a whiteboard or paper. Silent practice does not
   transfer — the bottleneck in a real interview is verbalising while thinking.
3. Record yourself. Play it back and check: did you state scope? Did you draw a
   conclusion from every estimate? Did you critique your own design?
4. Then read the lesson's answer and diff it against yours.

Start with [Case 1: the URL shortener](/system-design/case-1-design-a-url-shortener-your-project-master-this-one)
— it's your own project, so you can defend every decision from real experience —
and grade yourself with
[how to self-grade your practice](/system-design/how-to-self-grade-your-practice).
