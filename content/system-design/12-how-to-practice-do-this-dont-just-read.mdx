## Why reading does not transfer

System design is the one track here where reading is almost uncorrelated with
performance. You can read every lesson, absorb all of it, and still do badly in the
round — and that happens constantly, to people who genuinely know the material.

The reason is that the round tests a real-time production skill. You have to hold a
problem in your head, narrate a structure while drawing it, do arithmetic out loud
without getting flustered, notice your own gaps, and absorb an interruption at minute
22 without losing your place. None of that is exercised by reading; it is the
difference between knowing the rules of chess and playing under a clock.

The failure mode is specific. The candidate knows what a consistent-hashing ring is
and still ends the interview having covered a third of the problem, because they spent
eleven minutes on requirements without noticing, went quiet while thinking, and never
did the estimate. **Every one of those is a habit, and habits only come from reps.**

So this page is a method. The companion page,
[how to self-grade your practice](/system-design/how-to-self-grade-your-practice), is
the rubric you score each rep against: use this one to run the session and that one to
mark it.

## The solo mock: five rules

You do not need a partner to get most of the value. You need to make the session
resemble the real thing in the five ways that matter.

**A timer, visible, at 45 minutes.** Clock management is graded, and the only way to
build a feel for what five minutes of requirements gathering costs is to watch it
elapse. Split it the way the [framework lesson](/system-design/1-the-framework-how-to-drive-a-45-minute-design-interview)
does: five minutes requirements, five estimation, five API, ten high-level, fifteen
deep dive, five bottlenecks. When a segment's time is up, move on even if it is
unfinished — abandoning an incomplete section gracefully is itself the skill, because
in the real round you will have to.

**Paper or a whiteboard, not an editor.** On paper you cannot rearrange boxes, so you
have to plan the layout, which forces you to decide the shape of the system before you
start. You also draw more slowly, which is realistic: in the interview you are talking
while drawing and the drawing is the slow part. If the round will be remote, learn the
company's whiteboard tool beforehand — fighting the tool costs real minutes.

**Out loud, in full sentences, to an imaginary interviewer.** This is the rule people
skip and the one that matters most. Thinking silently and then summarising is a
completely different cognitive task from thinking aloud, and only the second is being
assessed. Saying "reads dominate writes by about a hundred to one, so I am going to
optimise the read path first" to an empty room feels theatrical. Do it anyway; the
awkwardness disappears after three sessions and what replaces it is the ability to
narrate under pressure.

**Recorded.** Audio is enough, video of the whiteboard is better. You cannot grade
yourself from memory, because memory edits — you will remember having said the
trade-off you meant to say. The first time you listen back you will hear two things you
did not know you did: a filler habit, and a long silence you experienced as a moment of
thought.

**Cold.** No case study, no notes, before the attempt. An attempt on a problem you have
just read is a recitation exercise.

## The session, end to end

A single practice session runs about 75 minutes:

1. **Pick the problem and read one sentence of it.** "Design a service that
   shortens URLs." That is all the setup a real interview gives you.
2. **45 minutes, timer running, recording on.** Follow the framework. Do not pause
   the timer to look something up; if you do not know a number, estimate it out
   loud and flag it as an assumption, which is what you would do in the room.
3. **Stop when the timer stops.** Mid-sentence if necessary.
4. **Ten minutes: grade yourself** against
   [the rubric](/system-design/how-to-self-grade-your-practice), listening to the
   recording rather than working from memory.
5. **Twenty minutes: read the case study** and compare. Not to check whether you
   got the same answer — you will not, and that is fine — but to find the
   considerations you did not raise at all. A missing consideration is a gap; a
   different choice usually is not.
6. **Write down one thing to do differently.** One, specific, behavioural. "State
   the storage estimate's conclusion, not just the number." Carry it into the next
   session.

Step 6 is what makes the series compound. Without it you will repeat the same three
mistakes for eight weeks.

## Using the case studies as practice, not reading

The five case studies on this site are worked solutions, which makes them dangerous:
reading one feels productive and teaches almost nothing, because watching a solved
problem creates the illusion that you could have solved it.

**Attempt first, always.** Read only the title, then run the full 45 minutes. The
attempt creates the hooks the reading then attaches to. A consideration you struggled
with and then saw handled well is learned; the same consideration read cold is
forgotten by the weekend.

**Then read, looking for omissions rather than differences.** Mark every topic the case
study raises that you never mentioned — those are your gaps. Where you both raised a
topic and chose differently, check whether you gave a reason; if you did, you were fine.

**Re-attempt the ones that went badly a week later**, not the next day. After a week
you will have forgotten the specifics and kept the structure, which is the thing you are
trying to build.

Start with
[Case 1, the URL shortener](/system-design/case-1-design-a-url-shortener-your-project-master-this-one),
because it is your own project: you can defend every decision from experience rather
than theory, and interviewers routinely open with "tell me about something you built".

## A weekly schedule

Sustained over five or six weeks, this is enough:

| Week | Reading | Practice |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Lessons 1 through 5 | Two attempts at Case 1, the second after grading the first |
| 2 | Lessons 6 through 9 | Case 2, then re-attempt Case 1 cold |
| 3 | Lessons 10, 13, 14, 15 | Case 3 — the fan-out trade-off, which is the most-asked shape |
| 4 | Lesson 11, the reference architecture | Case 4, plus one problem not on this site |
| 5 | Re-read lesson 1 and your own notes | Case 5, plus one unseen problem |
| 6 | Nothing new | Two unseen problems, graded strictly |

Two properties of that table are deliberate. **Reading decreases and practice
increases**, because the marginal value flips early. And **week 6 introduces no new
material**, because the last week before a loop should build confidence on a stable base
rather than add fresh uncertainty.

For unseen problems once the case studies are exhausted: a ride-hailing dispatch
service, a ticket booking system with seat reservation, a distributed job scheduler, a
metrics ingestion pipeline, a collaborative document editor, an ad click aggregator.

## Practising the parts you cannot do alone

**Being interrupted.** A real interviewer redirects you at unpredictable moments, and
recovering is a distinct skill: answer the question properly, then return to where you
were without losing the thread. To simulate it, write six interruption cards before you
start, shuffle them, and set a random alarm twice during the 45 minutes. When it goes
off, draw a card and deal with it immediately.

Cards worth using, because they are what interviewers actually ask: "why not use
Postgres for that?", "what happens if that cache goes down entirely?", "the read volume
is ten times what you assumed — what breaks first?", "walk me through what happens on a
write, in order", "how do you know this is working in production?", "that component is a
single point of failure, what would you do?"

**Scope pressure from a vague interviewer.** Real statements are deliberately
underspecified. Practise by writing the problem yourself as one ambiguous sentence and
then *committing to your own assumptions out loud* rather than resolving the ambiguity
first. The skill is stating the assumption clearly enough that it can be corrected.

**Being pushed deeper than you are comfortable with.** After an attempt, pick the
component you were least confident about and spend ten extra minutes answering "how
would I actually implement this?" until you hit something you cannot answer. That
boundary is where the deep dive will land, and it is better found at your desk.

If you can find a partner for even two sessions, spend them on the interruption problem
specifically, since that is the one with the worst solo substitute.

<Callout type="pitfall" title="The three ways practice goes wrong">
**Practising without a timer.** You will take 70 minutes and feel good about a
design you could not have produced in 45. The constraint is the exercise.

**Practising silently.** This trains the wrong skill entirely and it is the most
common shortcut, because talking to an empty room feels ridiculous.

**Practising without grading.** Reps without feedback entrench whatever you already
do, including the mistakes. Ten graded attempts beat thirty ungraded ones, and it
is not close.
</Callout>
