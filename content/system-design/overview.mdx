## What this track is assessed on

The design round decides your level, and it is where the mismatch between what candidates
prepare and what interviewers score is widest.

What people assume they are scored on is knowing the right architecture: they study
diagrams, memorise that news feeds use fan-out-on-write and URL shorteners use base62, and
arrive ready to produce the answer.

What is actually scored is **whether you can take an underspecified problem, narrow it
down, justify every decision with a number, and criticise your own design before the
interviewer has to.** There is no answer key — two candidates can produce entirely
different diagrams and both score well, or both badly. The diagram is evidence; the
reasoning is the product.

You can see this in how the round fails. Candidates rarely fail for choosing Cassandra over
Postgres. They fail because they started drawing boxes in minute two without asking
anything, added Kafka without naming the bottleneck it solves, never did the arithmetic
that would have shown one database was enough, or spent twenty minutes on authentication
and never reached what the interviewer cared about. Every one is a process failure.

The corollary is worth internalising early: **a simpler design you drive confidently and
critique honestly outscores a sophisticated one you cannot defend.**

## Where you stand, and what to prioritise

Your stated gap is no system design background at all. That changes the plan in three ways.

**You cannot skip the fundamentals in favour of case studies.** Starting with "design
Twitter" before you have a feel for what 10,000 requests per second means is how people end
up memorising solutions rather than acquiring a method.

**Your existing strengths transfer more than you think.** You know SQL properly, so the
database, indexing and sharding conversations are closer to home than they look — sharding
is partitioning, and a hot shard is a skewed key distribution. You have used Redis, which is
most of the caching lesson. And your DSA background makes consistent hashing and
rate-limiting algorithms just data structures. The genuinely new material is narrower than
"all of system design": estimation, the vocabulary of distributed failure, and the interview
method itself.

**The method is learnable faster than the content and worth more.** Someone who runs a
disciplined 45 minutes with a modest design beats someone with broader knowledge and no
structure, which is why the framework lesson comes first and you return to it before every
practice attempt.

Priority order for you: the framework, estimation, databases, caching, then consistency.
Those five carry most of the round.

## What "ready" looks like

The self-check here is a performance, not a recall test. Set a 45-minute timer,
pick a problem you have not attempted, record yourself, and check all of the
following were true.

- You asked at least three clarifying questions before drawing anything, and
  stated what was out of scope out loud.
- You produced a QPS number and a storage-per-year number, and drew an explicit
  conclusion from each — something of the form "that is one machine, so I will
  not shard".
- Your first diagram was simple, and every later component was preceded by naming
  the bottleneck it solved.
- You stated at least three trade-offs, naming the option you rejected and why.
- You identified a single point of failure and a hot-spot risk unprompted.
- You were still on the core problem at minute 40, not on authentication.
- You went two levels deep on one component when pushed.

An external bar: if you can drive Cases 1 through 3 end to end on a whiteboard unaided and
your recording satisfies the rubric, you are at the design level expected of a senior backend
engineer at most product companies. Case 1 should be flawless, because it is your project and
interviewers open with "tell me about a system you built".

## How long this track takes

About 50,000 words across 22 lessons, most fundamentals above 2,000 words and every case
study above 2,500. This is the longest track on the site and the one with least overlap with
what you know: roughly ten to twelve hours of reading plus at least ten hours of timed
practice, over five to six weeks.

The split matters more than the total. **Spend at most 40 percent of your time reading.** Two
weeks on the fundamentals, then one case study per week attempted cold on a timer and graded
against the rubric. People who invert that ratio can describe every component and still fail,
because the thing being scored is a skill.
