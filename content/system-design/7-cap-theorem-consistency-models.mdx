## Layer 1 — The mental model

You have replicated your data across three machines so that losing one doesn't
lose the data. The network between them breaks. A write arrives at machine A,
which can no longer reach B or C.

You have exactly two options, and no third:

1. **Refuse the write** until you can reach a majority again. Data stays
   correct; the system is *down* for that user.
2. **Accept the write** on A alone. The system stays *up*; A and B now disagree,
   and a read served by B returns stale data.

That is the whole of CAP. Under a **P**artition, you choose **C**onsistency or
**A**vailability. You never choose P — partitions are a property of networks,
not a design option. "We'll pick CA" is the single most common way to fail this
question: it means "we assume the network never fails," which is not a
distributed system.

```
        Normal                        Partitioned
   ┌───┐  ┌───┐  ┌───┐          ┌───┐ ╎ ┌───┐  ┌───┐
   │ A │──│ B │──│ C │          │ A │ ╎ │ B │──│ C │
   └───┘  └───┘  └───┘          └───┘ ╎ └───┘  └───┘
      all agree                   ╎ write to A:
                                  ╎  reject  → CP (correct, unavailable)
                                  ╎  accept  → AP (available, divergent)
```

<Callout type="pitfall" title="CAP is narrower than people quote it">
The 2002 Gilbert–Lynch proof is about one specific, very strong pairing:
**linearizable** consistency plus **total** availability (every request to a
non-failed node gets a response) plus partition tolerance. Real systems are
neither perfectly available nor perfectly linearizable, so "is X CP or AP?" is
usually a question about *defaults and tuning*, not a binary property. Say that
out loud in an interview — it's the difference between reciting CAP and
understanding it.
</Callout>

## Layer 2 — The mechanism

### What a partition actually is

Not just a cut cable. In practice, the thing your code cannot distinguish:

- a peer that crashed,
- a peer that is alive but unreachable,
- a peer that is alive and reachable but paused for 8 seconds in a stop-the-world
  GC or a VM migration,
- a peer whose replies are arriving, but slowly enough that you timed out.

**All four look identical from the outside: no response before the timeout.**
This is why partition tolerance isn't optional and why "just detect the failure
and handle it" isn't an answer — a perfect failure detector is impossible in an
asynchronous network. Everything below is a strategy for being correct *without*
knowing which of those four happened.

### Quorums: the actual machinery behind "CP"

Replication with `N` replicas, `W` acknowledgements required for a write, `R`
replicas consulted for a read. The rule that matters:

```
R + W > N
```

If the read set and the write set must overlap in at least one replica, then any
read is guaranteed to touch at least one replica that saw the last completed
write.

Worked, with `N = 3`:

| Config | R | W | Reads see latest write? | Survives |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `W=3, R=1` | 1 | 3 | Yes (3+1 > 3) | 0 replica failures for writes, 2 for reads |
| `W=2, R=2` | 2 | 2 | Yes (2+2 > 3) | 1 failure for both — **the usual choice** |
| `W=1, R=1` | 1 | 1 | **No** (1+1 < 3) | 2 failures, but eventual only |
| `W=1, R=3` | 3 | 1 | Yes (3+1 > 3) | fast writes, fragile reads |

`W=2, R=2` on `N=3` is the default for a reason: it is the cheapest setting that
both tolerates one machine dying and guarantees reads observe acknowledged
writes. Note what a majority quorum buys you beyond overlap — **two majorities
of the same set always intersect**, so only one side of a partition can ever
assemble one. That's what stops split-brain.

<Callout type="pitfall" title="R + W > N is not linearizability">
It's necessary, not sufficient, and this is a great follow-up to be ready for.
A quorum read overlapping a quorum write tells you the *value is present on some
replica you contacted* — it does not order concurrent operations. Real gaps:

- **Concurrent writes** to different replicas produce siblings. Something must
  resolve them: last-write-wins (silently loses data), vector clocks, or CRDTs.
- **Partial writes.** A write that reaches 2 of 3 and then the client crashes is
  neither committed nor rolled back; it will be read by some quorums and not
  others.
- **Sloppy quorums** (Dynamo-style) accept writes on *any* `W` reachable nodes,
  not the `W` nodes that own the key. `R + W > N` no longer holds across the
  partition, by design — availability was the point.

Linearizability needs a **consensus protocol** (Raft, Paxos, Zab), which elects a
leader and totally orders operations. Quorums are an ingredient of consensus, not
a substitute for it.
</Callout>

### PACELC: the half of the tradeoff CAP omits

CAP only says anything during a partition — which is rare. PACELC (Abadi, 2012)
asks the question that applies the other 99.9% of the time:

> **P**artition → **A**vailability or **C**onsistency;
> **E**lse → **L**atency or **C**onsistency.

Because even with a healthy network, a linearizable read must coordinate — talk
to the leader, or to a quorum — and coordination costs a round trip. Cross-region,
that's 50–150 ms you cannot optimise away. Wanting strong consistency *is*
wanting latency, permanently, not just during incidents.

| System | Partition | Else | In plain terms |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cassandra, Dynamo, Riak | PA | EL | Always fast, always up, converges later |
| MongoDB, HBase | PC | EC | Leader-based; pay coordination always |
| Spanner | PC | EC | Linearizable globally, and pays for it in write latency |
| DynamoDB (default) | PA | EL | Strong reads are opt-in, and cost 2× |

Bringing PACELC up unprompted is one of the cheapest ways to signal you've read
past the blog-post layer.

### The ladder of consistency models

"Strong" and "eventual" are the two ends of a spectrum with useful rungs in
between. Strongest first:

**Linearizable.** Every operation appears to happen instantaneously at a single
point between its start and end, in real-time order. If a write completes and
then a read begins, the read sees it. This is the model that makes a distributed
store behave like one variable on one machine. Cost: consensus on every
operation.

**Sequential.** All nodes see operations in the *same* order, but that order need
not match real time. A read may be arbitrarily stale, just never inconsistently
ordered.

**Causal.** Operations that are causally related (you read X, then write Y) are
seen in that order everywhere; concurrent operations may be seen in different
orders. Strong enough to stop the classic anomaly — a reply appearing before the
comment it replies to — and cheap enough to stay available under partition. The
sweet spot that under-appears in interviews.

**Read-your-own-writes.** You see your own updates; others may lag. Implemented
by routing a user's reads to the replica they wrote to, or by pinning reads to
the leader for a few seconds after a write. This is the fix for the single most
common user-visible replication bug: user edits their profile, page reloads from
a replica, edit appears to have vanished.

**Monotonic reads.** You never see time move backwards. Successive reads won't
show a value, then an older value. Implemented by pinning a user to one replica.

**Eventual.** Stop writing and replicas converge. Says nothing about when, or
about what you observe meanwhile.

<Callout type="interview" title="The distinction that separates levels">
**Linearizability is not serializability.** Interviewers use "strong consistency"
loosely and then check whether you do too.

- **Linearizability** is a *recency* guarantee about a **single object**. "My
  read returns the latest write."
- **Serializability** is an *isolation* guarantee about **multiple objects in a
  transaction**. "Concurrent transactions produce a result equal to running them
  one at a time — in some order."

Neither implies the other. Serializable execution may order your transaction
before one that finished an hour ago (so, stale). A linearizable key-value store
has no transactions at all. Both together is **strict serializability**, which is
what Spanner sells as "external consistency."

Tie it back: *"Snapshot isolation in Postgres is not serializable — it permits
write skew. That's why `SERIALIZABLE` exists and why it aborts transactions."*
That sentence connects this lesson to
[transactions & isolation levels](/sql/4-transactions-isolation-levels) and
tends to end the line of questioning favourably.
</Callout>

### What real systems actually do

- **Spanner (CP / PC-EC).** Paxos groups per shard plus **TrueTime** — GPS and
  atomic clocks giving a bounded-uncertainty interval rather than a timestamp. A
  write waits out the uncertainty window (a few ms) before committing, which
  makes timestamp order match real-time order globally. Buying linearizability
  with hardware is the trick worth knowing.
- **etcd / ZooKeeper (CP).** Raft and Zab. Used for the things that must be
  correct: leader election, locks, config, service registry. Small data, strong
  guarantees. Note the sharp edge: **ZooKeeper reads are not linearizable by
  default** — any follower serves them and may lag. You need an explicit `sync`
  first. etcd does give linearizable reads by default.
- **Cassandra (AP, tunable).** No leader; every replica takes writes. Conflicts
  resolved by last-write-wins on timestamps, so **concurrent writes silently lose
  data**. `QUORUM` reads and writes get you `R + W > N` but still not
  linearizability; for that you need lightweight transactions (`IF NOT EXISTS`),
  which run Paxos per operation and are roughly 4× slower.
- **DynamoDB.** Eventually consistent reads by default, `ConsistentRead=true`
  available at double the read-capacity cost. Knowing the *pricing* consequence
  is a good sign you've operated one.

<Callout type="pitfall" title="Is Redis CP or AP? (a favourite trap)">
Redis replication is **asynchronous**. The primary acknowledges your write
immediately, then propagates. So:

1. Client writes to the primary, gets `OK`.
2. Primary dies before the replica receives it.
3. Sentinel or Cluster promotes the replica.
4. **The acknowledged write is gone.**

Redis is therefore *not* CP — it can lose committed writes on failover — and it
isn't cleanly AP either, because a minority partition stops serving. It is best
described as "AP-leaning, and not a system of record." `WAIT numreplicas timeout`
blocks until replicas ack, which narrows the window but is not consensus: it
can't prevent a stale primary from having already acked.

The practical conclusion, and the one to state: **do not use Redis as the only
durable home for data you cannot lose, and be sceptical of Redis-based
distributed locks.** Redlock's safety was publicly disputed by Kleppmann
precisely because a GC pause can make a lock holder believe it still holds a lock
it has lost. If you need a lock for correctness, you need a fencing token; if you
need one for efficiency, Redis is fine.
</Callout>

### Choosing, per feature — not per system

The real skill is that one product needs several points on the ladder at once.
Take the URL shortener:

| Feature | Model needed | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Allocating a new short code | Linearizable | Two users must never get the same code. Needs consensus or a single allocator. |
| Resolving a code → URL | Eventual + cache | Immutable after creation, so staleness is impossible in practice. Serve from CDN/replica. |
| Click analytics | Eventual | Counters, aggregated. Seconds of lag is invisible. |
| "My links" dashboard | Read-your-own-writes | A link you just created *must* appear, or the product looks broken. |

Answering "it depends on the feature" and then enumerating features is what a
senior answer looks like. Answering "we'll use strong consistency" is what a
mid-level answer looks like.

## Layer 3 — Interview surface

<Callout type="interview" title="Follow-ups to rehearse out loud">
1. **"Why can't you have CA?"** Because P is not a choice. A CA system is a
   single-node system, or one that corrupts on partition.
2. **"And when there's no partition?"** PACELC: latency vs consistency. Strong
   consistency costs a round trip forever, not just during incidents.
3. **"Does `R + W > N` give you strong consistency?"** No — no ordering of
   concurrent writes, no handling of partial writes, and sloppy quorums break the
   inequality deliberately. Consensus is what gives ordering.
4. **"Linearizable or serializable?"** Recency of one object vs isolation of a
   multi-object transaction. Neither implies the other; both is strict
   serializability.
5. **"Is Redis CP or AP?"** Async replication → acknowledged writes can be lost
   on failover. Not a system of record.
6. **"How does Spanner get global linearizability?"** TrueTime's bounded
   uncertainty plus commit-wait, on top of Paxos.
7. **"Cassandra with QUORUM — are you safe?"** From staleness, largely. From
   concurrent-write data loss, no: last-write-wins discards a value silently.
8. **"User updates their profile and it appears to revert. Diagnose."**
   Read-your-own-writes violation from reading a lagging replica. Fix: pin reads
   to leader briefly post-write, or route by session.
9. **"Two successive reads show newer then older data."** Monotonic-reads
   violation — the requests hit different replicas. Fix: sticky routing.
10. **"Where would you accept eventual consistency in your own project?"** Have
    the answer pre-built (analytics, counters, feeds) and the contrast ready
    (money, inventory, identity, uniqueness).
</Callout>

### Failure modes to name unprompted

- **Split-brain.** Both sides of a partition accept writes and diverge.
  Prevented by majority quorums: two majorities always intersect, so only one
  side can have one. This is also why cluster sizes are odd — a 4-node cluster
  tolerates the same single failure as 3 while costing more.
- **Last-write-wins data loss.** Clock skew between nodes decides which
  concurrent write survives. Silent, unrecoverable.
- **Stale reads after failover.** A demoted primary that hasn't learned it was
  demoted keeps serving reads. Mitigated by leases and fencing tokens.
- **Lost acknowledged writes.** Any system that acks before durable replication:
  Redis, MySQL with async replicas, Kafka with `acks=1`.

<Callout type="tip" title="The one-sentence version, if you're asked in passing">
"Partitions aren't optional, so you're choosing whether to be wrong or to be
down — and PACELC adds that even when the network is healthy you're choosing
between latency and consistency. In practice I pick per feature: consensus for
uniqueness and money, eventual for counters and feeds, read-your-own-writes
wherever a user looks at their own data."
</Callout>
